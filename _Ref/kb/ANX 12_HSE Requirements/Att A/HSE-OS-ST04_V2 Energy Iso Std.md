1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Energy Isolation is the process of establishing a secure break in the energy supply to ensure that 
inadvertent reconnection is not possible. Effective energy isolation ensures that equipment remains safely 
isolated for the duration of any work activity and prevents accidental re-pressuring or re-energizing of 
equipment before personnel have ceased work and equipment integrity has been restored. 
Ideally any intrusive maintenance activities should be carried out within shutdown periods minimizing 
energy isolation requirements but this is not always practically possible and hence, when maintenance 
work has to be carried out on live plant, a high standard of isolations management shall be required. 
The safe and effective use of the Safe Isolation / De-Isolation and Lock Out -Tag Out processes on 
ADNOC Operational Facilities is the responsibility of the respective delegated Asset Owner for the facility. 
Oil & gas facilities and installations involve the processing of hazardous substances (flammable and/or 
toxic) often at high temperatures and/or pressures. The release of hazardous substances due to 
inadequate process isolation may lead to: 
(i) 
Local immediate effects to people (death or injury) and to the environment; 
(ii) 
Equally serious long-term effects to people and the environment; and 
(iii) 
Escalation of an initial release causing wider damage to plant and other systems. 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this document is to ensure that a well-defined process for containment of energy is 
implemented across all ADNOC Group Companies ensuring that a safe working environment is achieved 
by providing management control over the various hazardous work activities. This standard provides a 
detailed description of the Safe Isolation / De-Isolation and guidelines on Lock Out - Tag Out Processes 
for use on all ADNOC Operational Facilities.  
Plant reinstatement forms a critical aspect of any intrusive activity and a de-isolation activity can be 
equally hazardous or incorrect or incomplete reinstatement can also result in loss of containment. 
This standard sets out a safe, standardized and robust system for identifying and implementing plant and 
equipment isolations required for: 
(i) 
Compliance with applicable Statutory / Legal requirements and Regulations, Codes of 
Practice and ADNOC Standards; and 
(ii) 
Ensuring risks associated with exposure to hazardous energy sources are as low as 
reasonably practicable (ALARP). 
3. 
SCOPE 
The requirements of this document shall be applicable to all ADNOC group companies, employees, 
contractors, facilities (including interface areas), activities, services and products without exception. 
Contractors shall be responsible for ensuring that all activities performed within ADNOC operating 
facilities in the course of carrying out their contractual requirements are managed in accordance with the 
requirements of this document and reported, when applicable, to the contract holder.

6. 
ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES 
In this section, all roles within the Energy Isolation process are described together with their specific 
responsibilities and accountabilities. 
For proper harmonization of the Energy Isolation process across ADNOC Group, all Businesses should 
adopt the common terminology for naming of roles and understanding of their responsibilities and 
accountabilities. 
The ultimate success of isolation process depends on the competence and awareness of the people 
carrying out the activities.  If they do not have a sound understanding of permit or isolation requirements 
and their own responsibilities, the system will have a limited effect on maintaining a safe operation and 
preventing accidents. 
Please use and read this standard in conjunction with other WMS Standards. 
Role 
Responsibility & Competency 
Isolating Authority 
(ISA) 
An Isolation Authority is a person with overall responsibility for ensuring correct 
implementation of, and certification for, isolations being applied prior to intrusive 
work. 
An Isolating Authority (ISA) is a competent person with detailed knowledge of 
facilities in an asset. The Isolating Authority role develops the isolation 
requirement corresponding to their discipline (i.e.  Mechanical/Process, 
Instrument, LV Electrical, HV Electrical, and Inhibits & Overrides). They are 
specifically responsible to: 
 
Define the scope of the isolations /de-isolation requirements 
 
Undertake a risk assessment to determine the minimum level of 
isolation based on hierarchy of controls. 
 
Develop isolation / de-isolation requirements to provide the minimum 
level of isolation required and mitigate the risks to As Low as 
Reasonably Practicable (ALARP) including application of the required

 DETERMINING BASELINE ISOLATION STANDARDS 
A Baseline Isolation Standard is the minimum acceptable standard of final isolation for intrusive and non-
intrusive maintenance against live plant under normal circumstances. Baseline Isolation Standards shall 
be one of the following isolation methods: 
(i) 
Positive Isolation (Mechanical Isolation): removal of a spool and fitting of blinds, or fitting 
of a spade or spectacle blind to allow complete separation of the plant / equipment to be 
worked on from other parts of the system 
(ii) 
Proved Isolation: Valve isolation where effectiveness of the isolation can be confirmed via 
vent/bleed points before breaking into the system, and for the duration of the isolation. 
(iii) 
Non-Proved Isolation: Valve isolation. No provision to confirm effectiveness of valve 
closure prior to breaking into system 
Baseline Isolation Standards must in any case meet the Minimum Isolation Standards specified in 
Appendix 3 and certain Baseline Isolation shall be applied in the following situations: 
(i) 
The minimum acceptable isolation standard for Hot Work is the use of blinds. 
(ii) 
Where only a single valve provides an isolation barrier in the case of Acute Toxics or at 
pressures above 4 bar or where the material is above its flash point or above 60 deg c, such 
isolation may only be used to install a blind before further work can be executed. 
The Baseline Isolation Standard selection tool is not intended to apply to: 
(i) 
confined space entries; 
(ii) 
pipelines isolations (refer to Appendix 12 for guidance); 
(iii) 
extended term isolations, e.g. for mothballed plant; 
(iv) 
Or where isolation failure could produce a catastrophic outcome or could result in a situation 
from which recovery would be very difficult. 
The tool will indicate either that further consideration is required or that one of the categories of isolation 
shown in figure below is the appropriate ‘baseline’ standard. Within each category, the methods listed do 
not all provide an equivalent degree of security. For example, there is a hierarchy of mechanical security 
for the three isolation methods shown for category II (proved) isolation – once installed, the reliability of 
double block and bleed (DBB) is greatest while that for single block and bleed (SBB) is least.  
The choice of a final appropriate isolation plan shall be based on the risk assessment and be 
demonstrated to achieve a risk reduction to ALARP.  It is important to remember that, as the complexity 
of an isolation scheme increases, the opportunities for error also increase.

APPENDIX NO. 5. 
DESIGN CONSIDERATIONS 
Introduction  
Good design maximizes inherent safety and is fundamental to achieving safe and effective isolation 
without placing unnecessary constraints on plant operation. Some intrusive maintenance or internal 
inspection tasks will always require plant shutdown. Where isolation to enable intrusive activities is 
appropriate, suitable isolation arrangements on the plant should be clearly specified at the design stage. 
Although the opportunity to achieve an inherently safer design is greatest for new plant and equipment, 
the same approach should be followed for plant modifications. 
The potential for human failure, including error, should be addressed and, wherever possible, minimized 
in the design. 
The following issues should be considered and included within the design documentation: 
1. 
Provision for alternative modes of plant operation using differing flow routes;  
2. 
Equipment designed to allow for intrusive plant operations fully controlled under 
Standard Operating Procedures.   (Examples include filter change-out, sampling or 
removal of pipeline pigs from launchers and receivers); 
3. 
Equipment designed to meet requirements for access into equipment for inspection 
and/or maintenance;  
4. 
Designs to provide for safe isolation arrangements (valve types, spacers/spading 
points or spectacle blinds), safe means of testing /proving isolations including 
associated vents and drains for venting, flushing and purging; 
5. 
Wherever possible to eliminate the requirement for vessel entry through equipment 
design. 
Specific attention is to be given to the areas outlined below. 
(i) 
Mechanical isolation  
Design of new plant should include facilities for mechanical isolation (including the valve isolation 
to install the mechanical isolation) for vessel entry, for isolation of toxic fluids and to protect, by 
segregation, parts of the plant which (in some alternative operating modes) might be exposed to 
overpressure conditions. This latter situation applies where it is not reasonably practicable for the 
installed safety systems to protect all foreseeable operating configurations such as the separation 
of a high-pressure plant from its drainage system. 
(ii) 
Plant Identification 
A scheme to uniquely identify all process plant, piping, and valves should be drawn up and all 
items should be readily identifiable on the actual plant through the use of permanent (formal, simple, 
easily visible and unambiguous) labelling and referenced on the piping and instrumentation 
diagrams (P&IDs).

Pipework layout should minimize trapped inventories and allow easy removal of fluid for isolation

Ensure that pipework is of sufficient size and design to minimize the possibility of becoming blocked 
in service especially in the case of smaller bore drains or vents.  
Any piece of pipework intended for physical disconnection should be easily removable with 
supports providing adequate support during disconnection. Provisions shall be made for supports 
where temporary hoses will be required for bleeding. 
(iii) 
Valves 
Specify suitable isolation valves for the service fluid and operating conditions and ensure that the 
positions of all manually operated valves can be effectively secured in place. 
(iv) 
Pressure safety valves 
Isolation valves should be provided downstream of pressure safety valves for safe isolation from a 
shared flare or vent system if intrusive maintenance is intended at any time when the remainder of 
the flare or vent system is in operation.  
Where an isolation valve is downstream from a pressure safety valve, the design must ensure that 
the isolation valve can be secured in the open position at all times when the pressure safety valve 
is on-line.  
Where pressure safety valves are spared and continued plant operation is intended, suitable 
isolation facilities must also be provided upstream of the pressure safety valves and appropriate 
arrangements put in place to ensure that the isolation valves do not impair the performance of the 
pressure safety valves in service and that closure of the downstream isolation valve does not 
expose pipework downstream of the off-line pressure safety valve to over-pressure from the live 
plant. 
(v) 
Spared equipment 
For spared or parallel-operated equipment, isolation arrangements should allow complete 
segregation from on-line equipment for operational and/or maintenance reasons. The 
arrangements should also provide for operational/ maintenance requirements of associated shared 
services, including control systems and fluid disposal systems such as flare headers. 
(vi) 
Location of isolation and testing facilities 
Unless risk assessment indicates otherwise, isolation and bleed points should be as close as 
possible to the plant item as this aids control of the isolation arrangements and minimizes the 
inventory of fluid to be depressurized/drained.  
Ensure that bleeds are arranged so that: 
1. 
They are easily accessible for checking; 
2. 
Their discharge cannot harm personnel or plant and toxic or flammable material can 
be conveyed to a safe place for disposal.

PROVING INTEGRITY OF ISOLATIONS 
(i) 
Process Isolations: 
Valve isolation does not always provide a leak-tight seal. Bleeds or vents might be periodically 
used to check for pressure build-up to confirm that there is no leakage. Pressure gauges should 
be fitted to allow bleeding and venting.  
In such cases, to prove the integrity of the isolation, the subject isolation points shall not let past 
(leak) more than a specified quantity of fluid or result in a pressure build-up over a defined period 
of time. The piping volume and the substance concerned shall determine the acceptable ‘passing’ 
criteria and duration of test. These criteria should be defined in the operating procedures respective 
to each business and might be asset specific. As a general rule, pressure build-up tests should be 
conducted on all isolations for at least 10 minutes to prove their integrity and effectiveness. Usage 
of calibrated and reliable pressure gauges with the correct range is of paramount importance to 
the above requirements.  
Isolations remaining in place longer than one shift should be re-proved. Frequency of monitoring 
& re-proving shall be determined by IWAP Team or Area Authority and specified in the PTW / 
Isolation Certificate. 
Additional instructions might be needed for circumstances where isolations are more complex and 
proving could be made more challenging by: 
1. 
Viscous fluids, particularly if they solidify at near ambient temperatures; 
2. 
Presence of debris, residues, scale in drain lines or valves, preventing sealing; 
3. 
Formation of hydrates leading to blockage of valves and pipework such that pressure 
tests giving misleading results. Another importance aspect to consider is sudden 
release of trapped pressure when trying to melt hydrates; and

GUIDELINES AROUND PROCESS ISOLATIONS 
This appendix provides guidance around several aspects of process isolations.  
Guidelines for usage of Actuated Valves 
Actuated valves should be considered as a part of mechanical isolation requirements only if the fail-safe 
condition is closed and motive power to the actuated valve is physically disconnected (cable 
disconnection) from the source (actuator).  
Note: Reliance on switches should not be acceptable under any circumstances. 
If a remotely actuated valve is considered as part of isolations requirements, accidental valve operation 
should be prevented on command. Appropriate measures to prevent inadvertent operation should be 
considered by Isolation Authority: 
(i) 
Valve should first be closed; 
(ii) 
Where motive power (i.e. electricity, air or hydraulic fluid) is required for closing, an 
appropriate locking system shall be installed to the valve to mechanically constraint its 
opening. There might be cases where absence of motive power is required for valve closing. 
In both the cases, the isolation of motive power should be done via physical disconnection 
and testing should be required to confirm isolation of valve. Unauthorized de-isolation should 
be controlled using permit to work controls; 
(iii) 
Isolation certificate should record and describe the motive power disconnection points. The 
IC should be attached to a work permit if applicable. This aspect is important for appropriate 
reinstatement of the plant/equipment;  
(iv) 
If venting of valve might affect the isolation of motive power, a reliable locking mechanism / 
arrangement should be selected appropriate to the risk level; 
(v) 
Physical disconnection and isolation of control lines should be done for pneumatic and 
hydraulic operated valves which fail close; 
(vi) 
Pneumatic and hydraulic operated valves which fail open or fail as is should not be used for 
isolation purposes; 
(vii) 
In cases where electrical power is switched off with removal of fuse, the means of isolation 
should be secured by locking off. The key management for locks used to secure isolation 
should be in accordance with Group Company specific LOTO procedures. 
(viii) Selection of method for motive power isolation should be in proportion to the risk level; 
(ix) 
If remotely actuated valves are controlled by 3rd parties, a suitable communication and permit 
control protocol should be developed with special consideration for reinstatement including 
location (offsite or remote location); and 
(x) 
Power supply of electrically actuated valves should be mechanically isolated along with 
manual locking of overrides.

Guidelines for draining, venting, purging and flushing 
In systems which contain hazardous substances and prior to breaking containment, removal of bulk 
contents, cleaning of residual fluids and solid deposits should be required. 
Bleeds / vents in pipework connections allow fluid to be drained and depressurized from the system 
enabling safe depressurization and providing necessary information to verify the integrity of isolations. 
Inadequate provision and position of bleeds or vents might compromise the safety of an isolation point.  
Where intrusive work is required to install or remove a mechanical isolation, prior valve isolation must be 
applied followed by venting and draining to remove any fluid and to prove integrity of the isolation (e.g. 
double block and bleed). 
The required extent of purging and flushing should depend on the substance concerned and the nature 
of the intrusive activity. Stringent control should be required for intrusive hot work on systems containing 
flammable substances. Some precaution might be as follows: 
(i) 
Depressurising to atmospheric pressure;  
(ii) 
Cooling (or heat for cryogenic systems); 
(iii) 
Draining; 
(iv) 
Water flushing and/or filling; 
(v) 
Nitrogen purging; 
(vi) 
Extra measures such as air movement, back flushing, high-pressure water jetting, detergent 
wash; and 
Demonstrating (internally) a non-explosive atmosphere (substantially lower than the LEL) immediately 
prior to commencement of work. 
If ‘dead-legs’ are identified in the system, control measures should be in place to reduce risk to ALARP 
such as detailed instructions/SOPs for draining and flushing to ensure the ‘dead legs’ are adequately 
prepared. 
Careful considerations should be given for removal of hazardous substances to ensure further hazards 
are not created. Some examples of further hazards might include:  
(i) 
Overloading of the drains or vent systems; 
(ii) 
Uncontrolled/accidental ingress of air into pipework or equipment; 
(iii) 
Formation of hydrates; and 
(iv) 
Creating a vacuum in vessels which are not designed to withstand.

General 
(i) 
The main methods for pipeline isolation are: 
1. 
the isolation and decommissioning of the entire pipeline, or pipeline system; or 
2. 
The isolation and decommissioning of a localised section of the pipeline. 
Note: The isolation selection tool (Appendix 6) is not appropriate for the selection of pipeline isolations. 
Whole pipeline isolation 
(i) 
Isolation of a complete pipeline or pipeline system will normally be at the end points. This 
may involve existing isolation valves, spectacle blinds, removable spool pieces, etc. 
Procedures similar to those used for plant isolation may be used. However, for many pipeline 
applications, vents are left open to minimise the differential pressure across the downstream 
isolation barrier. When isolating sections of pipeline, you should consider the hazard from 
thermal expansion of liquid trapped in the section between isolation valves as this could 
result in line rupture. 
‘Localised’ isolations 
(i)

valves, removable spool pieces, pipe freezing, line plugging, pipe stoppers, bags, high

water.
