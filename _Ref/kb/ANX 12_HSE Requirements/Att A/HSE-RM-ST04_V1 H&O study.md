1. 
INTRODUCTION 
A Hazard and Operability (HAZOP) study is a structured hazard identification technique using a multi-
disciplinary team for identification of process hazards with respect to design and operation of a facility. 
This Standard describes the principles and procedures for conducting HAZOP studies for ADNOC Group. 
 
HAZOP study is undertaken, with the objective of: 
 
(i) 
Identifying potential hazards in the system. The hazards involved may include both those 
essentially relevant only to the immediate area of the system and those with a much wider 
sphere of influence, e.g. some environmental hazards; 
(ii) Identifying potential operability problems with the system; and  
(iii) Identifying cause of production deviations likely to lead to hazardous situations. 
A HAZOP study identifies deviations from the system design intent utilizing a core set of guidewords. The 
technique aims to stimulate the imagination of participants in a systematic way to identify hazards and 
operability issues. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this Standard is to provide an overall understanding of the HAZOP process and its 
application within the ADNOC Group. This Standard establishes consistent practices for planning, 
conducting and documenting HAZOP studies for the ADNOC Group. 
 
All HAZOP studies carried out for ADNOC Group shall be in accordance with the requirements of this 
Standard.  
 
Requirements for carrying out HAZOP studies at various stages of a project are provided in  
ADNOC HSE Impact Assessment (HSEIA) Standard [Ref. 1] and ADNOC Management of Change 
(MOC). Recommendations from audits or incident investigations may also initiate a HAZOP study. 
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to the ADNOC Group and its 
Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented, complied and monitored at all times.

QRA  
Quantitative Risk Assessment; It is a formal and systematic approach of 
estimating the likelihood and consequences of hazardous events, and 
expressing the results quantitatively as risk to people, the environment, 
asset or reputation.   
 
A structured approach to assessing the potential for incidents and 
expressing this potential numerically. In QRA statistical values are 
derived for potential loss of life and damage to resources and 
environment. Note: These values must never be interpreted as 
unavoidable and acceptable losses. 
 
It must always be recognised that the calculated fatality (or loss) figures 
are based on experience, statistical failure and incident rates 
representing an average historical quality of management. Incident 
investigations usually show that these ‘historical’ incidents were, with the 
benefit of hindsight, quite preventable.  
 
QRA is a tool which helps to translate this hindsight into foresight 
(planning) in order to assist management in deciding the best approach 
and show ways and means (e.g. improved engineering, procedures, 
supervision, etc.) to prevent the potential incidents from happening. QRA 
is not to be used to justify or encourage risk taking

7.1. 
HAZOP THROUGH PROJECT/FACILITY LIFECYCLE 
HAZOP study is an integral part of the project activity. Planned HAZOP studies shall be included in the 
Project Schedule on the critical path, with the timing determined by the availability of the main drawings 
of the correct quality.  
 
The types of HAZOP required during the Asset Development Phase include: 
 
1. 
FEED Stage HAZOP 
2.

The types of HAZOP required during the Asset Operating Phase include: 
 
1. 
Management of Change (MOC) HAZOP 
2. 
Revalidation HAZOP

Note: If preliminary P&IDs are available, a HAZOP study could be conducted at Concept Stage of the

7.1.1. FEED STAGE HAZOP 
This is typically the first HAZOP study for any project using the full technique and addressing all the 
details of the plant.  
 
(a) 
Timing  
The Piping & Instrumentation Diagrams (P&IDs) shall be of Approved for Design (AFD)/ Issued for 
HAZOP status for conducting the HAZOP.  
 
Design and P&ID review workshops must be conducted and the recommendations/findings from 
the review shall be incorporated in the P&IDs used for the HAZOP study. Attempts to carry out 
HAZOP before the design is fully defined to AFD status will probably result in failure in meeting the 
intent.

(b) 
Scope and Purpose 
FEED Stage HAZOP represents the first opportunity for all the design disciplines, project 
management and operations to be involved in a complete review of the project, considering all 
aspects of the design. The objective is to optimize the design and minimize any significant rework 
during detailed engineering stage. 
7.1.2. DETAILED ENGINEERING STAGE (EPC) STAGE HAZOP   
This HAZOP study is carried out during detailed engineering stage (EPC) of the Project. The objective is 
to look in detail at all aspects of the design and will be the most comprehensive HAZOP study within the 
Project. 
 
 
The detailed engineering stage HAZOP study is required to look at changes made to the approved FEED 
stage drawings resulting from the contractor's detailed design effort and the availability of vendor's 
information. PFDs, P&IDs and Equipment Data Sheets shall be frozen prior to HAZOP workshop. 
 
(a) 
Timing  
The PFDs, P&IDs and other documents used during the HAZOP study shall be Pre-Approved for 
Construction/Issued for HAZOP.  
 
The PFDs, P&IDs and other documents shall be updated to 'Approved for Construction (AFC)' 
status and signed off as agreed during the HAZOP.  
 
Any changes to the PFDs, P&IDs and other documents following this study should be analyzed 
through Management of Change (MOC) procedure which itself should include a MOC HAZOP. 
 
(b) 
Scope and Purpose 
The concept is to extend FEED stage HAZOP, picking up on details that were not available in the 
previous stage, and concentrating on the changes to the design, as well as any additional details 
made available by vendors (if any). Most often, as part of detailed engineering design, there will 
be numerous changes in the P&IDs from the FEED stage P&IDs. In such cases, a full 
comprehensive HAZOP study shall be conducted during the detailed engineering stage.  
 
Detailed engineering stage HAZOP shall also cover batch and sequential operations such as 
pigging, sampling etc.  Refer Section 7.2 for further details on batch/sequential operations.   
 
Vendor packages should be integrated into the detailed engineering HAZOP study with the 
appropriate vendor representative present for the review of large or specialist packages. At the 
time of HAZOP, if details for the vendor packages are not available, then separate vendor package 
HAZOP shall be conducted in the presence of vendor representatives.    
 
Vendor package HAZOPs should be conducted by the same core team (wherever practicable) with 
the vendor representative to ensure consistency and coverage of package interfaces and to ensure 
that the risks associated with integration of the vendor package with the rest of the plant are 
minimized. 
 
Where third party technology is used or equipment is part of the design, such as a 'Licensed Unit', 
suitably qualified and experienced engineers and specialists from the Licensor, and/or Equipment 
Supplier shall be part of the HAZOP team for those systems.  
 
All the recommendations of the HAZOP must be incorporated in the design and the Approved for 
Construction (AFC) drawings shall be issued.

7.1.4. REVALIDATION HAZOP (OPERATIONS PHASE)  
A revalidation HAZOP study is conducted to ensure that the previous HAZOP is complete and consistent 
with current operations of the facility and is compliant with applicable regulations where required. HAZOP 
revalidation requirement shall be assessed every five years; revalidation HAZOP shall be conducted if 
the assessment recommends so. Screening criteria provided in the following Table 7.1.1 shall be used 
for the assessment. 
 
The key objectives and typical information required for Revalidation HAZOP are listed below: 
 
(a) 
The key objectives of the revalidation are to: 
(i) 
Review previous HAZOP study report to ensure that the HAZOP technique was effectively 
applied and addressed all the requirements; 
(ii) 
Review the status of recommendations from previous HAZOP studies and ensure that those 
have been resolved; 
(iii) 
Review the changes in facility and ensure that HAZOP has been conducted for those 
changes; 
(iv) 
Review the process safety related incidents/ since the previous HAZOP; and 
(v) 
Review the compliance with applicable regulatory requirements and international standards.   
(b) 
The typical information that will be required is:   
(i) 
Process Description of the Unit/Plant; 
(ii) 
Most recent HAZOPs; 
(iii) 
Management of Change (MOC) documents; 
(iv) 
Updated PFDs and P&IDs; 
(v) 
Updated Cause and Effect (C&E) diagrams; 
(vi) 
Details of tie-in points (if any); 
(vii) 
Previous incident reports; and 
(viii) Documented status of recommendations closed from previous HAZOP.

7.2. 
BATCH OPERATIONS HAZOP (SEQUENTIAL OPERATIONS)  
Batch operations should be identified before HAZOP studies commence, as the application of 
guidewords/deviations to batch operation systems is more complex than their application to continuous 
systems, examples are the launching and receiving of pipeline pigs. It normally requires the simultaneous 
application of guidewords to both the procedural step and the associated equipment used for the step. 
The HAZOP is in fact normally driven by a review of the procedural steps with the P&ID review resulting 
as a natural consequence of this process.  
 
The HAZOP Chairman should develop a batch operation HAZOP technique applicable to the 
operation/system to be reviewed. Some components of batch HAZOP techniques are given below: 
 
(a) 
Review the physical location of the operation and the relevant engineering equipment/instruments. 
Consider the nature and proximity of neighbouring plant/operations;  
(b) 
Select a set of guidewords based on the list for sequential operations supplemented by the main 
process parameter guidewords of Flow, Temperature, and Pressure appropriate to the nature of 
the operation (see Table 7.2.1 for deviation matrix for batch/sequential operations); 
(c) 
Follow step-by-step operating procedures and identify the system used, including the 
process/utilities interfaces, and mark up the P&ID as appropriate;  
(d) 
Define the design intent for each step including the intended condition of the relevant equipment 
on P&IDs and/or layout drawings;  
(e) 
Having defined the design intent, apply the selected set of guidewords to identify deviations and 
potential hazards;  
(f) 
Consider existing safeguard (controls) referring as necessary to the Cause and Effect (C&E) 
Diagrams and make further recommendations as appropriate; and 
(g) 
Complete the HAZOP of the P&ID for sections not covered by the review of the procedures.

7.3. 
DRILLING AND WELL OPERATIONS HAZOP 
HAZOP studies shall be conducted for temporary (or permanent) equipment involving with well testing, 
clean-up operations and any other well operations such as workover, etc. This includes equipment from 
the Xmas tree through to and including disposal or storage systems such as tanks, flares and re-injection 
facilities. Any permanent equipment modified or operated in an alternative mode as part of well testing 
activities shall also be included. 
 
 HAZOP studies shall be conducted for:  
 
(a) 
Drilling rigs for surface gas and drilling fluid systems, including surface system hydrocarbon vents; 
(b) 
Well interventions that involve fluid movement ; and  
(c) 
Pressure pumping equipment and systems that are used for energized fluids and incompressible 
fluids. 
(d) 
Well Testing Operations  
(e) 
Underbalanced drilling operation  
(f) 
Hydraulic fracking operations etc.  
 
Well operations should be viewed as batch/sequential operations and hence the procedures should be 
reviewed using the guidewords for sequential operations (e.g. step omitted) with a small selection of core 
process parameter guidewords (e.g. more flow, high pressure, high temperature) and supplementary 
guidewords (e.g. SIMOPS, layout, access problems) which specifically address the nature of operation 
and the scope of the study. For example, the following supplementary guidewords provided in Table 7.3.1 
should be included in the guideword list of a drilling operation HAZOP study.   
 
Well operations such as Under Balanced Drilling (UBD), Well Testing and Hydraulic Fracking include both 
sequential operations and continuous operations (e.g. UBD surface operations); nodes and guidewords 
for such well operations should be selected accordingly for HAZOP study.

7.4.4. TIME REQUIRED PER NODE AND STUDY DURATION 
Predicting the time required for a HAZOP study is always a matter of judgement. It is however important

sessions per day.

7.5.1. NODES 
Nodes are sequential divisions of the facility into appropriately sized sections containing process lines 
and/or equipment. HAZOP study progresses through the facility node by node. The selection of the node 
size and the route through the plant is made prior to the study by the HAZOP Chairman. 
 
The HAZOP Chairman will select the nodes based on the stage of the project and the amount of 
information available. Nodes that are very small will result in longer study times, as each 
guideword/parameter combination would have to be recorded multiple times. Large nodes, such as those 
comprising of multiple equipment and pipe work would lead to the HAZOP team potentially jumbling 
guidewords and overlooking the hazards.  
 
Following guidance should be referred for node selection: 
 
(a) 
Major equipment (e.g. Vessel, distillation column etc.); 
(b) 
Change in process fluid state (e.g. from liquid to vapor); and 
(c) 
Change in design and operating parameters. 
A system of highlighting the nodes with different colours should be used to ensure that the limits of the 
node are fully understood by the team and that the extent of coverage (covered, excluded, duplicate 
section) is clear by the end of the study. The plant items encompassed by each node should be marked 
in distinctive and separate colours, with the node numbers alongside, in the same colour. Where a node 
spans two or more drawings, the colour used should remain constant. Lines outside the scope of the 
study on revamp or minor change HAZOPs should also be identified by a unique colour. The node mark-
up master drawings should be archived with the study.   
 
7.5.2. DESIGN INTENT AND PARAMETERS 
The design intent defines design limits for a component or system. The design operating conditions of 
each applicable parameter (e.g. flow, pressure, temperature, level etc.) and normal operating conditions 
in the node must be established. HAZOP study will examine or identify potential causes that would result 
in deviations to the design intent, leading to hazardous consequences and operability issues.  
 
Design intent must be explained to HAZOP team (typically by the Process Engineer) along with the 
normal operating procedure. 
 
7.5.3. GUIDEWORDS AND DEVIATIONS 
Guidewords are simple words or phrases used to qualify or quantify the design intent and associated 
parameters in order to develop meaningful deviations. 'No', 'less', 'more', 'reverse', 'part of', 'as well as' 
and 'other than' are the guidewords for HAZOP study. 
 
Potential deviations to the design intent are established by considering a series of guidewords and 
combining them with the parameters of operation. For example, if the guideword is 'more' and parameter 
is 'pressure' then the deviation will be 'more pressure'. 
 
Guidewords, basic parameters and deviation matrix for continuous process HAZOP study is provided in 
Table 7.5.1.  
 
Deviation will be applied for normal operations, start up, shutdown, emergency shutdown, maintenance, 
routine monitoring and cleaning for the life of the plant/facility. Risk assessment shall also be conducted 
for construction, installation, pre- commissioning and de-commissioning activities as well as dismantling, 
abandonment and disposal of plant components.

7.5.4. CAUSES 
There may be many causes identified for each deviation, and all potential causes should be discussed 
as the consequences and recommendations for action may be different. All the potential causes of the 
deviation should be identified by the team brainstorming. The HAZOP Secretary will record each separate 
cause as it is identified by creating new rows on the worksheet. Generally, causes will fall into one of 
these three categories: 
 
(a) 
Human error 
(b) 
Equipment failure 
(c) 
External events 
“Double jeopardy” events shall not be considered in HAZOP study. Double jeopardy events are assuming 
multiple independent events occurring at the same time and causing a hazardous situation. For example, 
a level control failure on one vessel, causing excess liquid to enter the closed drain system and a human 
error of inadvertent opening of a valve on another system connected to same closed drain system causing 
liquids to the closed drain when the closed drain system is not expected to handle liquids from both 
vessels. If the causes are independent, they are considered double jeopardy. Careful consideration 
should be given to common mode failures and process dependencies before determining the causes are 
mutually exclusive.  
 
Causes identified for the deviation must be within the node being studied e.g. equipment failure should 
be considered within the node. External impact is considered as a Cause if the impact is within the 
identified node. The exception is the battery limit or border node. If the node starts from a battery limit,

7.5.5. CONSEQUENCES 
Having identified the credible causes of the deviation, the team members shall analyse and assess the 
significance of the consequences. For e.g. guideword 'No Flow' due to the 'Cause' of manual isolation 
valve closure at the discharge of centrifugal pump would result in a 'Consequence' of over-pressurization 
leading to leakage from pipeline gaskets, causing fire, explosion, environmental impact. 
 
A hazard (event) will have associated impact from one or more consequences: Health & Safety, 
Environment, Reputation and Financial. The severity of the consequences of the deviation must be 
assessed (using the ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix in Appendix 1) and recorded without giving any credit 
to safeguards, i.e. assuming that there are no controls in place. If a hazard (event) leads to more than 
one consequence, use the highest severity level to represent the severity of the event as overall severity 
of the hazard (event). 
 
Consequences of the deviation identified in the node being studied can be within the node or outside the 
node being studied.

The team must identify all the existing safeguards that are available. The team shall discuss and agree 
on the effectiveness of the safeguard in preventing the consequences from occurring. Safeguard 
(Controls) for the scenarios can be within the node or outside the node being studied. 
 
7.5.7. RISK ASSESSMENT 
Risk Assessment shall primarily focus on 'Inherent safe design concept' by ensuring that the existing/ 
proposed engineering controls effectively function properly on demand. Risk ranking must be assigned 
based on severity of consequence considering failures of all safeguards.  
 
If the estimated severity level of consequence is 4 or above ('Major', 'Catastrophic' or 'Disastrous') no 
further risk ranking in HAZOP is required and that specific hazard (deviation) must be further assessed 
using LOPA technique for mitigated and residual risk ranking.  
 
For consequences with estimated severity levels of 3 or below, the mitigated risk shall be assessed by 
determining the likelihood of the event, considering all existing safeguards are in place. If existing 
safeguards are not able to reduce the risk to tolerable region and demonstrate ALARP, then 
recommendation is proposed for the introduction of additional safeguards, following which the 'Residual 
Risk' shall be assigned considering effective implementation of the recommendation. The ADNOC 
Corporate Risk matrix (Appendix 1) shall be used to determine the likelihood. Risk ranking could also be 
used for prioritizing implementation of recommendations.

7.5.8. RECOMMENDATIONS 
The purpose of the HAZOP meeting is to discover potential hazards of the process and its operation. It 
is generally not within its scope to try to solve the sometimes-complex problems and stipulate detailed 
actions. A prescriptive action recommendation may be counter-productive in that, it may inhibit the 
engineer from considering an alternative and perhaps a better solution. Recommendations shall be 
clearly worded to explain the intent with adequate references to the system, equipment etc. While wording 
recommendations, avoid using words such as “may”, “ensure”, “assure”, “consider”, “if necessary”, “if 
feasible”, etc.,  which may lead to a sense of ambiguity to the recommendation. 
 
Closed (simple) Actions 
 
It is preferable for the team to agree and recommend simple actions, which can be defined as 'closed', 
because they do not ask the responding engineer to consider options. The team should be of enough 
experience and seniority in order to make design decisions for the project. The team should therefore be 
able to take decisions whilst not spending undue time developing solutions to complex problems. 
 
Open (complex) Actions 
 
If the team is divided in its view, or if there is not enough time available to complete the discussion, an 
open action (recommendation for further work) may be appropriate. It is most important that the team's 
concern is clearly established in the worksheet so that the responding engineer knows the scope of study 
required and the objective he is trying to achieve. It is often helpful to offer a preferred solution whilst 
giving the responding engineer scope to suggest alternatives. 
 
Conditional Recommendations 
 
The team will occasionally propose changes to one node that are conditional on the acceptance of 
recommendations made on another node considered earlier. These will be invalidated if the earlier 
recommendations are rejected by the SME and hence the link between the two recommendations must 
be explicit in the minutes. Hence the use of conditional recommendations should be minimised as there 
is a potential threat that, in the course of HAZOP follow-up, the team’s primary concern may be lost. 
 
7.5.9. LINKAGE WITH LAYER OF PROTECTION ANALYSIS (LOPA) 
LOPA shall be conducted for identified hazards (deviations) in HAZOP studies that result in 
consequences with estimated severity level of 4 or above ('Major', 'Catastrophic' or 'Disastrous'). LOPA 
shall also be conducted for hazards with estimated severity level of 3, if recommended by HAZOP study 
team. 
 
LOPA relies on the result of the HAZOP for hazards (deviations) and associated initiating causes. 
Therefore, it is important that all hazards and initiating causes are captured during the HAZOP. 
 
It is also preferable that LOPA be performed immediately after the HAZOP study with the same team that 
participated in the HAZOP study.

7.5.11. HAZOP STUDY TEAM 
Team size 
 
A HAZOP team usually consists of five to eight people, although a smaller team may be sufficient for a 
small facility or a minor change to facility/system (MOC), and in these cases three to four people are 
adequate. If the team is too large, the group approach fails because so many people try to communicate 
with one another and are inhibited from working closely. It is also highly probable that a large team will 
naturally migrate towards an inner cabinet of contributors and an outer ring of spectators. If the group is 
too small, it may lack the breadth of knowledge needed to help assure completeness and creativity 
generated by multiple interactions. The HAZOP team is multi-disciplinary, and the brainstorming 
procedure relies on the broad experience of the team to identify potential hazards and operability 
problems. Individual or discipline reviews are unlikely to be able to identify all the possible inter-
departmental interfaces which are recognised as critical to the safety of complex plant. The personnel

7.7.6. ACTION CLOSE-OUT AND APPROVAL 
It is important that all HAZOP recommendations are reviewed, approved and followed-up to close-out at 
each of the stages of the project and operating facility. The response to the action should be returned

Following is the typical review and approval process for HAZOP actions: 
 
HAZOP Action Party 
 
Person assigned to complete the action must prepare the response to the action along with the supporting 
documentation and submit it to the reviewer.  
 
Reviewer  
 
Reviewer is typically the action party’s supervisor/Contractor Project Manager. The reviewer must ensure 
that the response meets the intent of the action and that quality checks have been performed and sign 
the action response sheet accordingly.  
 
Verifier 
 
Verifier is typically the technical authority in ADNOC or respective ADNOC Group. Verifier must ensure 
that the response meets the intent and is in compliance with ADNOC standards and procedures and 
subsequently sign the action response sheet. If the recommendations of HAZOP team is modified or 
rejected, then the verifier should consult the team that had carried out the respective HAZOP before 
signing off the action response. 
 
Approver 
 
Approver is typically the person in charge of a project or an operating facility from ADNOC Group. 
Approver must ensure that the responses for the action have been reviewed and verified by relevant 
technical authorities and signs off the action as complete.  
 
During the asset development stage, the CONTRACTOR shall issue the action response for ADNOC 
Group review and approval. Based on the nature of the recommendation, the respective technical 
authority from the Company shall approve all the recommendations.

HAZOP technique focuses on the design details as represented on a P&ID and the human interaction as

INTEGRATING HUMAN ERRORS (FAILURE) INTO THE HAZOP (Informative) 
1. 
General  
Investigations of serious accidents in the process industry regularly highlight human failures as the cause. 
Human failures can occur during operating and maintenance activities or when responsible authority fails 
to take action to prevent the escalation of hazardous events.  The objective of inclusion of Human Factors 
in HAZOP is to demonstrate that all reasonable measures have been taken to minimize risks associated 
with human failures.  Possible hazardous situations caused by human errors, should be seen as human 
process interaction (e.g., accidents that could be prevented by better training or instructions, better 
methods of operation, better design).  
Human error shall not be mixed with 'human factor engineering' and further intentional errors shall not be 
considered in HAZOP study e.g. deliberate tempering, by-passing or defeating of HSECES, changing set 
point or action alarm of HSECES or changing operating parameters. 
Types of Human Failures and Performance Factors  
 
Human failures usually are not random and there are different patterns or types that exist: 
(ii) 
 Unintentional errors - such as slips, lapses or mistakes  
(iii) 
Intentional errors - such as violations  
(iv) 
Physical (decisions)   
(v) 
Mental (decisions)  
The different types of human failures are important in making an assessment, and have different causes, 
which needs to be managed differently. The mental types of human failures are dependent upon the 
skills sets that the operator is required to perform the critical tasks and activities.  
Skill based: These are mental but automated routines which the operator needs to perform with little 
conscious attention.  
Knowledge based: These are mental activities which do not require routines or rules for handling and 
managing situations but need conscious attention. 
Rule based: These mental activities are rules based such as; if symptom X is identified, then cause is Y, 
and if there is cause Y, then act to do Z.
