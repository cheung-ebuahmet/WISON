1. 
INTRODUCTION 
As an operator of Oil and Gas assets and associated facilities, ADNOC has a general duty to take all 
measures necessary to prevent major accidents and to limit their consequences for human health and 
the environment.  To comply with this duty, it is required to adopt a structured and systematic approach 
for proactively identifying the hazards associated with a work, qualitatively assessing the risk and 
determining appropriate control measures. 
Whenever work has to be undertaken by ADNOC, Third Parties or Contractors on ADNOC Controlled 
Areas or in the immediate vicinity of Pipelines operated by ADNOC and Group Companies, it is essential 
to perform proper assessments of the Health, Safety and Environment to employees, contractors and 
others, for the purpose of identifying adequate and effective control measures. Such control measures 
shall be defined in accordance with the principles of ALARP (As Low As Reasonably Practicable). 
1.1. 
KEY ELEMENTS OF JOB SAFETY ANALYSIS (JSA) 
The ADNOC Job Safety Analysis process is described in a 3-step process which is pictorially depicted 
below along-with a brief summary. 
 
 
1.1.1. 
IDENTIFY AND DESCRIBE 
This step shall include clearly defining and documenting sufficient information about the nature and scope 
of work and any special tools involved, to facilitate an appropriate Job Safety Analysis (JSA) and ensure 
that the controls necessary to reduce the risks to ALARP can be properly identified. 
1.1.2. 
ASSESS HAZARDS AND CONTROLS 
The PTW standard identifies three Permit pathways based on the nature of activity associated with the 
work scope.  They are respectively Hot Work, Critical Work and General Work permits. The type of permit 
also determines the level of JSA required.  
A decision making tool called the “Work Categorization Chart” has been developed which assists in the 
determination of the permit type and level of JSA required at the Integrated Work Activity Planning (IWAP) 
stage. Please refer to WMS Standard: PTW for Work Categorization Chart.

The Work Categorization Chart will provide guidance to a decision making framework determining 
whether: 
(i) 
The nature of work activity has been previously assessed Hot Work, Critical or General by 
the respective Group Companies; 
(ii) 
The existing JSA matches the method statement/ activity description for the planned work 
activity; 
(iii) 
The existing JSA identifies all of the hazards associated with the planned work and specify 
appropriate control measures; 
(iv) 
The type of permit required depending on the nature of the work activity and the appropriate 
JSA level respectively. 
Once the JSA route and level is identified based on the nature of work activity, JSA shall be conducted 
with the following steps: 
(i) 
The possible hazards associated with the work are identified; and 
(ii) 
Preventative and recovery control measures and precautions necessary to reduce the level 
of risk to ALARP are identified. 
1.1.3. AGREE AND APPROVE CONTROLS 
In this element, a competent authority is required to approve the adequacy of the specified control 
measures as meeting the principles of ALARP, i.e. to reduce the risks of the hazards to the level 
considered “As Low As Reasonably Practicable”.  
For Critical and Hot Work permits, the controls are approved by Asset Approval Authority (AAA). For 
General permits, the controls are approved by Area Authority (AA).

2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this document is to establish mandatory requirements for conducting Job Safety Analysis 
prior to commencement of work (using ADNOC PTW system) in areas under control of ADNOC and its 
group companies. Job Safety Analysis is a key element of the Work Management System Framework to 
ensure that any work activities conducted are properly controlled coordinated and communicated. 
The Job Safety Analysis is carried out to ensure that content and location of a work scope are clearly 
defined, potential hazards of the work site and each of the tasks or activities to be performed are identified, 
the significance of hazards is adequately assessed and sufficient control measures are defined to reduce 
the risks to as low as reasonably practicable. 
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document shall be applicable to all ADNOC group companies, employees, contractors, facilities 
(including interface areas), activities and services without exception. 
Contractors shall be responsible for ensuring that all activities performed within ADNOC operating 
facilities in the course of carrying out their contractual requirements are managed in accordance with the 
requirements of this document and reported, when applicable, to the contract holder. 
In cases, where work is to be carried out on non-ADNOC facilities/greenfield locations, work may be 
managed under an alternate Safe system of Work. Where Contractor Safe System of work is 
implemented, Contract Administrator shall ensure that it is compliant with ADNOC requirements.  
This standard shall be used and read in conjunction with other WMS standards. WMS Framework 
standard contains governing principles such as custodianship, deviations, document retention, training 
and competence assurance which are applicable to all WMS standards.  
 
4. 
LAWS AND REGULATIONS 
There are no specific laws and regulations applicable to this standard.

of further reduction becomes disproportional to the additional 
risk reduction that would be achieved.

additional authorization and control for a number of activities.

Excavation 
A man-made cut, cavity, trench, or depression in the earth's 
surface formed by earth removal irrespective of depth. It 
includes breaking into ground (including drilling), cutting into 
floors, walls, or ceilings and penetrating areas with presence of 
underground cables or pipes. 
H2S Zone 
An area of the operational plant or pipeline, within a restricted 
area, where there is potential for, high levels of H2S to be 
present in the process stream. Four Zones: Green, Yellow, 
Amber and Red are defined, each with its own specific PPE & 
access control requirements, as specified within ADNOC CoP. 
V4-10.  
Red Zone is considered to exist if there is a significant risk of 
H2S release, which could be rapidly lethal. The Toxic LSIR level 
for Red Zones is 1E-3 per year.  
Amber Zone is considered to exist where persons affected 
would have time to don PPE carried ready for use, but would 
not have time to reach the nearest PPE location. The Toxic 
LSIR level for Amber Zones is 1E-4 per year.  
Yellow Zone is considered to exist if there is a possibility of H2S 
release, which could be rapidly injurious. The Toxic LSIR level 
for Yellow Zones is 1E- 5 per year.  
Green Zone is considered to exist where H2S concentrations 
above the 8 hour TWA limit is foreseeable, but the area is not in 
an otherwise classified zone. The Toxic LSIR level for Green 
Zones is 1E-6 per year. 
Hazard 
Anything with the potential to cause injury, ill health, damage to 
property or the environment.

definitions of Zone 0, 1, 2, 20, 21 or 22.

Authority

APPENDIX NO. 2. 
OVERVIEW ON ALARP PRINCIPLES 
The fundamental obligation placed upon the Oil and Gas Industry is to reduce all risks to safety to a level 
that is as low as is reasonably practicable (ALARP). This obligation is based on the principle that those 
who create and have control over risks have responsibility for their management and must actively 
assess them in order to ensure that sufficient risk reduction measures are implemented such that the 
residual risk is ALARP.  
The fundamental principle of risk-based hazard management is that whilst risks cannot always be 
completely eliminated, it should be possible to reduce them to a level that is ALARP, where they are 
tolerable to society because all reasonably practicable risk reduction measures have been implemented.  
The meaning of the word “tolerable” is important. The diagram below illustrates the principles of 
tolerability limits and relationship to ALARP. 
 
 
The triangle represents an increasing level of cumulative risk (all risks that a person, or population are 
exposed to) from a low risk situation in green at the base of the triangle to a high risk, red region at the 
top.

Identify Hazards and Assess Risks 
Most hazard identification techniques involve a team approach, since an individual 
generally may not have the depth of experience and expertise on all aspects of the site, 
activity and hazards.  
Group interactions are more likely to stimulate consideration of hazards that even well-
informed individuals might overlook.  Operational staff whose daily work exposes them to 
such hazards can make a valuable contribution to the hazard identification process.   
The Risk Assessment Team shall examine each work activity to identify the hazards, 
hazardous events and potential incidents whether they arise from the actions of people or 
from the conditions surrounding the activity. The assessment should identify who or what 
might be harmed, how could a loss occur or what could pose a threat to the environment. 
Hazards are assessed on the basis of their Inherent risk rating.  To estimate the Inherent 
risk potential of each hazard, RA team shall evaluate the hazards on the basis of the 
following: 
1. 
Worst Case Credible Scenarios for Severity of the hazard  
2. 
Likelihood or Probability of the worst case hazard severity scenario being realized 
The Risk Level shall then be assessed using the approved ADNOC RAM and a RAM Rating 
assigned to the Hazard. The RAM Rating can be used to guide the extent of control 
measures that should be implemented to meet the principles of ALARP before the cost of 
additional preventative or protective measures becomes disproportionate to the risk 
reduction achieved. 
(iii) 
Identify Controls and Recovery Measures 
For each identified hazard, the RA Team shall determine and identify the necessary hazard 
control measures.  Where practical, the hierarchy of control for risk management should be 
applied. 
Hierarchy of Controls and Principles of ALARP: The ALARP principle recognizes that no 
industrial activity is entirely free from risk. For any situation, there is a level of risk where it 
can be shown that the cost of additional preventative or protective measures is 
disproportionate to the risk reduction achieved. Work can proceed safely when the potential 
hazards, defined risks and intended control measures reduce the risk of harm to ALARP. 
Please refer to Appendix 3 for more details on ALARP principles.

actually eliminated or mitigated altogether or its impact reduced considerably before
