1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Environmental Impact Assessments (EIAs) are typically undertaken for proposed new projects and 
substantially altered existing facilities within the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) Group.  
 
EIA is a systematic procedure for the identification and assessment of environmental impacts associated 
with an activity, project or process, and subsequent determination of appropriate mitigation and 
monitoring practices. 
 
The process of EIA is an integral component of the overall Health, Safety and Environmental Impact 
Assessment (HSEIA) process and therefore reference should be made to ADNOC HSEIA Standard [Ref. 
1].  
 
The EIA process shall start at the earliest possible stage of a project to verify the environmental feasibility 
of a project in order to identify the important environmental aspects for further consideration in the 
following phases of a project. Such early environmental considerations may play a critical and 
fundamental role in determining proposed locations of pipelines, facilities and industrial plants, for 
example. 
 
The output of the EIA process is presented in the form of an EIA Report, which provides details of various 
analyses and assessments conducted during the EIA, the methodologies and assumptions used, 
information on the predicted significant impacts and their mitigation, and commitments for subsequent 
environmental management and monitoring. 
 
The EIA Report is to be submitted to ADNOC Group Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) for approval 
during the all phases of a project. 
2. 
PURPOSE 
This document presents the procedures for conducting an EIA for ADNOC Group projects, describes 
when such studies are necessary, and sets out related studies and management plans that may need to 
be completed depending on the nature of the project.  
 
This Standard also sets out the format and content of the EIA Report. The EIA Report is the means by 
which the outcomes of the EIA study are communicated to ADNOC Group HSE Function (GHSEF), 
including commitments made with regard to environmental mitigation and management measures. The 
statement of commitments outlined in the EIA report will serve as the binding agreement between ADNOC 
GHSE Function and the ADNOC Group (Business Directorates, GCs & Affiliates).  
  
The EIA forms an integral part of a project’s HSEIA, and hence this standard should be read in conjunction 
with ADNOC HSEIA Standard [Ref.1]. 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions at Headquarters (HQ), Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

LAWS AND REGULATIONS 
ADNOC operates under the guidance and directions of the Abu Dhabi Supreme Petroleum Council (SPC). 
By virtue of Article 94 of Federal Law No 24 of 1999 [Ref. 2], ADNOC Group is exempted from external 
regulatory scrutiny.  
 
Notwithstanding the exemption from external scrutiny, ADNOC Group Companies (GCs) must ensure 
that their operations comply with all relevant United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Abu Dhabi environmental 
laws and regulations. This requirement extends to relevant obligations arising from International 
Conventions to which the UAE is a signatory. The GCs must also fulfil the requirements imposed by 
international shareholders and/ or international finance institutions if and when applicable.  
 
The principal laws governing the process of EIA in the UAE, or have relevance to it, are: 
(i) 
Federal Law No. 24 of 1999 for the Protection and Development of the Environment [3] and 
its Executive Regulations. 
(ii) 
Law No 21 of 2005 for Waste Management in the Emirate of Abu Dhabi 
(iii) 
Council and Ministers Decree No. 12 of 2006 Regulation Concerning Protection of the Air 
from Pollution 
(iv) 
Soil Contamination User Guide, Environment Agency-Abu Dhabi, 2016 
(v) 
Federal Law by Decree No 6 of 2009, Concerning the Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy, 
which establishes the Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation (FANR). 
The main Articles of Federal Law No. 24 relevant to EIA are 18, 21, 35, 43, 44, 48, 53, and 59. 
 
The following Federal environmental regulations have been developed to which GCs must refer when 
necessary:   
(i) 
Regulations for the Protection of Air from pollution 
(ii) 
Regulations for the Protection of Natural Reservations 
(iii) 
Regulations for Insecticides and Agricultural Additives and Fertilisers 
(iv) 
Regulations for the Assessment of Environmental Effects of Installations 
(v) 
Regulations for the Protection of the Marine Environment. 
(vi) 
Regulation for Handling Hazardous Materials  
Where there is an absence of a particular emission limit or performance standard within the Federal or 
Abu Dhabi environmental regulations, GCs should refer to appropriate international standards (e.g. World 
Health Organisation (WHO), United States Environmental Protection Agency (US EPA), European Union 
(EU)). Additional discussion regarding the application of emission limits and environmental quality 
standards is presented within the ADNOC Pollution Prevention and Control (PPC) Standard, HSE-EN-
ST02. 
 
The Environment Agency - Abu Dhabi (EAD) is the designated Competent Authority, which oversees the 
implementation of Federal and local environmental regulations in Abu Dhabi. For ADNOC Group projects 
and activities that fall outside of ADNOC concession areas in Abu Dhabi or within EAD Environmental 
Protected Areas, an EIA must be conducted in accordance with EAD requirements. A summary of the 
procedures that must be followed in such cases is provided in Section 7.4 of this Standard. The 
submission of the EIA Report to EAD must be via ADNOC Group HSE Unit.

6. 
ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES 
6.1. 
GROUP COMPANY 
The GC shall be the ultimate owner of the HSEIA Dossier and be responsible/accountable for its 
development and implementation. 
6.1.1. HEAD OF GC CORPORATE HSE  
The Head of ADNOC Group Company Corporate HSE (GC CHSE) is the technical approver and 
custodian of the EIA Reports & HSEIA Dossiers.  The GC CHSE shall: 
 
Ensure that competent and independent subject matter experts are involved in the development of 
the EIA. GC CHSE shall review and technically approve the independent third-party consultants 
including the Curriculum Vitaes of the lead technical personnel associated with EIA Studies.; 
 
Review & technically approve the HSEIA screening study; 
 
Review & technically approve the EIA scoping report and EIA report during any stage of the project 
lifecycle (including the combined phase or modification projects); 
 
Review all technical content, clarifications & deviations on the requirements of EIA and associated 
studies and provide necessary approval; 
 
Provide approval for ‘Early Construction’ work based on the review of Construction Environmental 
Impact Identification (ENVID) and effectiveness of implemented controls; 
 
Ensure renewal of “Regulatory Approval” of the Operations EIA once in 5-years; 
 
Liaise with ADNOC GHSEF for Regulatory Approval of EIA Reports by timely submissions and 
follow up; and 
 
The GC CHSE Division shall be the custodian (single point repository) of all the GC EIA Reports & 
HSEIA Dossiers developed for various projects & operations. 
6.1.2. PROJECT MANAGER 
The Project Manager is the owner (responsible role) of the EIA Report and HSEIA Dossier during the 
project phases including first operations phase HSEIA for the project.  The Project Manager shall ensure 
that:  
 
EIA Reports & HSEIA Dossiers (which includes the EIA report and other HSE studies) are prepared 
in line with the requirements of all applicable ADNOC Standards and based on the approved HSEIA 
screening study; 
 
The independent third-party consultants, lead engineers and subject matter experts involved in 
development of EIA report and HSE studies are pre-qualified within ADNOC, proposed team is 
technically reviewed and approved by GC CHSE to ensure the competency of the personnel 
involved in the project work; 
 
Statement of Fitness is signed prior to seeking regulatory approval of the HSEIA and associated 
dossier from ADNOC GHSEF; 
 
EIA Reports and associated studies are forwarded to GC CHSE for their review and technical 
approvals; 
 
The EIA Reports & their updated versions are submitted and approved within the stipulated 
timeframes as per this standard; 
 
The recommendations arising from all environmental studies conducted during the course of the 
EIA are implemented by the project contractors and documented;

PROJECT LIFE CYCLE EIA PHASES 
An EIA shall be carried out by the ADNOC Group, or appointed third-party consultant, and submitted to

7.2. 
MAIN OBJECTIVES OF THE EIA PROCESS 
The main objective of an EIA is to evaluate quantitatively, or where this is not possible, qualitatively, the 
potentially significant impacts to the environment resulting from project activities.  This requires 
identification of all potential impacts (from routine and non-routine operations and activities) in a 
systematic manner, following which an assessment of the significance of each impact is then undertaken. 
 
Where significant adverse impacts are identified, alternative strategies, methods and/ or technologies are 
proposed ensuring that such impacts are minimised to an acceptable degree, if not removed entirely. The 
selection of appropriate mitigation measures depends upon the specific circumstances of the planned 
project, local environmental conditions and sensitivities, regulatory compliance, feasibility and cost. 
Choice of option should be informed by the need to comply with all regulatory requirements as well as 
the guiding principle of Best Available Techniques (BAT) which is discussed in additional detail within the 
ADNOC PPC Standard, HSE-EN-ST02. 
 
The EIA process helps define the environmental management and monitoring requirements of a project. 
Management requirements which are committed to are presented in supporting documents, such as the 
following: 
 
CEMP 
 
Waste Management Plan (WMP) (refer to HSE-EN-ST04 for further details) 
 
OEMP 
 
DEMP

7.2.2. WHEN IS AN EIA REQUIRED? 
ADNOC Group projects that will trigger the EIA process include, as a minimum, those listed in Section 2 
of the ADNOC Standard on HSEIA requirements [Ref. 1].   
 
In general terms, projects that can demonstrate compliance with all of the following conditions will not 
require an EIA to be undertaken: 
 
No change in the quantity or quality of existing discharges to the environment during the 
operational phase 
 
No requirement for new structures or infrastructure including additional camp facilities, utilities, 
access roads etc. 
 
No existing or future land use conflicts with the proposed project 
ADNOC Group must consult with ADNOC GHSE Function to determine the need for EIA in particular

If the EIA process is deemed necessary, the first stage is to conduct a screening study, the outcome of 
which will determine whether further work will be required and whether the project will need to proceed 
to a Scoping study and subsequent EIA stages.

KEY ELEMENTS OF AN EIA 
EIA is a process comprises a number of different elements which are executed in a logical sequence, 
starting with the identification of potentially significant issues, definition of scope, and ending with 
production of the EIA Report. The EIA Report is the formal output from the assessment and is used by

7.3.1. PROCEDURAL REQUIREMENTS



7.3.2. EIA PROCESS 
The key elements of the process are: 
 
Screening ; 
 
Scoping; 
 
Establishing the baseline; 
 
Assessment of impacts (including social impacts as required); 
 
Establishing mitigation commitments; and 
 
Developing environmental management and monitoring plans.  
 
These are discussed in further details in the subsequent sections. 
(a) 
Screening 
Screening is the process of deciding whether or not a project is likely to have significant 
environmental impacts and, therefore, whether or not an EIA is required. 
 
A Screening study involves conducting a preliminary review of the project, identifying the 
sensitivities of the site and surrounding land uses, considering the environmental releases to air, 
water and land and making a judgement on whether or not any significant impacts are likely to 
arise. The EIA Screening study and outcomes of the ENVID shall determine the requirement of 
full-fledged EIA study and the requirement for Social Impact Assessment. 
 
A walk-through survey is advised during the screening study. A screening checklist is presented in 
the ADNOC HSEIA Standard, HSE-RM-02 which may be used as a tool to ensure that all potential 
impacts are considered. If the screening process concludes that a significant environmental impact 
could potentially arise, then an EIA will be required.   
 
For ADNOC Group projects, screening criteria have been defined to assist Group Companies in 
deciding if an EIA is required for a particular project. These criteria presented in the ADNOC HSEIA 
Standard, HSE-RM-02 should be referred to when carrying out the screening assessment.  
(b) 
Preliminary Scoping Study 
Scoping is the process of identifying/confirming key issues that warrant additional assessment 
within the subsequent phases of the EIA process (i.e. what are the potentially significant impacts), 
and which aspects can be ‘scoped out’. The Scoping Report shall contain full justification with 
regards issues that warrant further detailed study and those that are ‘scoped out’. 
 
Scoping requires an evaluation of the project, a review of potential environmental and social 
impacts and their significance, and an appraisal of the most appropriate techniques for impact 
prediction.  
 
At the early stages in project planning and prior to the appointment of an EIA consultant, detailed 
information on matters such as impact potential is unlikely to be available to Group Companies. A 
preliminary scoping study will therefore need to be carried out by the GC to enable tenders for 
consultants to be prepared. The Environmental Impact Severity Matrix (EISM) detailed in Figure 
7.3.2 and the information in Appendix 1 should be used to help identify all significant environmental 
impacts.

(c) 
Final Scoping Report 
Once the EIA consultant has been appointed, the scoping study must be finalised and the final

 
Checklists; 
 
Risk Matrices; 




The choice of technique depends on the specific circumstances of the project, including previous 
experience, of the EIA project team, the nature of the project and environmental and social 
sensitivity of the area.  
 
Checklists and matrices provide a relatively simple method for identifying potentially significant 
impacts, but are not able to capture more complex issues, including interactions.  
 
Professional judgement and Brainstorming by experienced environmental specialists often 
provides a rapid solution to the scoping exercise, providing the individual(s) have the requisite 
experience to make the necessary judgements on potentially significant impacts. However, as with 
Brainstorming, this method lacks transparency and may be open to challenge. 
 
An ENVID exercise involves assembling a team of professionals from the project design team and 
key individuals from the EIA team.  The team is chaired by an experienced environmental specialist 
from GC, or for larger projects it is recommended that independent, experienced chairmen/women 
are used, and they work through a pre-prepared list of project activities and environmental aspects 
and identify which of the aspects is likely to have a significant impact. The process is 
comprehensive and provides a suitable audit trail for decisions on scoping. ENVID workshops shall 
be undertaken as outlined in HSE-RM-ST02 [Ref. 1] and HSE-RM-ST03 [Ref. 28]. 
 
The earlier ENVID carried out during screening may be updated/ used as input document for 
carrying out ENVID exercise for scoping study for larger projects where potentially significant 
impacts may be present.

definitive list of all potentially significant impacts and forms the basis of the detailed studies to be 
carried out during the EIA.





 
Brief statement on proposed methodology for the study

(d) 
Environmental Baseline  
The EIA should include a detailed evaluation of the baseline environmental conditions at the project 
site and its surroundings, including those areas outside the project site boundary where impacts 
could arise during any phase of the project life cycle.  
 
Collecting environmental baseline data at the early stages of the project will ensure that the 
information is available in time for the formal more detailed EIA required as part of the HSEIA 
process.  
 
Assessment of the significance of environmental impacts is based on identifying the magnitude of 
change in an environmental condition with a project, compared with the same condition without the 
project (i.e. the baseline condition).  It follows that without adequate baseline data, any detailed 
analysis of impacts will be compromised. 
 
Establishing the environmental baseline sensitivity of the receiving environment can be achieved 
through reviews of existing data sources, if available, but often requires the collection of primary 
field data. This may include, for example, information on local ecological resources, air pollution 
marine water or groundwater quality and noise levels. Where relevant, the environmental baseline 
study shall establish a social baseline and shall include the identification of social impacts.  
 
Where baseline information for a particular area is available from previous studies (including 
previous EIA studies), this should be used as input to the EIA process. However, this is only the 
case if it is judged that the existing data are a) recently acquired b) relevant and c) of sufficient 
quality to be used for subsequent impact assessment analysis. However, use of such data should 
be approved by the GC in consultation with ADNOC GHSEF, and may be done within the scoping 
process. 
 
When considering the appropriateness of existing data with regards to how recently it was collected, 
numerous factors will be considered on a case-by-case basis. For example, the likelihood of such 
conditions to have changed over the time period in question should be considered, as well as 
currently available measurement technologies, resources, etc. A 5-year ‘rule of thumb’ is 
sometimes applied, aligning with the 5-year HSE audit review cycle, but this should not be 
considered as an exhaustive rule. In general, data should however not be more than five years old. 
 
Collection of environmental baseline data can be both expensive and time consuming – it can in 
some cases require extended time periods covering several weeks or different seasons of the year.   
 
It is therefore essential that project planning and financing have sufficient flexibility to allow 
collection of essential baseline information for the EIA, without conflicts arising with the overall 
project development programme. The baseline collection exercise, including social baseline where 
relevant, should therefore commence during Front End Engineering Design (FEED) stage of the 
project life cycle, after the completion of the Scoping Study. 
 
What baseline data must be collected and over what time periods requires specialist expertise and 
judgements.  Where this expertise is not available within Group Companies, advice will be required 
from ADNOC Group HSE Function and/or the EIA consultant on the scope of any baseline data 
collection exercise.

(f) 
Climate Change  
Major industrial and developmental projects can lead to large-scale releases of Green House 
Gases (GHG’s), including carbon dioxide (CO2), methane (CH4), sulphur hexafluoride (SF6) and 
nitrous oxide (N2O). In general, all projects should assess their ‘carbon footprint’, review possible 
measures to reduce GHG emissions and assess any impacts the project may have on carbon sinks.  
 
Further assess potential local climate change risks on the project site and its associated 
infrastructure and control and adaptation measures to incorporate in design and operational phase.    
 
Emission sources will be identified, applicable GHG regulations and reporting protocols determined, 
and a GHG inventory prepared for various phases of project (construction, operation, 
decommissioning). Ways in which GHG’s will be minimised shall be communicated including any 
measurement and monitoring methods that will be utilised. The quantification of project emissions 
in carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2eq) shall at least include Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions in 
accordance with internationally recognised methods and good practices.  
 
Market based mechanisms for energy/ GHG reduction should also be considered. Where such 
opportunities are identified, details will be communicated within the EIA Report.

(g) 
Impact Predictions 
Impact predictions may be quantitative (e.g. quantities of waste generated, wastewater generated, 
air pollutant and noise levels) or qualitative (community perceptions, visual impacts, intrinsic 
ecological value). Best international practice should be applied at all times to impact prediction.  
 
Computer simulation models are frequently used in EIA studies to predict environmental impacts.  
Where such models are used, it is essential that they conform to the following standards: 
 
Fully validated and approved by an international regulatory body of appropriate status (e.g. 
US EPA, UK (United Kingdom) Environment Agency (EA)); 
 
Demonstrable track record of use in similar applications; 
 
Provide output in an easily interpretable format, including graphics. 
Input for atmospheric dispersion models, water quality models and others must be from 
authoritative sources such as US EPA, AP-42 [Ref. 10] or Oil and Gas producers (OGP) Methods 
for Estimating Atmospheric Emissions from exploration and production (E&P) Operations, 
International Association of Oil and Gas Producers (IAOGP) [Ref.11]. 
 
Where predictive modelling is used for the EIA, the EIA report must contain information justifying 
the use of selected models and basic model assumptions used. 
(h) 
Impact Assessment And Significance 
As noted in Section 7, assessing the overall significance of predicted impacts is potentially complex 
and requires input from environmental specialists. There is no universally agreed ‘significance 
model’ and different approaches are used, depending on the nature and magnitude of the impact 
and the sensitivity of the surrounding environment.  
 
Four types of environmental risk need to be considered: 
 
One-off impacts associated with activities such as civil engineering, commissioning and 
abandonment. 
 
Routine impacts associated with planned normal and abnormal operating conditions (e.g. 
maintenance, start up and shut down). 
 
Non-routine impacts such as those arising from process trip and emergency start-ups & shut 
downs, fire, and explosion. 
 
Historical impacts due to past activities. 
The extent to which these four types of impacts need to be addressed in the EIA will be dependent 
on the location in which the project is to be developed, environmental sensitivity of the site/area, 
the impact duration and effect, the historical impacts nearby or in the area and the operating 
characteristics of the facility.  
 
As a general rule, EIAs should also, where relevant, consider the use of raw materials, water usage 
and optimisation, and GHG emissions quantification and reduction. Any optimisation initiatives that 
have been adopted by the project will be communicated within the EIA Report. 
 
In all circumstances, the EIA must address all environmental impacts from routine releases to the

Significant Environmental Impacts 
The significance of a planned and accidental environmental impact depends on a number of factors 
including, but not limited to: 
 
Impact magnitude/severity (geographical scale); 
 
Impact duration (short, medium or long term); 
 
Reversibility (the ability of an impact to be rectified, either naturally or with intervention); 
 
Impact interactions and cumulative effects; 
 
Whether the impact is positive (beneficial) or negative (adverse), direct or indirect; and 
 
The sensitivity of the receiving environment. 
The sensitivity of the receiving environment is a key factor in deciding impact significance. An 
impact of a certain magnitude which is considered to be significant at one location may not, for 
example, be significant in another area where the receiving environment is less sensitive.

7.3.3. ENVIRONMENTAL INPUT TO THE DESIGN PROCESS 
The EIA process must be conducted in parallel and effectively integrated with the project design. This 
means that relevant information arising from the EIA must be fed into the design process in a timely 
fashion, thus allowing any mitigation requirements to be incorporated into the design.  
 
As a minimum, environmental specialists conducting the EIA must contribute to the various PHSER 
stages and be responsible for providing the design team with timely advice on mitigation requirements 
even though this may be preliminary in nature.   
 
Environmental input into the design process also encompasses assurance that BAT has been, or will be, 
incorporated into the design at all stages of the design process.  
 
The requirement for specialist environmental input into the design process is separate from but 
complementary to, environmental design input provided by the engineering design team.

Each EIA report must be subject to thorough quality assurance (QA) by suitably experienced and 
competent person(s) for correctness and full coverage.  This must be completed prior to submitting the 
report to ADNOC Group HSE Unit and GC CHSE respectively.
