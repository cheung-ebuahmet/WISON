1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Industry experience shows that fires or explosions in Oil & Gas and petrochemical facilities can result in 
extensive losses of property and lives. Realistic quantification of credible physical effects from accident 
loss of containment scenarios can be used to manage the associated risks by providing engineering 
solutions and procedural control. 
  
Fire & Explosion Risk Assessment (FERA) is a structured and systematic process to identify and assess 
risks from fire and explosion hazards. The results of this assessment are used to ensure safe facility 
layouts, specify passive and active fire protection requirements, and provide input for Escape and 
Evacuation Risk Assessment (EERA) [Ref. 2], Emergency Systems Survival Assessment (ESSA) [Ref. 
3], Building Risk Assessment (BRA) and Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) [Ref. 4] studies.  
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this standard is to define the approach to be used in conducting any FERA Studies for 
ADNOC facilities. The objectives of a FERA include: 
 
1. 
Identifying potential fire and explosion hazards in a facility or during an activity; 
2. 
Identifying the impact of fires and explosions on plant equipment, and facilities; 
3. 
Providing recommendations to minimize the severity of fires and explosions; 
4. 
Defining the required protection measures which control or mitigate the effects of fires and 
explosions; and 
5. 
Assessing opportunities to reduce risks further from fire and explosion hazards. 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to the ADNOC Group and its 
Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented, complied with and monitored at all times. 
 
It does not apply to fires inside buildings or to non-hydrocarbon fires such as electrical fires, 
cellulose/paper/metal fires etc. Further, this standard describes the process for assessing risks to assets 
only. Assessment of personnel risk (including toxic gas risk to occupants of buildings) shall be covered 
in the QRA [Ref. 4].  
 
Fires within the scope of this standard include: 
 
 
Gas fires - jet fires and low momentum releases; 
 
Liquid fires - pool fires and pressurized spray fires; 
 
Two phase fires - flashing liquids or gas with entrained liquid droplets; 
 
Blowouts – during drilling, production, work over, well service or subsea; 
 
Fireball - fire from a catastrophic release of a large quantity of pressurized gas or volatile liquid; 
 
Boiling Liquid Expanding Vapour Explosion (BLEVE) - catastrophic failure of a vessel containing 
volatile liquids following sustained fire exposure; and 
 
Boil-over - sudden steam generation in tanks storing liquid hydrocarbons at atmospheric conditions, 
causing a massive ejection of tank contents.

7.1. 
FERA LIFECYCLE 
Typically during concept phase, consequence modeling is carried out to assist in layout planning, 
determine the separation distances, etc. Comprehensive FERA is performed during the FEED Stage of 
the project and is later updated during the EPC (Detailed Engineering) Stage based on newly established 
information/data/engineering documentations etc. EPC FERA study also forms the basis for the 
operations and forms part of HSEIA dossier.  
 
For existing facilities, where FERA is not available, it shall be carried out at the first available opportunity 
(earliest) and update the HSEIA Dossier accordingly as per ADNOC HSE Impact Assessment (HSEIA) 
Standard, HSE-RM-ST02 [Ref. 6].   
 
As part of COMAH/HSEIA update, FERA shall be reviewed to determine if significant changes to facilities, 
fire protection measures & supporting system, manning, buildings, etc. are observed or carried out.  FERA 
study shall be updated to address these changes to ensure assessment reflect the updated changes, fire 
protection measures and supporting systems. HSEIA Dossier shall be updated and approved accordingly, 
as per ADNOC HSE Impact Assessment (HSEIA) Standard, HSE-RM-ST02 [Ref. 6]. 
 
Where no significant changes are identified over five years and outcome of previous FERA report is still 
applicable and technically robust, no update of FERA is required.  In such case, GC CHSE shall develop 
technical note justifying the same with relevant supporting documents (such as HAZID, risk register 
review, risk assessments, MOC, etc.) and shall submit to ADNOC for regulatory approval along with 
previous FERA report as part of HSEIA process for regulatory approval.

7.2. 
FIRE, EXPLOSION & ESCALATION  
There are many fire & explosion events that could potentially occur on an oil and gas and petrochemical 
processing facility.  These range from small electrical fires to large process fires, vapour cloud explosions, 
etc.  The FERA focuses on those fire & explosion events that could have a significant impact on the 
overall risk levels to personnel and asset either directly or by escalation, i.e. hydrocarbon fires and 
explosions. Fire and explosion scenarios which are studied in FERA are as below.  
 
 
Fire scenarios (jet fire, pool fire, tank fires); 
 
Explosions (Vapour Cloud Explosion, Dust Explosion, etc.); and 
 
Escalation (vessel burst, BLEVE, Boilover, etc.). 
 
7.2.1. FIRE  
Jet Fire: 
Jet fire arises from ignition of a high velocity gas, aerosol, 2 phase or liquid release, usually from a 
pressurized source.  Pressurized liquefied gases which will not form liquid pools upon release will 
disperse as a vaporizing liquid aerosol jet. Ignition of liquid or a vapour jet results in a jet fire which may 
cause impinging flames with high radiation intensities. The length and width of unobstructed jet flames 
vary as a function of the pressure upstream of the leak, the size and geometry of the hole and the wind 
speed.  The composition of the fluid including water cut plays an important role in determining the jet fire 
intensity.   
 
Pool Fire: 
Pool fire arise where pool formation of flammable/combustible liquids burns.  Based on the composition, 
pool fire can generate large quantities of smoke. Liquid pools can be formed by all hydrocarbon products 
containing pentane and heavier components. Additionally, propane and lighter refrigerated/cryogenic 
liquefied gases may also form liquid pools in the event of accidental leakage while they are handled at 
temperatures at or near their atmospheric boiling points.  Size of the pool and smoke generation plays 
an important role in determining the radiated heat and therefore needs to be taken into account while 
determining the Surface Emissive Power (SEP) of pool fires.  
 
Tank Fires: 
Storage tank containing flammable and combustible material can undergo various tank fire scenario 
based on the type of tanks and associated dykes.  These are classified as full surface fires, rim seal fires, 
bund fires, etc.  Based on the size and volume the impact and duration of fires may vary from couple of 
hours to days.  If not provided with sufficient protection measures can escalate to scenarios such as 
boilover.  
 
7.2.2. EXPLOSIONS 
Vapour cloud explosion 
Explosions are due to the sudden release of energy over a very short period of time and may or may not 
involve combustion or other chemical reactions. It is possible for a fire to lead to an explosion and an 
explosion might lead to a fire and secondary explosions if combustible gases or liquids are involved.  An 
explosion is defined as a rapid expansion of gases resulting in a rapidly moving pressure or shock wave 
of sufficient magnitude to cause potential damage or injury. 
 
A vapour cloud explosion is results of release of flammable material in the atmosphere, a subsequent 
release phase, and after some delay, an ignition of the vapour cloud.  Vapour cloud explosions generated 
from a release of flammable vapour can explode with widespread damage to plant and property and injury 
and or fatalities to people. A flame must propagate at a considerable speed or cloud has to be ignited 
very strongly to cause vapour cloud to explode.

Dust explosion 
A dust explosion is the rapid combustion of fine particles suspended in the air within an enclosed location. 
Dust explosions can occur where any dispersed powdered combustible or flammable material is present 
in high-enough concentrations in the atmosphere or other oxidizing gaseous medium, such as oxygen. 
Following conditions necessary conditions for a dust explosion.  
 
 
Dust is combustible or flammable and with particle size to allow combustion;  
 
The dust is suspended in the air at a sufficiently high concentration (flammable range); 
 
There is an oxidant (typically atmospheric oxygen); 
 
There is an ignition source; and 
 
The area is confined - a building can be considered an enclosure. 
 
Operations within ADNOC Group where dust explosion is possible is sulphur dust/sulphur granulation 
plant/sulphur handling, cement dust, polyethylene, propylene within the enclosed plant, etc.  
 
7.2.3. ESCALATION  
BLEVE/Fireball: 
Once a fire or explosion/fire sequence of hazardous events has started, escalation may occur due to 
vessel rapture scenarios due to high pressure arising from over-pressurization and/or reduction in vessel 
strength due to overheating from external fires.  A vessel rupture produces three primary effects: 
 
 
Release of vessel content; 
 
Fragmentation of the vessel coupled with missile effects; and 
 
Blast due to the expansion of the pressurized contents. 
The release of secondary content can cause secondary effects, such as buoyant fireball with heat 
radiation (if escalation is due to fire), a vapor cloud explosion, a flash fire or dispersion of a toxic material.   
 
A BLEVE Is explosion resulting from the failure of a vessel containing a liquid at a temperature 
significantly above its boiling point at normal atmospheric pressure, e.g. liquefied petroleum gases.  The 
fluid in the vessel is usually combination of liquid and vapor.  If the vessel ruptures, vapor is vented and 
the pressure in the liquid drops significantly.  This leads to instantaneous boiling throughout the liquid, 
through this process, a large fraction of the liquid can be vaporized in a very short time. The liberated 
energy in such case is very high causing high blast pressure and fireball due to ignition from external fire.  
 
Boilover 
The boilover phenomenon is defined as a violent ejection of fuel due to the vaporization of a water sub-
layer, resulting in an enormous fire enlargement and formation of boilover heat radiation and ground fire.

the presence of a water sub-layer, and the apparition of a zone of nearly constant temperature inside the

7.3. 
FERA METHODOLOGY  
FERA consist of assessment of fire zone, passive fire protection assessment, explosion assessment, 
escalation assessment and review of active fire protection accordingly, FERA methodology consists of 
following steps: 
 
(i) 
All assumptions used in FERA shall be developed on a “conservative best estimate” basis. 
All assumptions shall be documented, reviewed and approved by relevant SME from 
ADNOC Group CHSE within the concerned entity (Directorate, Functions, Divisions, etc.) 
across ADNOC Group prior to use. Refer to assumptions and modeling parameters defined 
in ADNOC Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) Standard, HSE-RM-ST10 [Ref. 4]; 
(ii) 
Identification of fire, explosion and flammable gas hazards i.e. flammable gas dispersion, jet 
fire, pool fire, Vapor Cloud Explosion (VCE), dust explosion etc. Section 7.4; 
(iii) 
Estimate hazard inventories based on isolatable sections and failure cases, i.e. isolation 
valve to isolation valve (for normal operating conditions); 
(iv) 
Determination of failure probabilities based on the part count method for each of the 
identified failure cases;  
(v) 
Define locations and facilities for control and mitigation, i.e. isolation and blowdown systems, 
etc. using P&IDs, PFDs, Plot Plans and other relevant details, including all major equipment 
items; 
(vi) 
Identify Potential Explosion Sites (PES) based on review of the plot plans, 3D models, PFDs, 
site visits, potential for gas accumulation, congestion and confinement within the 
plant/facilities and nearby areas. For each of the PES determine the blast potential using 
guidance in this standard;  
(vii) 
Identify receptors to be evaluated as part of FERA study. These are typically adjacent 
hydrocarbon handling equipment, critical structures, primary structure members, HSECES, 
EER measures and supporting structures and systems, buildings, etc.;  
(viii) Undertake consequence modeling for all identified isolatable sections and failure case 
scenarios (Section 7.7 and 7.8) using latest version of consequence modeling software 
package approved by ADNOC for various release profile, weather conditions, etc.  
Consequence modeling shall take into consideration the effects of engineering barriers 
which are in place and failure effect of those barriers i.e. isolation with blowdown, isolation 
with no blow down, and no isolation with no blowdown, etc.; 
(ix) 
Determine event probabilities for each of these failure cases considering all probabilities 
such as direction probabilities, wind profile, failure probabilities, ignition probabilities, etc.; 
(x) 
For each of the failure case, review the impact of fire, explosion events on the facility, and 
in particular: 
1. 
Assess the impact on the adjacent equipment, HSECES, key structures, EER 
measures and supporting structure/elements/system, etc. (Section 7.5); and 
2. 
Assess the impact on occupied process buildings, normally unmanned and/or 
buildings housing HSE Critical Equipment or required for emergency management 
(e.g. ECR, fire station, control room, etc.), accommodation, etc. (Section 7.5).

7.4. 
FIRE AND EXPLOSION HAZARDS IDENTIFICATION 
Fire and explosion hazards should be identified from review of the following input documents: 
 
 
P&IDs, PFDs unit/site Plot Plans; 
 
3D models; 
 
Process information and conditions from Heat and Material Mass Balance sheets and/or process 
simulation outputs. i.e. process stream composition, pressure, temperature, pressure, mass flow 
rate, liquid hold-up inventories; 
 
Collection and review of relevant safety data sheets; 
 
Emergency detection, shutdown and blowdown system information; and 
 
Hazard Identification Study Report (If available). 
Typical credible fire scenarios shall include:  
 
Full surface fire scenarios on tank in storage areas;  
 
Bund fires scenarios for tank in storage areas;  
 
Jet fires, pool fires and combined jet/pool fires for process areas, subsea releases, etc.;  
 
Vapour cloud explosions (VCEs);  
 
Boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion (BLEVE) for pressurized vessels; and 
 
Boil over scenarios, such as escalation of a full surface tank fire incident might need to be 
included depending the type of tank and location of the storage tank.

7.6.1. VULNERABILITY CRITERIA 
Fire Scenarios:  
Generally, steel equipment will not require protection when exposed to a heat radiation level of 12.5 
kW/m2 or less. At this radiation level or lower the temperature of the steel is expected to be less than 
400OC.  However, as the heat radiation level increases, temperature of unprotected steel equipment or 
structure reaches beyond the critical metal temperature (just above 400OC), Steel structure or equipment 
starts losing its integrity/yield strength when it is reaches the temperature in excess of 400OC.   
 
Rate of rise of steel temperature depends upon many factors such as view factor to flame, thickness of 
steel, material stored within the steel equipment, specific heat capacity, heat protection measures, etc.  
 
When a steel equipment containing process fluid is heated or its temperature is increased due to 
prolonged exposure to heat radiation above 12.5kW/m2, pressure within the equipment rises and yield 
also start decreasing.  Point at which yield strength and pressure increase crosses each other, escalation 
is considered to occur.  37.5kW/m2 or more is considered as Equipment/Component damage criteria.

While studying the pool fire assessment consideration shall be given to following: 
 
 
Most of the empirical models used in industry does not consider obstructions, walls etc. and thus

The obstructions not only changes behaviour of the flame but also changes the rain out locations.  
Empirical models estimate results considering no obstruction and hence rainout location may not

especially while determining vulnerability. This has direct impact on the location of pool centre and 
thus corresponding radiation levels on the equipment;

Based on the size of the release and presence of heavy hydrocarbon the amount of smoke

obstructions, and upon ignition the burn rate is considerable higher than evaporation rates,

shall be considering these rates; 
 
Location of release and pressure within the equipment plays critical role in the amount of inventory

determining inventory available for pool fire and its duration;  


 
Release material must be flammable and at suitable conditions of pressure or temperature;

instantly at release (immediate ignition). A VCE will occur if the flammable gas cloud is allowed to

7.9. 
ESCALATION ASSESSMENT 
7.9.1. BLEVE/FIREBALL 
A BLEVE Is explosion resulting from the failure of a vessel containing a liquid at a temperature 
significantly above its boiling point at normal atmospheric pressure, e.g. pressurized liquefied gases.  The 
fluid in the vessel is usually combination of liquid and vapor.  If the vessel ruptures, vapor is vented and 
the pressure in the liquid drops significantly.  This leads to instantaneous boiling throughout the liquid, 
through this process, a large fraction of the liquid can vaporize in very short time. The liberated energy in 
such case is very high causing high blast pressure and fireball due to immediate ignition from external 
fire.  A common cause failure leading to BLEVE is due to fire engulfment of a vessel containing liquid 
under pressure and hence blast effect and potential fireball is considered as governing scenarios.  
 
BLEVE Propensity:  
The potential for BLEVE depends upon various factors such as type of fluid, amount of liquid within the 
vessel, heat radiation, flame impingement or engulfment scenarios.  Industry grade steel starts losing 
integrity at about 400OC on the vapor side of the vessel.  Therefore, within very short time for scenarios 
such as jet fire impingement or pool fire engulfment these temperatures can be reached.  Typically, it is 
observed that jet fire impingement of more than 5 minutes or pool fire engulfment of more than 10 min 
has potential to reach these temperatures for vessel containing significant amount of hydrocarbon in liquid 
at pressure.  Therefore, all vessels containing liquid under pressure where fire duration and impingement 
and or engulfment can last more than 10 minutes shall be studied for BLEVE potential.  For fluid such as 
propane if duration is more than 5 minutes shall be studied for BLEVE potential.  For vessels where 
amount of liquid is significantly high such as large separators, due to heat absorption, temperature rise 
may take large time even though vessels are impinged or engulf for more than 10 min.  In such cases, 
15 minutes can be considered as threshold for BLEVE phenomenon [Ref. 14].  
 
However, these durations are indicative and can be too optimistic and more advance tools such as finite 
element analysis may be required to determine vessel failure duration and thus duration at which BLEVE 
may occur.  
 
BLEVE Blast Effect:  
The blast effect of a BLEVE results from the rapid flashing of liquid and expansion of vapor in the vessel’s 
head space when the pressure drops suddenly to atmospheric pressure.  The process starts with 
expansion from the initial volume which causes a shock wave travelling faster than the speed of sound.  
The fluids momentum causes the bulk fluid to expand to a pressure below atmospheric pressure resulting 
in a rarefaction wave following the initial shock.  After the interface has reached its maximum diameter, 
the pressure differential causes an inwardly moving wave which reflects at the origin to produce a second 
smaller shock.  The system continues to oscillate, until it come to rest.  The most important blast 
parameters are peak overpressure of the first or main shock and the impulse for the duration of the 
positive pressure of the main shock.  
 
It is recommended to use CCPS second edition model [Ref. 13] which are widely adopted by various 
industry accepted modeling software to determine the BLEVE blast effect.  The BLEVE frequency from a 
given vessel can be determined based on the impairment of the vessel from adjacent fire scenarios.  If 
BLEVE frequency is more than 1E-05/year, BLEVE blast calculations shall be taken forward to the overall 
explosion exceedance frequency calculations of receptors.   
 
BLEVE Fireball:  
Release that fuel fireballs are usually instantaneous releases and commonly involve the catastrophic 
failure of pressurized vessels.  Fireballs could dissipate large amounts of thermal radiation, which away 
from their visible boundaries, may transmit heat energy that could be hazardous to life and property.   
 
TNO & HSE UK provide static model for fireball calculations for immediately ignited flames following 
catastrophic vessel failures or BLEVE which is also widely adopted by various industry accepted modeling 
software.  The model assumes the fireball to exist at its maximum size over its lifespan and ignore 
transient flame characteristics.

7.9.2. BOILOVER 
The boilover phenomenon is defined as a violent ejection of fuel due to the vaporization of a water sub-
layer, resulting in an enormous fire enlargement and formation of boilover heat radiation and ground fire. 
 
A boil-over appears with the burning of oil or flammable fluid viscous enough to enable formation of froth, 
the presence of a water sub-layer, and the apparition of a zone of nearly constant temperature inside the 
fuel. This so-called hot-zone (wave of heat) has an expansion rate higher than the burning rate of the 
hydrocarbon free surface and therefore enhances the heat propagation downwards the water layer, 
decreases the time of boil-over apparition, and increases the intensity of consequences which mainly 
consist in flame enlargement and radiation increase. 
 
Radiation from boil-over are very intense and expands to larger area and can be a serious threat to 
adjacent facilities and firefighters.  Therefore, it is essential to determine the potential boilover scenarios 
and study its impact.   
 
As part of FERA study therefore detailed boilover assessment shall be carried out based on following 
steps.  
 
1. 
Determine propensity of boilover; 
2. 
Determine time to boilover initiations; 
3. 
Determine hydrocarbon mass in tanks when the boilover initiates; 
4. 
Determine velocity of the propelled fluid; 
5. 
Determine hydrocarbon mass generating the boilover; and 
6. 
Determine boilover heat radiation & effect distances.  
Detail calculations for these steps are available in UFIP modeling guidelines [Ref.14] and shall be used 
while carrying out boilover calculations.  
 
7.10. DUST EXPLOSION ASSESSMENT 
A dust explosion is the rapid combustion of fine particles suspended in the air within an enclosed location. 
Dust explosions can occur where any dispersed powdered combustible or flammable material is present 
in high-enough concentrations in the atmosphere or other oxidizing gaseous medium, such as oxygen.  
 
Following conditions necessary conditions for a dust explosion.  
 
 
Dust is combustible or flammable and with particle size to allow combustion; 
 
The dust is suspended in the air at a sufficiently high concentration (flammable range); 
 
There is an oxidant (typically atmospheric oxygen); 
 
There is an ignition source; and 
 
The area is confined—a building can be considered an enclosure. 
Operations within ADNOC Group where dust explosion is possible is sulphur dust/sulphur granulation 
plant/sulphur handling, cement dust within the enclosed plant, etc.

When an explosion occurs in a confined environment, it will lead to an expansion of the gas due to 
combustion and heating and therefore to an increase of temperature. Depending on the dust material, 
the maximum pressure changes. Pmax characterises the max explosion pressure for a given powder. The 
maximum pressure is usually in the range 4 to10 bars but it should be defined for every material.  Various 
literatures are available which provides the Pmax, typical value for sulphur dust are in the range of 6.8bar 
[Ref. 16].   
 
The pressure increase to Pmax can develop more or less quickly depending on the material. The pressure 
variation can be described by an explosion constant Kst The higher Kst, the quicker the explosion will 
develop and pressure increase. Kst is necessary to size explosion mitigation equipment like explosion 
panels so that they can act quick enough before the pressure approaches Pmax. 
 
A qualitative approach is adopted to assess the dust explosion hazard.  The steps which are included in 
the assessment are:  
 
Collection of physical data on the dust under consideration:  To conduct a good analysis it is 
necessary to know expected Pmax, Kst, and Minimum Ignition Energy (MIE) of the dust under evaluations.  
These information helps in design of the explosion mitigation systems and ignition control mechanism.  
 
Determine the potential for the dust explosion:  Based on the dust particle size and concentration 
determine the potential for the dust explosion.   
 
Determine frequency of dust presence: Assess the frequency at which a powder cloud can be formed, 
define the area classification.  
 
Determine the ignition sources: Identify potential ignition sources and assess the frequency of having 
an ignition source during presence of dust.  
 
Determine severity of dust explosion:  Based on the dust physical data, particle size, concentration of 
dust, presence of oxygen, potential turbulence, etc. determine the Pmax and Kst.   
 
Dust Explosion Assessment: Based on the severity of dust explosion and likelihood determine the dust 
explosion risk.  Assess measures to eliminate various ignition sources, use of inert gases, explosion 
mitigation measures such as venting and suppression systems, etc. Guidelines for dust explosion 
prevention and mitigation measures are available in standards such as NFPA 654 Standard for the 
Prevention of Fire and Dust Explosions from the Manufacturing, Processing, and Handling of Combustible 
Particulate Solids and NFPA 655 Standard for Prevention of Sulfur Fires and Explosions.   
 
7.11. FIRE ZONE ASSESSMENT 
Fire zones are areas of the plant sub-divided based on the potential for fire & explosion hazard, as 
assessed by the consequence & risk modeling and the efficiency of response means and protective 
equipment. Fire zones are areas within the installation where equipment are grouped by nature and/or 
by similar level of risk attached to them.  Fire zone assists in managing the separation distances between 
independent plant, emergency shutdown, flare design and defining firewater requirements.   
 
The partition into Fire zones is such that the consequence of fire, a flammable gas leak or an explosion 
corresponding to the worst credible event likely to occur in the concerned fire zone shall not impact other 
fire zones to an extent where their integrity could be put at risk.  The partition into fire zone is intended to 
limit the consequence of credible events but is not intended to avoid the occurrence of the credible events.  
A fire zone may be physically separated from other areas by space, fire barriers, diking, special drainage 
(e.g. impoundment basis) or by a combination of these so that fire is expected to be contained within that 
area. The escalation of the event is restricted to the affected fire zone and a minor impact on adjacent 
fire zones only.  
 
Criteria for fire zones is defined in ADNOC HSE Design Philosophy Standard, HSE-GA-ST07 [Ref. 15] 
and the fire zones shall be designed in accordance to the definitions given.

7.13. FERA WORKSHOP 
FERA workshop shall be conducted once the initial assessment and recommendations are identified.  As 
part of FERA workshop, initial assessment including consequence and risk results and contours shall be 
presented, discussed and brainstormed to capture asset/location and receptor specific issues against 
MAH. Operation issues considering practical approach shall be taken into account as part of the 
brainstorming session.  
 
Based on the assessment the recommendations to be refined/made specific or any additional 
recommendations shall be brainstormed and captured.  It shall be noted recommendations are in addition 
to regulatory or code requirements and shall not negate the regulatory or code or standards requirements.  
 
It is recommended to follow ALARP principles (elimination, prevention, mitigation, procedural, 
administrative, etc.), while discussing the additional recommendations in the workshop. The FERA 
workshop should be attended by a multi-discipline team and as a minimum should be participated by 
team conducting the EERA, project representatives, operation representatives and HSE team 
members/SMEs. 
 
Post FERA workshops, all applicable recommendations (excluding regulatory, standard or code 
requirements) shall be subjected to Cost Benefit Assessment based on Maximum Justifiable Spend 
(MJS). Assessment shall take into account all aspects including asset cost, production losses, fuel loss, 
environmental clean-up cost, implied cost of averting a fatality, etc. considering the design life of the 
facilities/plant.  
 
The FERA Workshop worksheets/outcome shall be documented in the FERA Report, inclusive of the 
justification for the selection and rejection of all identified potential risk reduction measures and 
requirement of further studies. 
 
7.14. ACTION PLAN 
All the identified risk reduction measures/recommendation shall be documented in FERA.  All

documented as part of action plan.  
 
For project phases, all the engineering recommendations and recommendations associated with EER or 
provision of EER measures, which are identified in the FEED & EPC stage, shall be documented in
