1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) Policy, 
is committed to provide a safe and healthy workplace for employees. This includes ensuring that a safe 
work environment is maintained in all the offices across the organization. 
 
Offices are considered relatively safer to work-in than other sites (i.e. workshops, construction, production 
facilities etc.). There are however potential hazards and risks associated with working in office facilities. 
Maintaining a safe and healthy office environment requires attention to work station design, indoor 
environment (such as temperature, humidity, light, noise and ventilation), space, task design, 
psychological factors (personal interactions, work pace, job control), physical hazards and sometimes, 
chemical or other environmental exposures. 
 
Situations in offices that can lead to injury or illness range from physical hazards (such as cords across 
walkways, leaving low drawers open, objects falling from overhead) to task-related (speed or repetition, 
duration, job control, etc.), environmental (chemical or biological sources) or design-related hazards (such 
as nonadjustable furniture or equipment), Job stress that results when the requirements of the job do not 
match the capabilities or resources of the worker may also result in illness. Hence it is essential that 
employees familiarize themselves with this part and implement the requirements in their respective work 
areas. 
 
Requirements set out in this Standard shall be strictly complied with to raise awareness and improve 
focus on the office safety topic, often relegated to a lower rank in priorities despite recognized hazards. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
This Standard outlines requirements for eliminating, preventing and controlling hazards generally 
associated within Offices across ADNOC Group. 
 
This standard aims on identifying and implementing appropriate control measures to manage the risks, 
threats, hazards and impacts associated within office. 
 
The purpose of this Standard is to: 
 
(i) 
Describe the common hazards and associated risks in the office work environment; 
(ii) 
Increase safety awareness amongst employees working in office locations. 
 
This Standard describe the minimum requirements for managing office safety to protect ADNOC Group 
employees and contractors from adverse effects by establishing barriers and controls. 
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors. 
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7. 
REQUIREMENTS 
Offices vary from multi-storeyed, air-conditioned buildings to small areas such as those offices located in 
remote sites of onshore and offshore locations. While hazards in the office cannot always be as obvious 
as those in refineries, drilling rigs and offshore platforms, office personnel can also be exposed to a range 
of HSE hazards, including but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Ergonomic hazards such as those due to design of workstations; 
(ii) 
Electrical hazards; 
(iii) 
Chemical hazards such as those from cleaning agents; 
(iv) 
Fire Hazards; 
(v) 
Indoor Environment; 
(vi) 
Biological hazards such as those in pantries and rest rooms; 
(vii) 
Psychosocial hazards such as stress, fatigue;  
(viii) Hazards involving Manual handling; 
(ix) 
Hazards involving Working at Heights; and  
(x) 
Slips, trips and falls;  
The following sections elaborate the above mentioned hazards along with requirements and guidelines 
on managing the risks that arise from them. 
 
HAZARD IDENTIFICATION  
 
Hazard identification is the process of identifying situations or events that could give rise to injury or illness. 
Workplace inspections, audits, walk throws, incidents and near-miss reports are the important tools for 
workplace hazards identification. More specifically, the consideration of type of injury or illness possible 
(for example musculoskeletal disorders (MSD)) and the situations and events that could create potential 
for the injury or illness (for example, prolonged bending over a low desk during a collating task) helps in 
identifying risk and the associated controls. All personnel shall be informed about the workplace hazards 
through appropriate communication. For further details refer to ADNOC Risk Management Standard [Ref. 
28] and ADNOC Hazard Communication Standard. [Ref. 18] 
 
The type of hazards that can be present in an office includes but not limited to: 
 
(i) 
Ergonomics hazards such as poor workstation setup in an office, poor posture and manual 
handling leading to musculoskeletal injuries  
(ii) 
Fire hazards such as fire from damaged power cords, fire from combustible materials etc.; 
(iii) 
Electrical hazards such as damaged electrical cords or overloaded power points that can 
lead to the risk of electric shock; 
(iv) 
Chemical hazards, such as vapours in the atmosphere from paint, solvents and bleaches or 
airborne particles like photocopier toner; 
(v) 
Mechanical hazards, such as filing cabinets that tend to tip over when heavily laden top 
drawers are opened; tripping hazards; 
(vi) 
Physical hazards, such as glare or reflections from screens; hot components of photocopiers; 
poorly designed chairs that do not provide the user with adequate back support; poorly 
designed jobs and tasks that demand prolonged work in a fixed posture; 
(vii) 
Psychosocial hazards, such as the need to perform excessive workloads under pressure, 
lack of satisfaction from a job where there is inadequate recognition of work performed or 
repetitive work and insufficient task variety; and 
(viii) Environmental hazards such as indoor environment, wastes, usage of resources, etc.

7.1. 
ERGONOMICS  
Ergonomics focuses on how we position our body and interact with the environment when we are working. 
In an office setting, ergonomics means finding a way to be comfortable while using a computer and 
telephone and performing tasks like copying, printing and faxing. 
 
Poor ergonomics may affect a personnel’s musculoskeletal system. Initial symptoms that may be 
experienced by the personnel include fatigue, discomfort, and pain in the muscles and tendons. These 
symptoms may progress to more serious injuries or illnesses such as carpal tunnel syndrome, tendonitis, 
thoracic outlet syndrome and tension neck syndrome. 
 
To protect against the risk identified concerning to injuries resulting from ergonomics, all offices shall 
ensure the ergonomic requirements are followed for office equipment as listed below but not limited to: 
 
(i) 
Workstations, table and chairs; 
(ii) 
Computers and Visual Display Units; 
(iii) 
Files cabinets, storage and filing cabinets; 
(iv) 
Walkaways, aisles and stairs; 
(v) 
Electrical appliance, cables, cords and lighting; 
(vi) 
Housekeeping; 
(vii) 
Placement of fire protection equipment; and 
(viii) Walkways and stairs;  
For further details, refer to the ADNOC Ergonomics Hazards Standard [Ref. 16]. 
 
7.2. 
CHEMICAL SAFETY  
Some of the substances used in the offices can be hazardous, however, these generally pose little risk 
under normal circumstances and conditions of use within the office environment. Examples of such 
substances include cleaning fluids, liquid paper, glues, inks, solvents and cleaning agents. 
 
After doing a survey of materials being used in the office and obtaining SDSs from the suppliers, copies 
of these shall be assembled at one or more accessible points as a register. For example, they can be 
kept in a ring binder in the photocopier room. 
 
An assessment of exposure shall be conducted for each hazardous substance used in the office. 
 
Chemical hazards related to the usage of office copying machines are provided under Section 0. 
 
For further details, refer to the ADNOC Chemical Hazards Standard. [Ref. 24] 
 
7.3. 
ELECTRICAL SAFETY  
Electricity has the potential to cause significant damage to assets and severely injure or cause fatalities. 
The main hazards associated with electricity to office personnel are: 
 
(i) 
Electric shock and burns from contact with live parts; 
(ii) 
Injury from exposure to arcing, fire from faulty electrical equipment or overloading of power 
sources; and 
(iii) 
Explosion caused by defective electrical appliances. 
Electric shocks can also lead to other types of injury, for example causing a fall from ladders.

(i) 
Live flames;

Sparks from electrical equipment or wiring;

Limit the use of extension cords to only those instances when fixed wiring is not available;

Install smoke detection equipment throughout the office.

7.5. 
INDOOR ENVIRONMENT 
7.5.1. NOISE MANAGEMENT AND HEARING CONVERSATION  
Noise within the office can originate from internal and external sources. Internal noise sources includes 
office equipment (e.g. telephones, printers and photocopiers), people (e.g. conversations) and 
background noise generated by the building (e.g. from lifts and air conditioning). Background noise 
generally goes unnoticed unless there is a malfunction of equipment. In fact, some background noise is 
desirable as an absolutely quiet environment can be uncomfortable. External noise sources can include 
road traffic and general industrial noise. 
 
Generally, the levels of noise in office areas are below those levels known to pose a risk to hearing. In 
offices, ‘annoyance’ noise is more likely which can interfere with communication, annoy or distract people 
and affect a person’s performance of tasks like reading and writing. 
 
Noise that prevents a person from understanding an instruction or warning signal can also be a risk to 
safety. For these reasons, it is important to consider what can be done to control unwanted noise in the 
office. 
 
To identify disturbing noise sources in an office it is best to ask the people working in the area a series of 
questions. For example: 
What noise is most disturbing (if any)? 
(i) 
When does it occur? 
(ii) 
What effect does it have? 
(iii) 
How do you deal with disturbing noise? 
This can be done using a general walk-through survey that includes interviewing people.  Where noise 
issues have been identified as a problem in an office environment, assessment and development of noise 
control measures shall be undertaken. A qualified person shall be consulted where specialist assessment 
or advice is required. [Ref. 3] 
 
In order to control noise in an office environment, ADNOC Group shall consider the following steps [Ref. 
3]:  
 
(i) 
Use a layout which separates noise generating activities or equipment from tasks requiring 
concentration; 
(ii) 
Isolate noisy equipment such as printers or photocopiers by placing them in separate rooms; 
(iii) 
Use sound-absorbent materials, including suitable floor coverings, wall panels, ceiling 
panels and dividing screens. Installation of barriers shall also take into account the effect 
this can have on ventilation and any sense of isolation it can cause with staff; 
(iv) 
Provide acoustic-grade dividing screens to reduce conversation noise.  
(v) 
Select equipment with the lowest noise specifications practicable; 
(vi) 
Install noise barriers – including double-glazed windows, solid walls and fences – to reduce 
external noise sources; 
(vii) 
Lower the volume setting on a disruptive telephone. This is a simple way to reduce existing 
noise levels; 
(viii) Adopt administrative controls such as encouraging employees to use meeting areas away 
from work areas for conversations; and

7.5.2. LIGHTING IN OFFICES  
Sufficient lighting shall be provided, whether it is from a natural or artificial source, to allow safe movement 
around the offices and to allow personnel to perform their job without having to adopt awkward postures 
or strain their eyes to see. 
 
The following factors shall be taken into account: 
 
(i) 
The nature of the work activity; 
(ii) 
The nature of hazards and risks in the workplace; 
(iii) 
The work environment; 
(iv) 
Illumination levels, including both natural and artificial light; 
(v) 
The transition of natural light over the day; 
(vi) 
Glare; 
(vii) 
Contrast; and 
(viii) Reflections. 
Different lighting levels are needed for different times of the day. Too much lighting can result in glare. 
The following shall be considered to prevent low or excessive levels of lighting, glare or reflection: 
 
(i) 
Providing additional lighting, such as a lamp on a movable arm; 
(ii) 
Changing the position of existing lights; 
(iii) 
Changing the location of the workstation; 
(iv) 
Increasing or decreasing the number of lights; 
(v) 
Changing the type of lighting used e.g. from white light to blue light; 
(vi) 
Changing the diffusers or reflectors on existing lights; and 
(vii) 
Using screens, visors, shields, hoods, curtains, blinds or external louvers to reduce 
reflections, shadows and glare.

(ii) 
Reflections from a work surface - Light reflected from a surface can make it difficult to see 
what is on it. For example, it can be difficult to read a screen when light from artificial lighting 
or windows is reflected from it. To identify reflections, observe a work surface or screen and 
ask the operator if they have difficulty seeing their work due to reflections. 
Reflections from screens have been reduced by the development of colour monitors, 
Windows-based systems, LCD screens and non-reflective screen surfaces. Additional 
controls for reflections include positioning the screen side-on to the main light source. A light 
screen background also reduces difficulties caused by reflections. If these options do not 
resolve the problem, then consider moving the workstation to another position. This is 
particularly relevant where the screen is used for prolonged periods of time. These controls 
shall be used in preference to the use of screen filters, which can reduce the quality of the 
screen display and require regular cleaning. 
 
Annoying reflections can also occur in workplaces where there are highly polished floors or 
glass covered wall paintings. These issues shall be addressed when planning and setting 
up an office. Even glossy paper documents can reflect light and become unreadable 
 
(iii) 
Shadows across a work surface - Shadows can reduce the visibility of work, contribute to 
glare problems and cause the adoption of poor posture in order to view work. Increasing the 
number and spread of overhead lighting, repositioning work or redirecting lighting are the 
main ways of reducing shadows. Barriers to light falling on the work surface – for example, 
an overhead shelf – shall be removed or relocated to reduce shadows. An adjustable task 
lamp may provide specific lighting where shadows are a problem, where light from a 
particular direction is required or when an increase in general lighting is not practicable. A 
task lamp can, however, create pools of light, causing the eyes to have to adapt rapidly when 
looking at the whole work surface, so the removal of barriers to light falling on the work 
surface is the preferred control measure. 
(iv) 
Natural Light - When identifying, assessing or controlling lighting issues in offices, it is 
required to take into account the time of day and year, as this shall affect the quantity and 
quality of natural light in a work area. This is particularly important when designing lighting 
systems. Some of the office lighting issues can be caused by natural light entering a work 
area. By providing staff with control and adjustment of natural light, for example, venetian or 
vertical blinds, many of these issues can be addressed. 
(v) 
Colour - Choice of colours can determine the mood of an environment and the level of 
reflection from a surface. It is recommended that ceilings have high reflectance, (reflecting 
around 80% of the light) and are usually white or off white. Walls shall have 50 – 75% 
reflectance (subdued cool colours) and a gloss or semi-gloss finish. Floors shall have low 
(less than 20%) reflectance and therefore shall be darker and not glossy. The use of colourful 
posters or non-reflective paintings can relieve monotony and provide visual relief. 
(vi) 
Flickering lights - Some lights can be a source of annoyance, particularly older fluorescent 
tubes which may flicker when malfunctioning. Regular maintenance shall be done in order 
control the effects of light flicker.

7.5.3. INDOOR AIR QUALITY MONITORING  
Indoor air quality refers to the air quality within buildings and structures. It is directly related to the health 
and comfort of building occupants and is essential for the wellbeing and health of all personnel. Health 
effects may appear even after a single exposure to pollutants in the air, such as irritation of the eyes, 
nose, and throat, headaches, dizziness, and fatigue. Long-term exposure may result in more serious 
illnesses such as respiratory diseases, heart disease and cancer. 
 
For this reason, workplaces shall be adequately ventilated. Fresh, clean air shall be drawn from outside 
the workplace, uncontaminated by discharge from outlets through adequate filtration, and be circulated 
through the workplace. 
 
Workplace inside buildings shall have an air-conditioning system. An air-conditioning system shall: 
 
(i) 
Provide a comfortable environment in relation to air temperature, humidity and air movement; 
(ii) 
Prevent the excessive accumulation of odours; 
(iii) 
Reduce the levels of respiratory by-products, especially carbon dioxide, and other indoor 
contaminants that can arise from work activities; and 
(iv) 
Supply an amount of fresh air to the workplace, exhaust some of the stale air as well as filter 
and recirculate some of the indoor air. 
Enclosed workplaces shall be supplied with comfortable rates of air movement (usually between 0.1 m 
and 0.2 m per second). [Ref. 4] 
 
Air-conditioning system shall be regularly serviced and maintained in accordance with manufacturer’s 
instructions. Cooling towers that form part of many air-conditioning systems can be a favourable 
environment for Legionella bacteria if they are not properly designed and maintained. Exposure to these 
bacteria can cause the potentially fatal Legionnaire’s disease.  
 
Smoking and usage of bakhoor is prohibited inside the premises of ADNOC office facilities to protect the 
indoor air quality and to contribute to the health and wellbeing of all employees. This includes common 
work areas, conference and meeting rooms, private offices, hallways, restaurants, kitchens, stairs, 
restrooms and all other enclosed facilities. 
 
Smoking includes use of electronic cigarettes (E-cigarettes) and tobacco products such as chewing 
tobacco and is only allowed at designated smoking areas located outside the office premises. 
 
For further details on indoor air quality in the ADNOC Group offices, refer to the ADNOC Indoor Air Quality 
Standard [Ref. 26].

7.6.1. HEALTH HAZARDS 
(a) 
Ozone 
Photocopiers produce small amounts of ozone (an unstable form of oxygen). Ozone is also 
produced naturally in the upper atmosphere by ultraviolet solar radiation. If present in sufficient 
concentration, this odorous gas is irritating to the eyes, the lungs, throat and nasal passages. 
Ozone is normally detected by smell at concentrations of 0.01 to 0.02 parts per million (ppm). 
Symptoms can occur at levels of approximately 0.25 ppm or above.

The concentration of ozone within the breathing zone of the operator is dependent on the amount 
of ozone discharged from the copier, the rate of decay of the ozone, the volume of air in the room, 
the concentration of ozone in any intake air and the ventilation provided in the room. 
 
The majority of the ozone is produced during the charging and discharging of the drum and paper. 
Machines which utilise a negative DC process, with the electrostatic drum carrying a negative 
charge, produce the greatest amount of ozone. Some ozone is produced by ultraviolet emission 
from the photocopier lamp. 
 
Under normal circumstances, the concentration of ozone around photocopiers is not sufficient to 
cause symptoms. Ozone is readily decomposed back to oxygen. The rate of decomposition is 
dependent on time, temperature (the gas breaks down much more rapidly at a high temperature) 
and contact with various surfaces. Activated carbon will provide 100 per cent decomposition given 
sufficient contact with ozone. Many photocopiers have activated carbon filters fitted to decompose 
ozone. 
 
(b) 
Selenium and Cadmium 
The photoconductive material in photocopiers is usually selenium. Cadmium sulphide, zinc oxide 
and organic polymers are also used. Trace amounts of these materials can become airborne. 
However, under normal operating conditions the resultant concentrations of these pollutants are 
well below those concentrations associated with health effects. 
 
(c) 
Toner materials 
Toners contain carbon black. Pure carbon black has not been associated with adverse health 
effects. As currently manufactured, carbon blacks contain extremely low levels of impurities and 
do not warrant concern regarding health effects. 
 
Toner dust can irritate the respiratory tract, resulting in coughing and sneezing. The fine toner 
powder used in dry photocopying can be released from copiers, particularly if they lack 
containerized toner systems and automatic shut-down devices on waste toner compartments. 
 
(d) 
Liquid Toners 
Some modern toners are in liquid form and so rarely irritate the skin, but exposure to solvents 
within them can dry and crack the skin and mildly irritates the eyes. The same hazards apply to the 
various solvents used for cleaning duplicating machines. They also pose a fire risk if not stored 
adequately. Frequent contact with the toner or other solvents can cause dermatitis or asthma. 
 
(e) 
UV Radiation 
Ultraviolet radiation can also be released through the glass plate, but at a very low level. Modern 
equipment does not present a bright light hazard beyond short term discomfort to the eyes, but it 
is recommended that the photocopier lid be kept down. 
 
(f) 
Laser Printers 
Airborne particles within an office environment are a combination of particles generated from 
various sources, included are laser printers which emits paper fibres, organic vapours and 
inorganic gases. Laser printers emits nanoparticles of substances, which could increase the effects 
of exposure. 
 
(g) 
Physical Factors 
Possible discomfort from the light, heat and noise generated by office copying machines should be 
considered. 
 
Fluorescent, metal halide or quartz exposure lamps are most commonly used in photocopiers. Eye 
damage has not been shown to be caused by photocopier operation.

7.7. 
MANUAL HANDLING  
Manual handling refers to any activity requiring the use of force exerted by a person to lift, push, pull, 
carry or otherwise move something. Manual handling occurs in almost all working environments, including 
work in the office. 
 
Examples of manual handling tasks commonly performed in offices include lifting and carrying boxes of 
photocopying paper; moving office furniture and equipment such as computers and printers; handling 
large files, books and legal documents; prolonged data entry; and opening and closing filing cabinet 
drawers. 
 
Manual handling of loads may cause disorders due to gradual and cumulative deterioration of the 
musculoskeletal system through continuous lifting/handling activities, e.g. low back pain. It can also cause 
acute trauma such as cuts or fractures due to accidents. Work-related low back pain and injuries are the 
most common musculoskeletal disorders caused by manual handling. 
 
Factors that increase the risk of injury include: 
(i) 
The load being too heavy, large, difficult to grasp or unstable;  
(ii) 
The task being too strenuous or involving awkward postures or movements; 
(iii) 
The working environment lacking sufficient space;  
(iv) 
Slippery, uneven or unstable floors; 
(v) 
Extreme temperatures; and 
(vi) 
Poor lighting. 
Personnel involved in any manual handling activity which poses a risk of injury shall ensure that it is 
avoided wherever possible, and if they prove to be unavoidable then the risk shall be properly managed. 
 
Injury to manual handling shall be avoided by considering the following preventive measures: 
 
(i) 
Designing and organizing tasks to avoid manual handling completely, or at least minimizing 
it; 
(ii) 
Using automation and lifting equipment; 
(iii) 
Organizing manual handling tasks in a safe way, with loads split into smaller ones, and 
proper rest periods provided; and 
(iv) 
Providing information and training to personnel on tasks and the use of equipment and 
correct handling techniques and 
(v) 
Using trolleys for movement of larger items. 
For further details on manual handling, refer to ADNOC Manual Handling Standard. [Ref.17]

7.8. 
SLIPS, TRIPS AND FALLS  
Slips, trips and falls are a common source of injuries in all working environments, including the office. 
Slips occur when there is a loss of grip between the shoe and the floor. This commonly occurs when there 
is a contaminant between the shoe and the floor such as when someone walks on a newly mopped floor 
that is wet.  
 
Trips occur when a person’s foot hits a low obstacle in the person’s path, causing a loss of balance. Trips 
often results from obstructions and uneven surfaces and can usually be avoided by ensuring that floor 
surfaces are clear and even. However, sometimes the obstacle may not be easily visible or noticed. 
 
Falls can be a result from slips and trips but may also be falls from a different height. Falls are likely if 
chairs ,shelves or ladders are used as steps to reach upper storage levels. Falls can also occur on poorly 
designed or badly lit stairwells or worn stair edges. 
 
Slips, trips and falls in the office can result in: 
 
(i) 
Sprains or strains; 
(ii) 
Broken bones when trying to break the fall; 
(iii) 
A back injury due to the sudden and forceful impact during a fall; 
(iv) 
Burns if it occurs near hot surfaces or if the person is handling hot fluids; and 
(v) 
Cuts if it occurs near sharp objects. 
The following factors can contribute to the risk of slips and trips. It is usually a combination of these factors 
that create the risk of a slip or trip. 
 
7.8.1. CONTAMINANTS 
Contaminants are considered as anything that ends up on a floor that results in a slip. Contaminants can 
be in liquid form such as water, oil or grease, or solids such as dust, plastic bags or off-cuts. Eliminating 
floor contaminants can greatly reduce the risk of slips. This can be done by cleaning the floors properly 
and regularly. Prompt attention to spills is also important in order to prevent slips. 
 
7.8.2. FLOOR SURFACE 
Floor surfaces require sufficient grip to prevent slipping, especially in areas which are prone to 
contaminants. The greater the thickness or viscosity of the contaminants, the greater the slip resistance 
of the flooring required to protect against slipping.  
 
Floor surfaces shall be cleaned regularly to ensure that the floor does not become slippery. In addition, 
anti-slip mats may be used to increase grip on the floor. 
 
7.8.3. OBSTACLES AND OTHER TRIP HAZARDS 
Trips most often occur because of uneven flooring or cluttered walkways with low obstacles which are 
not easily visible or noticed. Common examples of low obstacles include trailing cables, uneven edges 
to flooring, gratings or covers, loose mats or carpet tiles and changes of floor surface level. 
In order to prevent the trips, the following measures shall be taken: 
(i) 
Good housekeeping practices; 
(ii) 
Ensuring the floor surface is in good order such as being free from holes, uneven surfaces, 
curled up linoleum or carpet edges;

7.10. WORK AT HEIGHT  
Working at height introduces increased risk of high severity injuries and death. Common cases include 
falls from ladders and through fragile surfaces (e.g. glass). Working at height shall be avoided if possible. 
However, if not possible, the following requirements shall be followed.  
 
Ladders shall only be used if no other safer equipment (such as Podium Steps) can be used. Ladders 
can be used for low-risk, short duration activities that do not require higher level fall protection inside the 
offices. As a guide ladders and step ladders shall be used for no more than 30 minutes. [Ref. 7] 
 
Pre-use check of the ladders shall be carried out by the user at the beginning of the work and after 
something has changed e.g. if the ladder has been dropped or damaged, moved from a dirty to a clean 
area etc. 
 
The following are items to be checked: 
 
(i) 
The Stiles – ensure they are not bent or damaged, as the ladder could buckle or collapse; 
(ii) 
The Feet – if they are missing, worn or damaged the ladder could slip. Also check the ladder 
feet if moving from soft/dirty ground to smooth, solid surfaces to make sure that there is 
nothing embedded to prevent the feet from making contact with the ground; 
(iii)

7.13. WORKPLACE STRESS  
Work is generally beneficial to mental health and personal wellbeing. There are circumstances, however, 
in which work can have adverse consequences for health and wellbeing. Risks to psychosocial health at 
work can arise from organisational or personal factors, with the major factors being poor design of work 
and jobs, poor communication and interpersonal relationships, bullying, occupational violence and fatigue. 
 
Risks to psychosocial health due to work shall be viewed in the same way as other health and safety 
risks, and a commitment to prevention of work-related stress shall be included in an organisation’s health 
and safety policies. 
 
Stress is a word used loosely in the community and is a complex emotional experience. Positive 
challenges motivate people psychosocially and physically to learn new skills and master their jobs. This 
‘good’ stress can lead to high levels of satisfaction and productivity. However, challenges can also be 
personally distressing and disruptive. Work that results in a person feeling unable to cope and 
unsupported can lead to an adverse psychosocial reaction known as stress and can lead to illness, injury 
and job failure. However, stress is not just feeling sad, upset or angry. It is interactive and people can 
have very different responses to the same factors. We do know that some workplace factors can 
contribute to stress reactions. 
 
These can arise from content of work, for example: 
 
(i) 
Work, which is monotonous or lacks variety, or too much or too little work to do; 
(ii) 
Work that involves employees having to hide their feelings when dealing with customers, or 
performing work that goes against personal or social standards; 
(iii) 
Unpredictable, long or unsocial working hours, poorly designed shift systems; 
(iv) 
Employees being unable to participate in decisions about their work or control how they do 
it; and 
(v) 
Environmental conditions such as poor lighting, excessive noise, heat and complex or 
inadequate equipment or technology. 
Stress can also arise from the social and organisational context to work, for example: 
(i) 
Inadequate communication systems with supervisors or co-workers; 
(ii) 
Uncertainty, e.g. no clear instructions on what employees are expected to do; 
(iii) 
Job change, e.g. in task content, hours, location, supervision, without adequate consultation; 
(iv) 
Recruitment policies which fail to ensure employees have suitable skills for the work and do 
not adequately train staff; 
(v) 
Under/over promotion or job insecurity; 
(vi) 
Work involving reward systems (pay, recognition or promotion) leading to employees 
working long hours, taking work home or pushing themselves to a point where their health 
and safety is at risk; 
(vii) 
Poor relationships between supervisors, peers or others at work, not acknowledged and 
rectified; 
(viii) Emotionally or physically demanding jobs with inadequate support; 
(ix) 
Inconsistent people management or not in line with workplace policies, e.g. discrimination 
related to performance appraisal; and 
(x) 
Conflicting demands of home, family and work.

7.18. WASTE MANAGEMENT  
Waste management is the collection, transportation, disposal or recycling and monitoring of waste. In the 
office environment, waste can be in the form of solid or liquid.  
 
The collection, disposal and recycling of waste paper shall be planned and maintained to minimize 
disruption and hazards in the office. The location and use of paper shredding machines shall take into 
account the noise they generate and the mess from spillage when they are emptied. The placement of 
paper into a shredder can be hazardous if items of clothing such as ties become trapped. Shredders with 
an angled entry chute is recommended to be used. 
In order to allow for waste paper to be recycled, the following steps shall be taken: 
 
(i) 
Separate the recyclable paper waste from other wastes; 
(ii) 
Ensure recycle bins or collection facilities are provided and are properly maintained; and 
(iii) 
Create awareness on the recycling initiative that is in place. 
The principles of waste management are based on a hierarchy of management practices which are 
fundamental to the development of a sustainable waste management strategy. Effective management of 
waste begins with pollution prevention. This means the elimination, change or reduction of operating 
practices which result in discharges to land, air or water. If elimination of waste is not possible, then waste 
should be minimized through the application of the hierarchical principle of source reduction, reuse, 
recycling/recovery, treatment and responsible disposal.
