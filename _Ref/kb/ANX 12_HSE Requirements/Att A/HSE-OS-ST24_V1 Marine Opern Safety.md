1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy, 
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all personnel who 
are associated with marine operations, are fully aware of all the hazards involved and have the 
competence, training and awareness necessary to undertake such operations. 
 
In compliance with Safe of Life at Sea (SOLAS) regulations Chapter V, this Standard sets out minimum 
requirements that will ensure all marine vessels shall be sufficiently and efficiently manned from a safety 
point of view. The SOLAS regulations has exemptions based on vessel classification and tonnage, such 
as ships not propelled by mechanical means.  
 
The mandatory requirements to ensure safe marine operations and meet the relevant ADNOC HSE 
Management System (HSEMS) Manual [Ref. 24] expectations, shall cover all the activities that involve 
every type of marine vessels operating for or on behalf of ADNOC Group Companies and Affiliates.  
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this standard is to provide an overall understanding of hazards associated with marine 
transport and operations within the ADNOC Group. This standard establishes a consistent safety 
guideline to the personnel involved in the activities within the ADNOC Group. 
 
This Standard applies to all ADNOC Group operations and activities where the primary purpose is to 
establish standards for assessment and control of risks covering all marine operations including but not 
limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Offshore Logistics 
(ii) 
Acceptance Criteria for Vessel; 
(iii) 
Field Restrictions; 
(iv) 
Navigation, Mooring, and Anchoring; 
(v) 
Safety of Port Operations; 
(vi) 
Crew Certification and Competence; 
(vii) 
Emergency Response; 
(viii) Cargo handling; and 
(ix) 
Personnel Transfer. 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors. 
 
This Standard shall apply, as applicable, to the following: 
 
(i) 
All vessels and offshore marine units operating directly or indirectly by ADNOC Group of 
Companies; 
(ii) 
Personnel involved in or responsible for marine activities on behalf of ADNOC Group of 
Companies; and 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7. 
REQUIREMENTS 
7.1. 
OVERVIEW 
All requirements set out in this Standard are aligned with IMO regulations and related goal-based 
standards framework shown in below Figure 7.1.1. 
 
The objectives of the mandatory application of the ISM Code (as applicable) being to ensure: 
 
(i) 
Compliance with mandatory rules and regulations related to the safe operation of vessels 
and protection of the environment; and 
(ii) 
The effective implementation and enforcement thereof by GC’s, Affiliates and marine 
contractors working for ADNOC Group. 
Note: Compliance with ISM Code is not mandatory for non-self-propelled marine units such as barge 
drilling units. 
 
All accountable and responsible parties shall accordingly strive and ensure that the appropriate 
organizational structures needed for effectively managing marine operations, ashore and on board 
vessels, is in place to ensure the highest standards of HSE are maintained. 
 
All personnel involved in marine operations shall be made familiar with the requirements of this Standard 
to the level of detail required by their assigned roles and positions. 
 
Effective enforcement shall include verification that the GC’s, Affiliates and marine contractors’ HSEMS 
fully complies with the requirements stipulated in the ISM Code, as well as verification of compliance with 
all IMO mandatory rules and regulations. 
 
Sustained efforts will contribute to enhance and improve the HSE culture across the organization. With

7.3.2. VOYAGE PLAN 
(a) 
Objectives 
The development of a plan for voyage or passage, as well as the close and continuous monitoring 
of the vessel's progress and position during the execution of such a plan, are of essential 
importance for safety of life at sea, safety and efficiency of navigation and protection of the marine 
environment. 
 
The need for voyage and passage planning applies to all vessels. There are several factors that 
may impede the safe navigation of all vessels and additional factors that may impede the navigation 
of large vessels or vessels carrying hazardous cargoes. These factors shall be taken into account 
in the preparation of the plan and in the subsequent monitoring of the execution of the plan. 
 
Voyage and passage planning includes appraisal, i.e. gathering all information relevant to the 
contemplated voyage or passage; detailed planning of the whole voyage or passage from berth to 
berth, including those areas necessitating the presence of a pilot; execution of the plan; and the 
monitoring of the progress of the vessel in the implementation of the plan. These components of 
voyage/passage planning are analyzed below. 
 
(b) 
Appraisal 
All information relevant to the contemplated voyage or passage shall be considered. As a minimum 
the following items shall be considered in voyage and passage planning: 
(i) 
The condition and state of the vessel, its stability, and its equipment; any operational 
limitations; its permissible draught at sea in fairways and in ports; its manoeuvring data, 
including any restrictions; 
(ii) 
Any special characteristics of the cargo (especially if hazardous), and its distribution, 
stowage and securing on board the vessel; 
(iii) 
The provision of a competent and well-rested crew to undertake the voyage or passage; 
(iv) 
Requirements for up-to-date certificates and documents concerning the vessel, its 
equipment, crew, passengers or cargo; 
(v) 
Appropriate scale, accurate and up-to-date charts to be used for the intended voyage or 
passage, as well as any relevant permanent or temporary notices to mariners and existing 
radio navigational warnings; 
(vi) 
Accurate and up-to-date sailing directions, lists of lights and lists of radio aids to navigation; 
and 
(vii) 
Any relevant up-to-date additional information, including: 
 
Mariners' routeing guides and passage planning charts, published by competent 
authorities; 
 
Current and tidal atlases and tide tables; 
 
Climatological, hydrographical, and oceanographic data as well as other appropriate 
meteorological information; 
 
Volume of traffic likely to be encountered throughout the voyage or passage; 
(c) 
Planning 
Based on the appraisal, a detailed voyage or passage plan shall be prepared which covers the 
entire voyage or passage from berth to berth, including those areas where the services of a pilot 
to be used. As a minimum the detailed voyage or passage plan shall include the following factors:

Execution

accordance with the plan or any changes made thereto. Factors which shall be considered when 
executing the plan, or deciding on any departure therefrom includes: 
 
(i) 
The reliability and condition of the vessel's navigational equipment; 
(ii) 
Estimated times of arrival at critical points for tide heights and flow; 
(iii) 
Meteorological conditions, (particularly in areas known to be affected by frequent periods of 
low visibility) as well as weather routeing information; 
(iv) 
Daytime versus night-time passing of danger points, and any effect this may have on position 
fixing accuracy; and 
(v) 
Traffic conditions, especially at navigational focal points. 
(e) 
Monitoring 
The plan shall be available at all times on the bridge to allow officers of the navigational watch 
immediate access and reference to the details of the plan. The progress of the vessel in 
accordance with the voyage and passage plan shall be closely and continuously monitored. Any 
changes made to the plan shall be clearly marked and recorded.

which includes Vessel Statutory and Mandatory Certificates. Having these certificates on board the vessel,

operations. 
 
By definition, statutory certificates are certificates which are required by the law of the country where the 
vessel is registered. These relate to safety and environment protection laws and regulations, which the 
vessel is required to comply with such as SOLAS, MARPOL, STCW, MLC 2006. 
 
Mandatory Certificates are the certificates which are not required by law but are mandatory to carry on 
board for trading purpose.   
 
As applicable, vessel crew and masters in particular shall be aware of what all certificates are statutory; 
i.e. the ones that are required to be onboard by law. Not having these certificate onboard when a vessel 
to sea without any can have drastic implications.

7.10. OPERATIONAL CONTROL 
7.10.1. OPERATING IN PETROLEUM PORTS 
(a) 
Terminal/Berth Restrictions 
No Vessels except the vessels visiting the ADNOC Group terminals for Loading/Discharging 
operations are allowed to enter the Petroleum Ports without having a craft entry permit issued by 
Petroleum Ports Authority (PPA). 
 
Unless directed by the Harbour Master of the relevant port, Marine crafts, vessels and rigs shall 
not approach within 500 meters around any berth/terminal at which any vessel is engaged in 
loading or discharging any petroleum cargo of any description. 
 
No communication by any craft, vessel or rig with a vessel alongside any berth is allowed without 
prior permission of the port control authority. 
         
(b) 
Berthing & Jetty Operations 
All the berthing and jetty operations shall be performed as per The Petroleum Port Regulations 
[Ref. 2] which includes specific port information and requirements. 
 
In general, berthing and mooring shall be controlled to minimize the hazard the vessel represents 
to the port facilities or offshore installation and vice versa.   
 
Terminal arrangements shall be designed to avoid hazards, prevent incidents and reduce risks to 
people, the environment and asset to As Low As Reasonably Practicable.  This shall be 
demonstrated by a formal Risk Assessment carried out by the relevant ADNOC Group. The 
following hazards where relevant may be considered in the Risk Assessment: 
 
(i) 
Allision of a vessel with a jetty; 
(ii) 
Objects dropped from a vessel onto a jetty or subsea facility such as a pipeline or which may 
compromise the safety of divers or submersible vessels; and 
(iii) 
Potential for anchors to interact with subsea systems including pipelines or wellheads. 
Vessel or shore gangways, as appropriate, shall be rigged and ready to provide safe access for 
boarding personnel.  
 
It shall be the responsibility of the Master and Crew to ensure that the mooring arrangement is 
adequate in all respects to maintain the vessel in the berthing position during cargo handling 
operations. Furthermore, the vessel shall be moored to the satisfaction of the Pilot. 
 
(c) 
Vessel Double-Berthing 
If, for operational purposes, a vessel shall double berth: 
 
(i) 
The outside vessel shall provide safe access to the inside vessel 
(ii) 
The inside vessel shall provide safe gangway access to the shore. 
 
(d) 
Anchoring in Petroleum Port 
All ADNOC Petroleum Ports have designated anchorages as delineated on the relevant British 
Admiralty charts. All vessels shall obtain prior permission from the relevant port authority for 
anchoring in these areas.

7.10.4. ADDITIONAL REQUIREMENTS FOR OFFSHORE FIELD AREAS  
(a) 
500 meters Safety Zone 
Offshore installations shall be designed to withstand reasonably foreseeable accidental collision 
impacts. The greatest proportion of risk from collision comes from the vessels that are invited to 
enter the 500m zone, i.e. the ones that can and should be managed by the installation. 
 
Current installation structural design standards such as BS EN ISO 19902 do not give a specific 
accidental impact energy value to be considered but require reasonably foreseeable collision 
events to be analyzed and designed for. A collision energy value of 14MJ has traditionally been 
accepted as reasonably appropriate, representing a high energy collision (where substantial 
damage may be incurred but not lead to progressive collapse or endanger personnel), of a 5000t 
displacement vessel (typical supply vessel size), drifting out-of-control (at a speed of 4 knots), in a 
sea state with significant wave height of approximately 4m. However, accidental impact capability 
ranges of fixed installations ranges from 6MJ or even less to 25MJ for some modern installations. 
 
The Guidelines for Offshore Marine Operations (GOMO), OGUK Guidelines for Ship/Installation 
Collision Avoidance information on agreed industry good practice, Offshore Technology Report 
Effective Collision Risk Management for Offshore Installations (OTO 1999 052) shall be referred 
for prevention of any accidental impact due to collision.

(i) 
Critical Activity Mode of Operation (CAM) 
Any DP vessel, including DP class 2 and 3, can have the redundancy concept defeated if its 
systems and equipment are not configured and operated in the correct manner. The purpose 
of a CAM of operation is to detail in a clear and unambiguous manner how to configure a 
vessel’s DP system, including power generation, distribution, propulsion and PRS, so that 
the DP system, as a whole, meets its maximum level of redundancy, functionality and 
operation and is as fault tolerant and fault resistant as it can be. For DP class 2 and 3 vessels, 
the CAM usually defines the most robust fault tolerant configuration of the DP system, 
ensuring that a single point failure does not result in a condition exceeding the vessel’s 
identified WCF. 
 
The CAM gives tabular guidance to the DPO on actions to take when the CAM configuration 
is not met. 
 
Every DP vessel has a unique CAM configuration. A CAM configuration is derived from a 
detailed review of the vessel’s DP FMEA and its operational characteristics. The CAM 
configuration should be the default operational mode for a DP vessel, when conducting 
activities deemed to be critical. 
 
(ii) 
Task Appropriate Mode (TAM) 
This is a risk-based mode and shall be derived from a comprehensive risk assessment 
process. TAM is how to configure and operate the vessel’s DP system, accepting that a 
failure could result in a condition exceeding the vessel’s identified WCF possibly leading to 
blackout or loss of position. 
 
A TAM configuration is a choice that is consciously made. This mode may be appropriate in 
situations where it is determined that the risks associated with a loss of position are low and 
will not result in damage to people, environment or equipment. The conditions under which 
a DP offshore supply vessel may operate in TAM should be defined and could, for example, 
relate to operations well clear of the 500 metre safety zone of floating or critical subsea 
assets and where the consequences of a loss of position have been evaluated and deemed 
acceptable.

RIGS AND BARGES STANDARD TOWING ARRANGEMENTS 
Towing operations mean towing on the stern using towline and does not include side-tow or pushing. All 
towing equipment shall be clearly marked, color coded and have valid certification. Before the tow 
proceeds, the tug, the towing arrangements, the towed unit, watertight integrity and the sea-fastening 
arrangements of equipment and cargo, if any, shall be subject to a satisfactory inspection by an approved 
Third Party or Marine Warranty Surveyor. 
 
(a) 
Arrangement 
Onboard vessel fittings for towing shall be located on stiffeners and/or girders, which are part of 
the deck construction so as to facilitate efficient distribution of the towing load. Other arrangements 
may be accepted (for chocks in bulwarks, etc.) provided the strength is confirmed adequate for the 
intended service. 
 
(b) 
Load Consideration 
The minimum design load applied to supporting hull structures for onboard vessel fittings is to be: 
 
(i) 
For normal towing operations, 1.25 times the intended maximum towing load (e.g. static 
bollard pull); 
(ii) 
For other towing service, the minimum breaking strength of the tow line shall be according 
to IACS Recommendation No. 10 “Anchoring, Mooring and Towing Equipment” (see Notes); 
(iii) 
For fittings intended to be used for, both, normal and other towing operations, the greater of 
the design loads according to (i) and (ii).

 
Side projected area including that of deck cargoes as given by the loading manual is to be taken 
into account for selection of towing lines and the loads applied to onboard vessel fittings and 
supporting hull structure. 
 
The increase of the minimum breaking strength for synthetic ropes according to Recommendation 
No. 10 [Ref. 3] needs not to be taken into account for the loads applied to onboard vessel fittings 
and supporting hull structure.

(a) 
Speaking and Listening 
Communication involves a sender, a receiver, and a message that is understood by both. Two-
way, face-to-face communication is the best way to convey messages on the job. This method

and allows the receiver to ask questions about the message.

important to tailor the message to meet the needs and characteristics of the receiver or audience. 
Use a combination of oral and written communications for technical or complex messages.

use positive reinforcement as well as criticism and correction.

better listening are: 
 
(i) 
Not talking when others are speaking. 
(ii) 
Understanding the other person’s point of view.

Maintaining eye contact.

Sharing the responsibility for communicating effectively. 
(v) 
Clarifying the message

7.12. PERSONNEL TRANSPORT 
One of the activities specific to offshore operations is the transfer of personnel between vessels and other 
offshore structures. Such transfers can include movements of personnel at crew change and shift change 
from vessel to vessel and also between vessels, offshore structures, barges and crew boats as well as 
to and from the quayside. All personnel transfer arrangements shall be in line with the guidance provided 
in International Maritime Contractors Association (IMCA) [Ref. 5]. The primary methods of personnel 
transfer covered are: 
 
(i) 
Small boat or launch; 
(ii) 
Larger crew boat or support vessel; 
(iii) 
Personnel transfer basket; 
(iv) 
Gangways, bridge or accommodation ladders, including motion-compensated hydraulic 
gangways; 
(v) 
Mating ‘surfer’ structures allowing personnel to transfer safely. 
Note: All personnel shall wear SOLAS Approved Life Jackets and shall have hands free while moving 
to/from the vessel or installation. 
 
7.12.2. VESSEL TO VESSEL TRANSFER USING A SMALL BOAT 
A small boat is any craft of a type likely to be stowed on a larger vessel, platform, barge or offshore 
structure, and most often launched and recovered from some form of a lifting device. Typically, such craft 
are less than 10m in length. Vessel to vessel transfer using a small boat shall only be undertaken when 
alternative means for the transfer are impracticable or less safe. A person shall be available in the small 
boat and on the vessel or offshore structure to assist those being transferred. 
 
Weather and sea state shall be assessed by the Masters of the vessels involved so as to determine if it 
is suitable for the use of a small boat and to allow close approach and safe transfer even when making a 
lee. A Fast Rescue Boat (based on the risk assessment prior to the operations) with its operational crew 
shall be ready for launching from one of the vessels. 
 
Vessel to vessel transfer shall be planned to avoid transfer during the hours of darkness. If transfer of 
specific personnel becomes unavoidable at night, this shall be dealt with in a specific and dedicated risk 
assessment and the operation only undertaken when it is considered safe to do so.

(a) 
Embarkation/Disembarkation 
The means of embarking and disembarking personnel to and from the crew boat at either end of 
the transit is very important. This shall be conducted in as safe a manner as possible, as it can 
prove to be the most hazardous part of the operation. 
 
In practice, personnel transfer by crew boat may also include transfer by small boat, basket, 
gangway, surfer or accommodation ladder. Participants involved in the transfer shall be briefed on 
the procedures and on the lifesaving and emergency equipment available. 
 
When the crew boat comes alongside a vessel or offshore structure, relative movement shall be 
taken into consideration, as well as the relative heights of the decks between which personnel 
transfer is made. Assistance shall be available at either end of the personnel transfer with the 
presence of experienced person to supervise the moment of transfer and maintain communications 
with the bridge. Personnel shall only step across with the ability to freely use both hands and some 
form of hand rail or support shall be provided. Where necessary there shall be access in or through 
bulwarks using movable gates, such that personnel need not climb over rails or bulwarks during 
transfer.

Specially designed attachments to crew boats, larger vessels and offshore structures, often 
referred to as ‘surfers’, are sometimes used. These may take the form of a purpose built 
device on the bow of a crew boat (‘male’ section), designed to fit into a receptacle frame or 
structure on a larger vessel or offshore structure (‘female’ section). The crew boat or smaller 
vessel approaches and docks with the ‘surfer’ receptacle allowing personnel to step safely 
across. 
 
(ii) 
Swing Ropes 
The use of swing ropes for personnel transfer is prohibited.

Crew boats shall have sufficient SOLAS approved life jackets and life rafts for all personnel

escape routes in the event of emergencies, location of emergency equipment such as flares etc.

of transit, sufficient potable water available for the number of personnel in transit and appropriate

Gangways and accommodation ladders are the primary means by which personnel transfer between a 
vessel and the quayside, and occasionally from one vessel or offshore structure to another. Gangways

should be fitted with non-slip walkways and handrails. Equipment shall be regularly inspected and 
maintained, including a visual check to ensure it is clean and free of slip/trip/fall hazards. Appropriate

personnel falling from the gangway or accommodation ladder, an appropriate safety net shall be used. 
Where required, a life buoy fitted with a line and water activated light shall be made available. 
 
Gangways and accommodation ladders shall be adequately lit along their full length. Their approaches 
and egress routes shall be kept free of obstructions and trip hazards and should provide direct and safe 
access to the deck at each end. Gangways and ladders shall not be used at angles of inclination which 
render their use unsafe. All gangways and personnel using them shall be monitored and controlled.  
 
(a) 
Bridges 
Some larger vessels (for example heavy-lift crane vessels, pipelay barges, accommodation 
vessels or MODUs) have long (around 50m) bridges to effect transfer of personnel. These can be 
fixed at one end and slide on rollers at the other end to allow for relative movement. Such 
equipment can also be hydraulically controlled and can be lifted into place and supported by a 
crane or else have its own dedicated support mechanism. They may be fitted with alarm systems 
activated by a certain amount of movement. The bridges and the personnel crossing them should 
be closely monitored and controlled. 
 
(b) 
Hydraulic Gangways 
These are purpose-designed gangways mounted on a vessel which connect to another 
vessel/offshore structure to allow personnel to pass safely across. They are fitted with hydraulic 
heave compensation which adjusts the gangway length and/or horizontal/vertical angles to 
compensate for the vessel relative movement. Such equipment may also be fitted with a ‘traffic 
light’ system to prevent movement of personnel onto the gangway if any automatic adjustments 
are taking place.

The EEDI is a non-prescriptive, performance-based mechanism that leaves the choice of

will be tightened every five years to keep pace with technological developments of new 
efficiency and reduction measures. Reduction rates have been established until the period

from a reference line representing the average efficiency for vessels built between 2000 and 
2010. The EEDI is developed for the largest and most energy intensive segments of the 
world merchant fleet and embraces emissions from new vessels covering the following 
vessel types: tankers, bulk carriers, gas carriers, general cargo vessels, container vessels, 
refrigerated cargo carriers and combination carriers.

vessels and cruise passenger vessels having non-conventional propulsion. These 
amendments mean that vessel types responsible for approximately 85% of the CO2 
emissions from international shipping are incorporated under the international regulatory 
regime.

Law 21 of 2005 concerning Waste Management in emirate of Abu Dhabi. Centre of Waste

waste in the Emirates. 
 
Major hazardous waste streams in UAE are Petrochemical waste, Medical Waste, and e-Waste. 
To avoid hazardous waste export the cash-rich government-owned petrochemical companies have 
established centralised treatment facility. Centre for Waste Management (CWM) has established 
treatment facilities for Medical Waste Incineration, Engine Oil treatment, Tyre Shredding through 
PPP business model. 
 
The UAE Government is encouraging private companies for e-waste recycling. Major electronic 
distributors have established e-waste collection centres and subsequent transfer to established 
facilities. However, good fraction of e-waste is still collected in an unorganised manner and 
exported to South and Southeast Asian countries. 
 
Shipment of hazardous waste coming into UAE is being well-monitored and well-controlled. In 
recent past only a single incident of mixed plastic waste was reported. The waste was imported 
through Dubai ports to be treated at a facility in Ajman. The Dubai government objected to import 
and the issue was raised at Environment Agency (UK). As a result, the transporting company was 
fined about GBP 75,000. 
 
UAE has efficient law enforcement machinery. Thus after the establishment of CWM in 2008, there 
has been significant improvement in waste management in general and hazardous waste 
transportation in particular. Further impetus is required by the government to invest into the better 
treatment/storage facilities for e-waste, nuclear waste and other such waste. The legislation shall 
also be made more illustrative to check the aberrations regarding trans-boundary movement of 
hazardous waste.

leaves the generator facility where it was produced, until it reaches the off-site waste management 
facility that will store, treat or dispose of the hazardous waste. The manifesting responsibilities vary 
depending on the mode of transportation (highway, water, rail or air).

7.16.4. DRILLS AND EXERCISES  
It is important to differentiate between Drills and Exercises as follows: 
  
(i) 
Drills are live activities that are limited to specific procedures usually involving participants 
from the same organisation or location.  They shall be conducted as per the schedule 
established by the GC or Affiliate marine logistics department, to ensure proficiency and 
quality of performance. 
(ii) 
Drills are used to teach repetitive or psychomotor skills that are intended to become “second 
nature” with the objective of maintaining a high level of readiness, practise hands-on skills, 
test equipment, test procedures.  
(iii) 
Exercises, by contrast, are held on a less frequent basis but are usually at a larger scale, 
thus constituting more complex training events when compared to drills.  
 
Their purpose is to test and update the emergency response set up involving the vessel. 
 
Exercises and drills shall be used to simulate, over a period of time, all the identified credible emergency 
scenarios, taking into consideration the fact that exercises require more complex planning and lead time.  
 
An exercise can be a tabletop exercise or a realistic full scale deployment of resources in which 
responders and equipment is tested.
