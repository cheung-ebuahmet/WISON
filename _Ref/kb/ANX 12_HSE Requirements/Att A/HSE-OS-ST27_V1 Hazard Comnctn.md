1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Communicating hazards at the workplace is of vital importance as it alerts and informs the personnel of 
potential hazards in the workplace, thus, contributes in overall risk management of a facility. It includes 
various techniques for communicating hazards to employees, contractors, visitors and offsite parties as 
per the nature and severity of the hazard. This enables the personnel to quickly identify the hazards and 
follow control measures as instructed by the hazard communication technique in order to avoid an 
unwanted event. Hazard communication is used when all other control measures have been considered 
but the hazard remains. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this standard is to provide standardized requirements for communicating all identified 
hazards at workplace and eventually protect people from injuries and illnesses associated with those 
hazards. In order to accomplish this, this standard includes labelling for hazardous chemicals, SDS, 
safety signs, safety markings and offsite hazard communication. In addition, this standard provides key 
international references for design and application of hazard communication techniques. 
  
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group and its Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7. 
REQUIREMENTS  
7.1. 
OVERVIEW 
Hazard communication is a system to effectively communicate the hazards and related information to the 
personnel. The information is primarily collected from the Safety Data Sheet (SDS) for chemicals and risk 
assessments for the facility or part of the facility. It includes all hazardous chemicals which are known to 
be present in the workplace and to which employees may be exposed under normal work conditions or 
in foreseeable emergencies. This ensures the management, control and handling of the hazardous 
chemicals is done in a safe and appropriate manner reducing risk to people, property and environment. 
In addition, this ensures that both office and site-based workplaces provide appropriate level of hazard 
communication to enable employees be aware of their surrounding hazards and controls to follow, should 
the hazard(s) releases. 
 
Hazards can be communicated by using a number of techniques depending upon the type of hazard and 
intent of communication. These techniques include: 
 
(i) 
Labelling  
(ii) 
SDS 
(iii) 
Safety Signs  
(iv) 
Safety Markings 
(v) 
Acoustics and Illumination

(a) 
Eliminate 
This includes removing the hazard from the workplace. This is the most effective control measure 
and shall always be considered before any other control measure. For example, redesign the job 
or substitute a substance so that the hazard is removed or eliminated. If eliminating the hazard is 
not reasonably practicable, then substitution, isolation, engineering and procedural control 
measures, or a combination of these control measures to minimize the risk shall be considered. 
 
(b) 
Substitute 
This includes substituting the hazard with a less hazardous option. For example, using a small 
platform to access work at height instead of step ladders.  
 
(c) 
Isolate/Separate 
This includes isolating or separating the hazard from personnel by methods such as enclosing or 
guarding. For example, lock out and tag out of the source of electrical energy during operations or 
maintenance activities, erection of a physical barrier around the work area, restricted access to 
electrical equipment. 
 
(d) 
Engineer 
This includes engineering the control measures to minimize risk. For example, using work 
equipment or other measures to prevent falls where a person cannot avoid working at height, 
installation or using additional machinery such as local exhaust ventilation to control risks from 
dust or fume. 
 
(e) 
Organization 
This includes organization of personnel trainings and improving competencies for the assigned 
task and ensuring effective communication among various parties involved in the assigned task. 
 
(f) 
Procedures 
This includes identification and implementation of documented procedures designed to conduct 
the activity in safe manner. For example, reducing the time workers are exposed to hazards i.e. by 
job rotation, prohibiting use of mobile phones in hazardous areas, providing hazard 
communications, and performing risk assessments. 
 
(g) 
Personal Protective Equipment 
This includes provision of Personal Protective Equipment (PPE). PPE shall be used only after all 
the previous control measures have been assessed and found ineffective in controlling risks to a 
reasonably practicable level. PPE is the last wall of defence in the overall hierarchy of controls and 
include insulating gloves, safety goggles, coveralls, hard hat, safety shoes, respirators, safety 
harness etc. 
 
ADNOC Personal Protective Equipment Standard [Ref. 2] shall be adhered to for the use of proper 
PPE. 
 
Hazard communication lies in the procedural controls. When all the above controls have been 
implemented for identified hazard and it still remains, it shall be communicated to employees, contractors 
and visitors by means of appropriate hazard communication techniques.

create a globally harmonized hazard classification scheme for producing a compatible chemical labelling

Harmonized System (GHS). The aims of the GHS are to: 
 
(i)

(ii)

(iii)

GHS provides a logical and comprehensive approach to: 
 
(i)

(ii)

the defined hazard criteria; and 
(iii)

All hazardous chemicals in ADNOC facilities shall be labelled and provided with SDS as per the 
requirements of GHS [Ref. 3]. 
 
7.3.2. GHS SCOPE 
GHS covers all hazardous chemicals and shall be adopted to cover chemicals in the workplace, transport, 
consumer products, pesticides and pharmaceuticals. GHS includes: 
 
(i) 
Harmonized criteria for classifying substances and mixtures according to their health 
environment and physical hazards; and 
(ii) 
Harmonized hazard communication elements, including requirements for labelling and SDS. 
The target audiences for GHS include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Workers 
(ii) 
Transport workers 
(iii) 
Emergency responders 
(iv) 
Consumers 
7.3.3. ELEMENTS OF GHS 
The two major elements of GHS are: 
 
(i) 
Classification of the hazards of chemicals according to the GHS rules and guidance on 
classifying pure chemicals and mixtures according to its criteria or rules. 
(ii) 
Communication of the hazards and precautionary information using labels and SDS: 
1. 
Labels - with the GHS, certain information shall appear on the label. For example, the 
chemical identity may be required. Standardized hazard statements, signal words and 
symbols shall appear on the label according to the classification of that chemical or 
mixture. Precautionary statements may also be added if required; and 
2. 
SDS - the GHS SDS has 16 sections in a set order, and minimum information is 
prescribed. SDS requirements have been described in Section 7.4 in detail. 
GHS requirements do not apply to office facilities or building, where employees’ normal job is not likely 
to cause chemical exposures or who may not encounter hazardous chemicals other than commercially 
available household cleansers, room fresheners, disinfectants etc. But if the employees from such office

continuous operation and maintenance. The movement of those dangerous goods takes place by

7.4. 
SAFETY DATA SHEETS (SDS) 
7.4.1. GENERAL 
An SDS provides key information regarding a chemical product. Any chemical supplier shall provide a 
complete SDS to ADNOC Group. The manufacturer/supplier shall ensure all SDSs are kept up-to-date 
and provide the latest version as soon as possible. 
 
ADNOC Group shall be responsible for: 
 
(i) 
Acting in accordance with a risk assessment in regard of the conditions of chemical product 
use; 
(ii) 
Taking necessary precautionary measures in a given work situation; and 
(iii) 
Keeping users informed appropriately about hazards relevant to their individual workplace. 
When formulating specific instructions for the workplace, the general recommendations of relevant SDSs 
shall be considered. 
 
Since an SDS is merely chemical product related, it cannot take into account all the possible situations 
which may arise at any given workplace. Therefore, an SDS only constitutes a part of the information 
necessary to establish a safety programme. 
 
When a chemical product is a mixture, it is not necessary to produce individual SDSs corresponding to 
each relevant ingredient. Instead, a single SDS for a mixture may be produced and provided. Where 
information on each ingredient constituting the chemical product is useful, it should be provided. 
 
Any vehicle, aircraft or marine unit transporting hazardous substances/chemicals shall have a copy of 
latest SDS. 
 
7.4.2. CONTENTS OF AN SDS

(ii)

(iii) 
In an SDS, the source of information need not necessarily be provided. Nonetheless, the 
source should preferably be provided to increase confidence in the information; 
(iv)

(v)

(vi) 
The page numbering system should include the total number of pages or should indicate the 
last page as such; 
(vii)

(viii) Where a specific serial number and revision date (version number) are written on the first

7.4.3. MIXTURE OF HAZARDOUS CHEMICALS 
Chemical mixtures vary widely in their specific chemical contents and concentrations. Some mixtures

protection, health hazard information and emergency evacuation. 
 
The selection and application of safety signs shall be determined by ADNOC and/or Group Company

hazards.

The purpose of appropriate safety signs placement is to draw attention rapidly to objects and

Acoustic signals and illuminated signs need to be inspected at regular intervals in order to ensure that 
they are functioning correctly. The more hostile environment, the more frequently those shall be inspected 
and maintained. 
 
(a) 
Acoustic Signals 
(i) 
Acoustic signals shall be set at a level which is considerably higher at their frequency than 
the ambient level, for example 10 dB above the level of ambient noise at that frequency. It 
shall be ensured that the level is neither excessive nor painful for personnel in the vicinity. 
(ii) 
Signals shall be easily recognisable, particularly in terms of pulse length and the interval 
between pulses or groups of pulses; 
(iii) 
Acoustic signals shall not be used more than one at a time, this can cause confusion among 
personnel. 
(b) 
Illuminated Signals 
(i) 
Illuminated signs shall be bright enough to be seen without causing glare. 
(ii) 
Care shall be taken to ensure that a number of illuminated signs are not used together if this 
could give rise to confusion. Confusion could also arise if an illuminated sign is placed close 
to any other similar light source. 
(iii) 
The luminous area of the sign shall be of a single safety color or contain a pictogram on a 
specified background consistent with the Table 7.5.1 above. 
(iv) 
If an illuminated sign can be either on; continuously or  operate intermittently (e.g. flash on 
and off), use the flashing sign to indicate high level of danger or a more urgent need for 
intervention or action; 
(v) 
The duration and frequency of flashes for an intermittent illuminated sign shall be such as to 
ensure the message is appropriately understood, and avoid any confusion with other 
illuminated signs, including continuous illuminated signs. 
(vi) 
If a flashing sign (e.g. flashing beacons) is used instead of, or together with an acoustic 
signal, it is important to synchronize the two. This mean that the duration and frequency of 
flashes need to be in line with both pulse length and interval of acoustic signal. For example 
with fast flicker rates, epilepsy could be triggered in some personnel or in other cases some 
types of electronic pulse could be a danger in response of stores containing certain 
explosives. 
(vii) 
Where flashing signs (e.g. flashing beacons) are used to warn of imminent danger, it is 
particularly important to ensure that control measures are in place to either detect failure of 
the sign quickly or to prevent its failure.

7.9. 
USING HAND SIGNALS TO DIRECT HAZARDOUS OPERATIONS 
Hand signals can be used to direct hazardous operations such as crane or vehicle manoeuvres. It shall 
be ensured that the signals are precise, simple and easy to make and to understand. Signaller 
competency shall be assured for making and using correct hand signals. 
 
Section 6 and Appendix 1 of ADNOC Lifting Operations Standard [Ref. 20] provides detailed description 
of roles and responsibilities of the means of communication and codes of hand signals. 
 
7.10. OFFSITE HAZARD COMMUNICATION 
In case, the hazard identified in ADNOC facilities can cause damage to the surrounding or nearby areas, 
it shall be communicated to the relevant party for response purposes. If the hazard is due to a hazardous 
chemical, a copy of the SDS of hazardous chemical shall be sent to relevant medical authority. 
 
7.11. MAINTENANCE OF SAFETY SIGNS 
All safety signs and markings or other means of hazard communication such as acoustic and illuminations 
shall be properly maintained so that they are capable of performing the function for which they are 
intended. This can range from the routine cleaning to regular checks of illuminated signs and testing of 
acoustic signals to see that they work properly. 
 
All safety signs shall maintain their intrinsic features under power failure - either from emergency lighting 
or phosphorescent material - unless the hazard is itself eliminated by the power failure. 
 
7.12. TRAINING & COMPETENCE 
Training of users is an integral part of hazard communication. All employees and contractor personnel 
who are required to interpret labels, SDS, safety signs and markings, shall be provided with appropriate 
training for the nature their work or potential exposure. Key target audience that is more exposed to 
hazards include workers at sites, emergency responders, personnel involved in transportation, handling 
and those involved in the preparation and maintenance of labels, SDS and other hazard communication 
techniques.  
 
Employees and/or personnel involved in the supply of hazardous chemicals shall be appropriately trained. 
As a minimum, the following elements shall be considered: 
 
(i) 
The hazards of the chemicals in the work area. (The hazards may be discussed by chemical 
or by hazard categories such as flammability); 
(ii) 
The details of the hazard communication program developed, including an explanation of 
the labels received on shipped containers and the workplace labelling system; 
(iii) 
how to read and interpret information on labels and the SDS;  
(iv) 
the order of information to obtain and use the appropriate hazard information;
