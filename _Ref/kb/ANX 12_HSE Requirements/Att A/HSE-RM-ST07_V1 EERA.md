1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Escape, Evacuation and Rescue Assessment (EERA) is a technique to assess the performance of the 
escape, evacuation and rescue (EER) measures and arrangements.  
The objectives of an EERA is to ensure that there are adequate measures at the facility to enable people 
to escape, muster, evacuate and be rescued in the event of a Major Accident: 
 
Detection and alarm systems are in place to communicate the emergencies effectively; 
 
Escape routes are available and useable under emergency conditions, from all work locations to 
the place where people muster; 
 
There are places where people can muster and /or take refuge with supporting systems while an 
emergency is being investigated and if needed while evacuation preparations are being made, 
which take into account the nature of the hazards that personnel may be exposed to; 
 
There are evacuation arrangements and effective support systems that are suitable to allow all 
people on the facility to be evacuated in a controlled manner under emergency conditions; and 
 
There are arrangements to recover people who leave a facility in an emergency and transport them 
to a place of safety. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this Standard is to set out the requirements and provide an overall process of the EERA 
and its application within ADNOC Group. This Standard establishes the mandatory requirements and 
consistent practices for conducting and documenting EERA studies at ADNOC Group. This standard also 
outlines methodology, approach and requirements for Temporary Refuge Impairment Assessment (TRIA) 
applicable to Temporary Refuge, if provided or recommended as a part of EERA study. 
 
All EERA studies for ADNOC Group shall be undertaken in accordance with the requirements of this 
Standard.  
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to the ADNOC Group and its 
Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented, complied with and monitored at all times.

7. 
REQUIREMENTS  
7.1 
EERA LIFECYCLE 
The EERA is an integral part of the risk management process to ensure that the risk is managed at 
tolerable levels and ALARP demonstrated for both project activities and operations.  
 
Typically, an EERA is performed during the FEED Stage of the project and is later updated during the 
EPC (Detailed Engineering) Stage based on updated safety studies, documents and newly-established/ 
updated information and or data. EERA study later forms part of the HSEIA dossier. 
 
For existing facilities, where EERA is not available, it shall be carried out at the first available (earliest) 
opportunity and update the HSEIA Dossier accordingly as per ADNOC HSEIA Standard, HSE-RM-ST02 
[Ref. 1].   
 
As part of COMAH/HSEIA update, EERA shall be reviewed to determine if significant changes to facilities, 
escape & evacuation measures, EER supporting system, manning, refuge, etc., are observed or carried 
out.  EERA study shall be updated based on these changes to ensure assessment reflect the updated 
changes to EER measures and supporting system. HSEIA Dossier shall be updated and approved 
accordingly, as per ADNOC HSEIA Standard, HSE-RM-ST02 [Ref. 1].

7.2 
ASSESSMENT METHODOLOGY 
7.2.1 
OVERVIEW 
EERA is goal-based assessment where goals are set for escape, evacuation rescue provisions and 
supporting systems such as detection, alarm and communications, etc.  An assessment is carried out to 
determine if these goals are achieved for the identified Major Accident Hazards (MAHs). The goals are 
set to ensure that each aspect of Escape, Evacuation and Rescue (EER) are designed robustly and 
performs effectively within the required timeframe to allow all personnel to safely escape, muster, 
evacuate and be rescued to a safe place.

7.2.5 
ENDURANCE PERIODS 
One of the critical steps in EER goal assessment is to determine expected maximum escape and 
evacuation times.  Calculation of the time needed to escape helps in determining whether personnel can

SMOKE DISPERSION & INGRESS CALCULATIONS 
Smoke from burning hydrocarbons consists of a mixture of gaseous combustion products and unburned carbon 
particles. The term "smoke" is used here to describe the mixture, and the term "soot" is used to describe the solid 
particles. The nature of the smoke depends strongly on the supply of oxygen to the fire. Two basic types are 
distinguished: 
 
Well-ventilated (or fuel-controlled) fires, which occur in the open, such as on an open deck or on the sea 
surface or open onshore areas. These fires have sufficient air to achieve complete combustion. 
 
Under-ventilated (or ventilation-controlled) fires, which occur inside modules or congested process areas. 
These fires are unable to draw sufficient air to achieve ideal combustion. 
 
Smoke Generation 
For most hydrocarbon fuels, the stoichiometric ratio is 15 kg air per kg of fuel. Thus, in well-ventilated fires with 
sufficient air for complete combustion, the smoke production rate is 16 times the fuel burning rate. The 
temperature of the generated smoke may be up to 1200OC, although it may also be much less.  Worst-case 
conditions may in fact be when the temperature is lower, causing the plume to rise less. Theoretical combustion 
calculations for fires inside modules have indicated smoke outlet temperatures of 400-700OC.  
Smoke Composition 
Combustion gases from burning hydrocarbons consist mainly of a mixture of nitrogen, carbon dioxide (CO2), 
carbon monoxide (CO) and water vapour. Because of the high proportion of nitrogen in the air, hot nitrogen tends 
to dominate in the combustion products. The concentration of toxic gases in the smoke is important because of 
their potential impact on personnel. For modelling purposes, it is necessary to know the initial concentration close 
to the fire. The dilution in the smoke plume can then be predicted theoretically, to determine the concentration at 
any point downwind. 
The proportion of toxic gases in the smoke depends on the chemical structure of the burning materials and the 
degree of ventilation to the fire. The differences between different hydrocarbons are quite small, and ventilation 
has the main effect. In general, reduced ventilation greatly increases the ratio of CO to CO2 in the combustion 
gases. Typical gas concentrations close to the fire are given in table below. In each case the oxygen 
concentration is zero.

Smoke Dispersion Modelling 
For modelling of the smoke, gas plume approach shall be considered.  Based on the type of fire (well ventilated 
or under ventilated or gas or liquid fire), composition of smoke is determined considering Nitrogen, Oxygen, 
Carbon Monoxide and Carbon Dioxide.  Based on the heat and material balance for representative streams and 
assuming stoichiometric combustion of H2S, representative SO2 shall be calculated.  As combustion products are 
nitrogen dominant (essentially hot air), the releases shall be modelled considering fluid representative similar 
molecular weight in the dispersion calculations. A characteristic source temperature of 600 to 1000°C shall be 
used. The mass flow rate of combustion gases is taken to be 16x the release rate of the scenarios/flammable 
material. Because a real smoke plume tends to be longer than predicted dispersions, it may be appropriate to 
artificially extend the predicted plumes. This can be achieved by using F stability conditions, instead of the D 
stability. 
Gas and Smoke Ingress within TR 
The possible routes for gas or smoke ingress within the TR includes ventilation intakes, doors, seepages through 
small leaks, failure of or damage to structure/external fabrics, etc.  
In normal conditions, air is circulated through enclosed areas, including the TR, by the HVAC system. With a 
typical ventilation rate of 6-10 air changes per hour, it is vital to stop the ventilation before gas or smoke reaches 
the HVAC inlets. However, it is also desirable to keep the ventilation running for as long as possible, to preserve 
internal conditions and to maintain pressurisation, which minimises smoke ingress through open doors.  Based 
on the type of building and usage, building may be designed for various Air Change Rate (ACR).  In any cases, 
it is recommended to use design ACR rates or actual rates for existing facilities based on the available records. 
The reliability of the HVAC gas or smoke detectors and dampers should be addressed during the detailed design. 
Gas or smoke detectors take at least 20 sec to activate, and hence even automatically closing ventilation dampers 
will not exclude gas or smoke altogether.  Thus, for a short time, the HVAC will draw in gas or smoke from outside 
at the normal ventilation rate, and this phase may dominate the resulting internal gas or smoke concentration.  
There are usually several doors to the TR. The main entrance is likely to be used by personnel escaping from 
the process areas and emergency teams addressing the fire or rescuing wounded personnel. If the wind direction 
is critical for TR impairment by gas or smoke, this entrance is likely to be in the gas plume and or smoke plume 
for fire scenarios. 
In the Piper Alpha accident, a door to the accommodation was hooked open, and the area became smoke logged 
within about 30 minutes, despite the HVAC system having shut down. The probability of such a failure to seal the 
TR depends on the effectiveness of the platform safety management in promoting procedures for keeping doors 
closed and for reacting correctly in an emergency. In an emergency, an air-lock is unlikely to be used as carefully 
as in normal operations. 
As long as the HVAC system continues to function, the TR should remain positively pressurised, and gas or 
smoke ingress through open doors should be minimal. However, once even minor levels of smoke or gas reach 
the HVAC intakes, the system is designed to shut down. Then, gas or smoke will be able to enter through the 
doors. A ventilation rate of 1-5 air changes per hour has been suggested for this phase.  However, based on the 
size of the room, number of estimated opening/door operations during evacuation, airlock design etc. estimate 
can be done for the potential air change rates for these buildings and shall be used for the remaining duration.   
Although a TR is ventilated through the HVAC intakes, and has designated exhaust outlets, all with smoke 
dampers, the TR may not be perfectly air-tight. A typical HVAC system design would allow for seepage through 
small leaks including doors at a rate equivalent to one air change per hour under the positive pressure from the 
HVAC system. If the HVAC system shuts down, gas or smoke may enter the TR by the opposite routes after 
internal positive pressure has dissipated.
