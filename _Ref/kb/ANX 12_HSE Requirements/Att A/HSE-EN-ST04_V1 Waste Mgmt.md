streams are generated during normal day to day operations.  Proper management of this waste is 
essential, not only for environmental reasons, but also to ensure compliance with UAE laws, and to avoid 
long-term claims for environmental damage liability.

A list of the main sources of waste produced in the oil and gas industry and their potential environmental 
significance is provided in Appendix 1. In addition to the above, a variety of wastes are produced during 
marine vessel operations. These are all subject to the requirements of MARPOL. 
 
In order to minimize potential adverse impacts on the environment and to protect people from exposure 
to hazardous substances, it is important that these wastes are effectively managed and disposed of 
properly.  This requires a systematic approach to waste production/handling/disposal, based on best 
practice principles of waste management.  
 
The effective management of wastes must be considered at all stages in the life cycle of a project, from 
the initial business case evaluation through to decommissioning and disposal. Adopting a life cycle 
approach facilitates an optimisation of costs, resources and environmental and social benefits.  
 
Ultimate responsibility for the safe collection, handling and disposal of wastes lies with Group Company 
management.  Group Companies and subcontractors are responsible for implementing the requirements 
of this Standard and for ensuring that all wastes are managed in a safe and environmentally acceptable 
manner, consistent with individual Group Company waste management policies and objectives. 
 
This document sets out the framework for an effective waste management strategy for Group Companies.  
It is based on international oil industry best practice and international regulatory approaches to waste 
management, taking into account local legislative requirements and the specific circumstances of the 
UAE. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
This Standard establishes the guiding principles by which waste must be managed and disposed of in 
the ADNOC Group.  In particular, this document is designed with the following objectives: 
 
 
Provide guidance on the different types of waste that may be generated by Group Companies and 
how they must be controlled/managed. 
 
Establish the requirement for waste inventories. 
 
Assist Group Companies in the development of waste management strategies, based on the 
principle of hierarchical control. 
 
Establish requirements for a Duty of Care. 
 
Ensure that waste management forms an integral part of Group Company operations management 
procedures. 
 
Address past practices and corrective action. 
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group and its Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

LAWS AND REGULATIONS 
4.1. 
EXISTING LEGISLATION 
Waste disposal in the UAE is regulated through the following legislation: 
 
 
Federal Law No. 24 of 1999 for the Protection and Development of the Environment. 
 
Regulation for Handling Hazardous Materials, Hazardous Wastes and Medical Wastes, 2001  
 
Law No (21) of 2005 for Waste Management in the Emirate of Abu Dhabi. 
 
FANR-REG-26: Regulation for Pre-disposal Management of Radioactive Waste 
Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation 
 
 
Federal Law by Decree No 6 of 2009, Concerning the Peaceful Uses of Nuclear Energy, which 
establishes the Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation (FANR). 
FANR is the independent government body charged with regulating and licensing nuclear activities in the 
UAE, which in addition to the nuclear power programme, includes radioactive material and radiation 
sources used in medicine, research, oil exploration and other industries. 
 
The Authority determines all matters relating to the control and supervision of the nuclear sector in the 
UAE, in particular nuclear safety and security, radiation protection and safeguards. All obligations under 
the relevant international treaties, conventions or agreements entered into by the UAE are carried out by 
FANR. 
 
The FANR responsibilities include the control of disposal of radioactive waste and the licensing of facilities 
that dispose of radioactive waste, which includes NORM. 
 
Abu Dhabi Centre of Waste Management (TADWEER) 
 
Established in December 2008, the Centre is the lead agency responsible for controlling and coordinating 
waste management activities throughout the Emirate. This includes implementing the Waste 
Management Strategy and all aspects of service delivery required to establish a full cycle integrated waste 
management system.  
 
At present, the TADWEER strategy covers all wastes generated throughout the Emirate of Abu Dhabi 
including hazardous wastes produced by the ADNOC Group (oil and gas industry) and excludes 
radioactive wastes. However, the activities of the TADWEER are developing and Group Companies must 
ensure that their waste disposal practices fully comply with any future TADWEER requirements.  
 
Group Companies must ensure that their activities comply with all relevant Federal and Local laws and 
regulations at all times, including any that may be introduced after the publication of this Standard.

7.1. 
WASTE CLASSIFICATION, SOURCES & INVENTORY 
Under UAE Regulations, waste is defined as:  
 
‘All hazardous or non-hazardous wastes including radioactive wastes to be disposed of according to the 
terms of law including solid wastes, liquid wastes, gas, fume, vapour, dust, hazardous wastes and medical 
wastes’. [Ref. 6]. 
 
Waste is defined under European legislation as any substance or object which the producer or the person 
in possession of it discards, or intends or is required to discard (see European Union Framework Directive 
on Waste, 75/442/EEC as amended by Directives 91/156/EEC,91/692/EEC and 2008/98/EC [Ref. 1]).  
The term includes scrap material, effluent, or unwanted surplus substances or articles which require to 
be disposed of because they are broken, worn out, contaminated or otherwise spoiled.   
 
Hazardous Waste is defined under European legislation as any waste which exhibits certain properties 
as listed in Annex 2 of  Directive 2008/98/EC including: 
 
 
Explosive 
 
Oxidizing 
 
Highly flammable (with a flash point less than 21oC) or flammable (flash point between 21C and 
55C) 
 
Irritant 
 
Harmful 
 
Toxic 
 
Carcinogenic 
 
Corrosive 
 
Infectious 
 
Toxic for reproduction 
 
Mutagenic (capable of inducing genetic mutation or increasing its rate) 
 
Waste which releases toxic or very toxic gases in contact with water, air or acid. 
 
Sensitizing 
 
Eco toxic (potential for biological, chemical or physical stressors to affect ecosystems) 
 
Waste capable after disposal of yielding another substance which possesses any of the above 
characteristics.  
In the United States, separate definitions apply to solid and hazardous waste. Solid waste is any 
discarded material that is not excluded under other sections of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR).  
 
A solid waste is a hazardous waste if it exhibits any of the characteristics of hazardous waste listed in 
Sub part C of 40 CFR Section 261.4; Reactive, Corrosive, Ignitable, Toxic, or is listed as hazardous under 
Section 261.32.

into two parts:  
 
Part I for all garbage other than cargo residues, applicable to all ships.

7.2.1. WASTE MANAGEMENT STRATEGY 
The principles of waste management are based on a hierarchy of management practices which are 
fundamental to the development of a sustainable waste management strategy. Effective management of 
waste begins with pollution prevention. This means the elimination, change or reduction of operating 
practices which result in discharges to land, air or water. This principle of pollution prevention should be 
incorporated into the design and operation of all Group Company facilities (see ADNOC Pollution 
Prevention and Control Standard [Ref. 14]).

hierarchical principle of source reduction, reuse, recycling/recovery, treatment and responsible disposal.  
Waste management in Group Companies must be based on a strategy that considers all elements of the 
hierarchical waste management process i.e.  
 
 
Wastes Inventory and characterisation as described in Section 7.1;

7.2.3. SEGREGATION

This is important from a safety point of view.  Segregation also allows wastes of a similar type to be 
combined, thus simplifying storage/treatment/recycling/disposal arrangements. It is important that waste 
is properly segregated into different types, so that opportunities for reuse and recycling are not 
compromised by, for example, mixing hazardous and non-hazardous wastes.

segregation can result in extensive lab analysis; thereby causing waste disposal costs to escalate.  The 
principles of segregation which are applied to transport, storage and use of materials apply to both 
hazardous waste and non-hazardous waste with the following criteria:

Flammable substances must be kept away from sources of ignition and from oxidising agents;

methods most widely used in the oil and gas industry are briefly described below.  
 
All marine vessels shall comply with the requirements of the International Maritime Organization (IMO), 
including the provisions set out in the International Convention for the Prevention of Pollution from Ships

7.3.1. SOURCE REDUCTION 
Source reduction is the process of eliminating or minimising as far as possible, the volume and/or toxicity 
of wastes through use of alternative materials and/or more efficient processes, practices or procedures, 
including e.g. 
 
 
Material elimination. 
 
Inventory control and management. 
 
Material substitution. 
 
Reduction in the consumption of natural resources. 
 
Process modification.  
 
Reduced consumption of non-degradable toxic material. 
 
Reuse of products and packaging. 
 
Use of more durable environmentally friendly products. 
 
Use of non-hazardous products for cleaning, operation and maintenance. 
 
Improved housekeeping 
 
Opportunities for reductions in waste volumes are often limited by the nature of the activity being carried

 
Use products supplied in reusable containers. 
 
Enter into return agreements with suppliers for return of unused   products and recyclable 
containers. 
 
On-site use of decanter/centrifuge to recover oil from tank bottom sludge

7.3.4. TREATMENT 
In situations where source reduction, reuse and recycling/recovery options have been fully explored and 
cannot be implemented feasibly, treatment of the waste must be considered as an alternative option to 
minimize waste volumes and/or toxicity. 
 
Treatment options depend largely on the waste characteristics, and are usually limited by technical and 
economic factors.  Environmental sensitivity of a location is also a major factor in determining the 
appropriate management practice for a specific waste.  This may require acquiring data on hydrogeology 
and biological/ecological communities. 
 
Potential treatment options to destroy, detoxify and/or neutralise waste residues include processes such 
as: 
 
 
Physical methods e.g. gravity separation, filtration, centrifugation, soil washing, solidification, 
stabilisation, encapsulation.   
 
Biological methods e.g. waste water treatment, composting, tank-based reactors, land farming via 
bio-remediation and/or photo-remediation. 
 
Thermal methods e.g. energy recovery via incinerators, destruction via incineration, burning, 
thermal desorption, detoxification.   
 
Chemical reaction e.g. neutralisation, stabilisation, extraction, precipitation.  
Further detail on some of the above options is provided in the following sections. 
 
The method of treatment selected depends on various factors, such as the nature of the waste, volume 
to be treated, availability of suitable facilities and cost.  Clean-up criteria and limits for discharges to land, 
surface and subsurface water bodies are provided in HSE-EN-ST02. 
 
The BeAAT facility at Ruwais includes a range of technologies for hazardous wastes treatment.  Group 
Companies should discuss potential treatment methods with the BeAAT site management. 
 
(a) 
Solidification, Stabilization and Encapsulation 
These techniques are used to convert wastes into another form, which is less reactive or less 
leachable.  The waste is not destroyed, but merely converted into a dry solid form.  Techniques 
used include cement based and pozzolanic processes, and chemical stabilisation.  These 
techniques are particularly effective for stabilisation of heavy metals, as the high pH of the cement 
mixtures renders the metals biologically unavailable.  However wastes with a high concentration 
of organics, salts or bentonite have been found to interfere with the stabilisation process and are 
not suitable for this technique.

(b) 
Composting 
This is a biological treatment technique similar to land treatment primarily for food, wood chips,

The use of the Waste converter to process garbage as a means to reduce the volume and weight

logistical requirements and the final disposal costs. The system functions by macerating the waste

(c) 
Bio treatment (bioremediation) 
Organic materials contained in wastes can be biodegraded using natural biological processes.  
However, natural biodegradation may be limited due to the arid and high saline conditions that 
exist within Abu Dhabi.  These limitations can be overcome by optimising the natural 
biological/environmental conditions.  This requires: 
 
 
An adequate supply of hydrocarbon degrading bacteria. 
 
Availability of sufficient oxygen (mixing and tilling) for cell metabolism. 
 
Availability and balance of nutrients and micro-nutrients necessary for optimum bacterial 
metabolism moisture control. 
 
Temperature and pH control. 
The type and concentration of wastes to be degraded will have an impact on the biodegradation 
process.  Some elements may be readily degraded at low concentrations; but can inhibit 
degradation at higher concentrations.  Additionally, some elements may degrade very slowly using 
biological processes and may require a period of acclimatisation or co-metabolites for degradation 
to occur. 
 
Generally, an organic waste should be analysed to determine the feasibility of bioremediation 
before starting the process.  High levels of asphalt and/or polynuclear aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) 
in a waste can make biological treatment a poor option for the rapid remediation of hydrocarbons.  
Conversely, high concentrations of alkanes in a waste will make it a good candidate for biological 
treatment.

bioremediation.  Organic streams with POPs or heavy metals above recognised environmental 
threshold levels must be consigned for controlled incineration instead.

7.3.5. DISPOSAL 
Disposal must be seen as the last resort and should be practiced only when the hierarchy of waste



 
Landfill. 
 
Fuel blending. 
 
Waste pits. 
Any disposal of waste must be in accordance with the requirements of this Standard; including the 
Duty of Care obligations. Group Companies must ensure that all hazardous wastes are sent for 
disposal to the BeAAT facility at Ruwais where they will be treated prior to final disposal. 
 
Further detail to some of the above options is provided in the sections below. 
 
(a) 
Deep Well Injection 
Many liquid wastes can be disposed of into Simsima and Um Radhuma formations or deeper 
reservoirs.  The waste disposal well must be in a reservoir formation which is geologically and 
mechanically isolated from usable supplies of water and must not contain commercial quantities of 
oil or gas.  This method of disposal is suitable for wastes such as produced water, blow down 
liquids, dehydration and sweetening waste liquids, and waste drilling fluids. All disposal to deep 
wells is subject to risk assessment. 
 
Waste liquids can also be disposed of into deep wells, or used effectively in liquid injection projects 
to boost reservoir pressures. 
 
(b) 
Surface Discharge 
Treated oil based and synthetic based drill mud cuttings can be disposed of via land spreading 
techniques. The oil content is restricted to less than 0.1% for land spreading techniques and is 
subject to an environmental risk assessment. 
 
Water Based Mud (WBM) cuttings are classified as Non-Hazardous waste based on the chemical

Landfills are specially engineered facilities used for bulk disposal of large volumes of waste.  
Landfills can be used for both hazardous and non-hazardous wastes, provided they are properly 
designed to contain leachate and cannot contaminate the environment.  Hazardous wastes that

different levels of containment, depending on the type of waste to be deposited in them and the 
potential environmental risks.  The actual levels of containment should be monitored throughout 
the life of the landfill so that these do not exceed the design levels.



 
An environmental risk assessment study is carried out on the proposed site of the landfill

 
The landfill site is properly managed and maintained throughout its life and a Competent

 
The site is securely fenced to prevent dangers to people or foraging animals. 


INTERNATIONAL SHIPMENT OF WASTE 
Hazardous and other waste must, as far as practically possible, be disposed of as close as possible to 
where it is produced (Proximity Principle).  Consistent with this principle, export of waste to other countries, 
whether for disposal, treatment or other activity must be only authorised in specific circumstances and 
shall be endorsed by ADNOC GHSE Function.  Where export of waste is considered to be the Best 
Practicable Environmental Option, Group Companies must ensure the shipment complies with all the 
requirements of Federal and Abu Dhabi authorities or Local Authorities and the conditions of the 
Transboundary Movement of Hazardous Waste stipulated in the Basel Convention [Ref. 2].  In particular, 
Group Companies must ensure that: 
 
 
Any Transboundary movement is conducted in a manner which will protect human health and the 
environment. 
 
Shipments do not take place to countries or neighbouring emirates which have prohibited the import 
of hazardous wastes, or where there is reason to believe that the waste will not be handled in a 
safe and environmentally sound manner. 
 
Receiving countries receive full information about the waste shipment, including effects on human 
health and the environment. 
 
No waste is exported to any area south of 60° South latitude. 
 
Any waste destined for export to another country is properly labelled and transported in accordance 
with generally recognised international rules and standards. 
 
All waste is accompanied by suitable waste transfer documentation covering the point of 
commencement of trans-boundary movement to the point of final disposal. 
 
Any waste exported from the UAE is managed in an environmentally sound manner at its country 
of destination. 
In some circumstances, it may be appropriate to return waste to the original supplier of the material for 
reprocessing/recycling.  In these circumstances, the return of waste to the supplier will still require 
adequate control and the preparation of waste transfer documentation, as described above.  Group 
Companies must obtain documentary evidence that the waste reached its intended final destination.  
As the waste producer, it is the Group Companies responsibility to be fully conversant with all the 
requirements of local and international laws/regulations/conventions relating to international transport of 
waste and to ensure adherence to the same at all times. 
 
In all cases of proposed international shipment of waste, Group Companies must first consult with 
ADNOC GHSE Function and provide the necessary assurance that all the requirements of UAE Federal 
Regulations and the Basel Convention will be complied with, including provision of documentary evidence 
to confirm compliance. In the absence of this assurance, international shipments of waste will not be 
permitted.

Produced Water 
Produced water is the normally largest waste stream by volume and the proportion of this waste varies with 
production. The volume of produced water increases as the hydrocarbon reserves become depleted. Produced 
water is contaminated with oil and other substances making its discharge to the marine environment generally 
unacceptable, without treatment. Produced water is therefore commonly injected into the formation via a deep 
well to enhance oil recovery.  
Drill Fluids and Cuttings 
Well drilling results in production of large quantities of waste drilling fluids and cuttings.  
Drilling fluids are generally categorized into two types: Water based Fluids (WBDF) and Non-Aqueous Drilling 
Fluids (NADF).  Diesel based fluids are also available. The solid medium used in most drilling fluids is barium 
sulphate, with bentonite clays being used as thickener.  In addition, drilling fluids contain a number of chemicals 
and the final composition depends on specific downhole formation conditions.  
Use of NADF and diesel based fluids at offshore is not permitted in UAE unless special provisions are granted 
by ADNOC. Spent WADF drilling fluids should, wherever possible be collected and reused.  
Where this is not possible, options for disposal include injection into a dedicated disposal well, injection into the 
annular space of a well or collection and disposal onshore.  
Where none of the above options is feasible/possible, direct discharge to sea may be the only alternative. In such 
cases, Group Companies must conduct an appropriate environmental risk assessment and prepare a cuttings 
and disposal plan which results in the minimum environmental impact.   
Pollution prevention and control measures to consider prior to discharge of spent drilling fluids and cuttings 
include: 
 
Minimizing environmental hazards relating to residual chemicals and additives through careful selection of 
drilling chemicals and restricting use to those exhibiting low toxicity, bioavailability and bioaccumulation 
potential. 
 
Use of high efficiency solids control equipment to reduce the need for fluid change and minimising the amount 
of residual fluid on drilled cuttings. 
 
Use slim-hole multilateral wells and coiled tubing drilling techniques, where feasible, to reduce the quantities 
of fluids and cuttings. 
 
Any discharges of spent fluids or cuttings to the sea must meet relevant UAE and international standards for 
discharge to the marine environment and be made via a caisson at least 15m below the surface. Details of 
relevant discharge standards are provided in the HSE-EN-ST02.
