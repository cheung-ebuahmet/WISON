1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy, 
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all persons who 
undertake manual handling operations are fully aware of the hazards involved and have the competence, 
training and awareness to undertake such activity. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
(i) 
To provide standard requirements on identifying hazards and assessing risks associated 
with manual handling activities in ADNOC operations. 
(ii) 
To recommend controls for reducing risk of potential injuries to personnel, for tasks, 
individuals’ capability, load, environment and other factors. 
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions at HQ, GCs and Affiliates) and its Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored. 
 
4. 
LAWS AND REGULATIONS 
The relevant UAE legislations applicable to this Standard includes but not limited to: 
 
(i) 
UAE Federal Law No. 8 (1980), Chapter V:  Safety, Protection, and the Health and Social 
Care of the Employees; Article (91). 
(ii) 
Federal Law No.: 24 of 1999 for the Protection and Development of the Environment. 
(iii) 
UAE Ministerial Order No. (32), 1982. Specifying Preventive Methods and Measures for 
Protecting Workers against Work Hazards. 
Group Companies shall ensure that their activities comply with all relevant Federal and Abu Dhabi 
laws and regulations at all times, including any that may be introduced after the publication of 
this Standard.

7. 
REQUIREMENTS  
ADNOC Group operations involve various activities which require employees to perform manual handling 
or lifting. The general perception about a manual handling is that it is a low risk activity, as many manual 
handling problems involve small loads. 
 
7.1. 
HAZARDOUS MANUAL HANDLING 
Manual handling activities can be hazardous and can lead to minor injuries and/or Musculoskeletal 
Disorder (MSD). These activities typically require a person to lift, lower, push, pull, carry, hold or restrain 
an object, load or a person and may involve one or more of the following:  
 
(i) 
Repetitive or sustained force; 
(ii) 
High or sudden force; 
(iii) 
Repetitive movement; and 
(iv) 
Sustained or awkward posture. 
7.2. 
TYPES OF MANUAL HANDLING INJURIES 
Manual handling, in severe cases, can result in fatigue and can result in injuries of back, neck, arms, 
shoulders and other body parts participating in manual handling task. Following are the two (02) 
categories of injuries that can result from manual handling: 
 
(i) 
Minor Injuries 
(ii) 
Musculoskeletal Disorder (MSD) 
7.2.1. MINOR INJURIES 
This includes injuries due to sudden, unexpected events: 
 
(i) 
Cuts, bruises and abrasions, mostly involving the upper body and limbs; 
(ii) 
Fractures, either as a result of falls or dropping heavy objects onto the body; and 
(iii) 
Strains and muscle injuries involving the spine. 
7.2.2. MSD 
MSD includes damage to the musculoskeletal system of the body including muscles, tendons, ligaments, 
bones, joints, bursa, blood vessels and nerves, due to gradual and cumulative wear and tear through 
repetitive manual handling. MSDs can be divided into three (03) types:

(i) 
Upper Limb Disorders (ULDs) that affect the arms, from fingers to shoulder, and neck. They 
are often called Repetitive Strain Injuries (RSI), Cumulative Trauma Disorder (CTD) or 
Occupational Overuse Syndrome (OOS). 
ULDs are common both in the workplace and elsewhere and can result from various kinds 
of work. They can be associated with the use of Display Screen Equipment (DSE) or work 
using vibrating tools. 
(ii) 
Lower Limb Disorders (LLDs) at work affect the hips, knees and legs and usually happen 
because of overuse and workers may report lower limb pain, aching and numbness without 
a specific disease being identified. 
Acute injury caused by a violent impact or extreme force is unlikely. However, athletes and 
military personnel are more prone to these injuries. 
(iii) 
Back pain and back injuries can be due to many causes, but without exception will be 
aggravated by poor posture, inadequate support and poor working practice - bad 
ergonomics. It is important to maintain the correct spinal posture (the S-curve).  
ADNOC Occupational Health Hazards Standard [Ref. 1] provides detailed information on the occupational 
health aspects of prolonged exposure to hazardous manual handling activities and other factors that 
could have a relation with manual handling issues, such as ergonomics, vibration, heat stress, lighting, 
etc. 
 
7.3. 
HAZARD IDENTIFICATION, RISK ASSESSMENT AND CONTROLS 
7.3.1. HAZARD IDENTIFICATION 
A suitable hazard identification shall be based on a thorough practical understanding of hazards 
developed through employee engagement/input or questionnaire, observations of tasks, review of 
available data etc. It shall account for the factors involving Task, Individual, Load, Environment and Other 
factors (TILEO) as described below: 
 
(i) 
Type of manual handling tasks to be performed 
(ii) 
Individual capabilities 
(iii) 
Loads to be handled 
(iv) 
Environment in which the task will be carried out 
(v) 
Other factors 
7.3.2. RISK ASSESSMENT 
The key element is that manual handling hazards are identified, and the associated risks assessed, to 
put in place appropriate control measures or strategies. In general, the key steps in the risk assessment 
for manual handling shall:  
 
(i) 
Identify the hazards associated with TILEO factors and assess the risks; 
(ii) 
Identify the control measures that are currently in place; and 
(iii) 
Implement additional controls to manage the risk. 
(a) 
Qualitative Assessment 
As a minimum, a qualitative risk assessment shall be conducted that involves examining the 
characteristics of manual handling activity in more detail to assess whether the forces, movements 
and postures are undertaken in such a way that they give rise to the risk of MSD.

7.3.4. RISK ASSOCIATED WITH TASKS AND CONTROLS 
(a) 
Large Vertical Movement of Load  
The ideal height for handling a load is around waist. Lifting or lowering a load above or below this 
point requires greater physical effort and increases the risk of injury. 
 
Where a load has to be lifted or lowered through a wide height range it will normally need to be 
handled outside this preferred zone. In this case the weight of the load shall be limited to that which 
can safely be handled at the least favourable height.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Avoid significant variation in the height of storage or working surfaces; 
(ii) 
Dedicated storage areas for easier movement of loads; 
(iii) 
Where practicable, store heavier objects around waist height, with space above or below 
this level being used for lighter or more easily handled items; 
(iv) 
Hold the load closer to body where lifting of loads from floor level is unavoidable, to allow 
the stronger leg muscles to be used in lifting instead of back. Factors that can affect such 
manual lifting include: the size of the load, obstacles on the floor, lifting from within deep bins 
or poor stance; and 
(v) 
Team lifting, if necessary, to place heavier items into their storage location if they have to be 
lifted from the floor. 
(b) 
Holding Object at a Distance  
When an object is held away from the body, stress on the lower back increases substantially. As a 
rough guide, the stress on the lower back is about five times as much when a load is held at arm's 
length than when it is held close to the body. The friction of a load against the worker's clothing 
also helps to support and steady the load when it is close to the body. 
 
Greater loads are imposed on the back, shoulders and arms when a load is handled with arms 
outstretched. As a result, there is a greater risk of injury when load is handled while reaching 
upwards, particularly if the stretching is prolonged or repetitive. Control of the load is also more 
difficult when the arms are outstretched. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to any changes that can be made to the task, 
which allow the load to be held closer to the handler's body. 
  
(c) 
Twisting/Bending  
Twisting/bending while carrying the load increases the force on the lower back even if it is done by 
bending the back, or by leaning forward with the back straight. In both cases, the weight of the 
trunk (body) is added to the weight of the load being lifted. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Avoid lifting loads from the floor when seated; 
(ii) 
Avoid the need for twisting during handling by using swivel chairs; 
(iii) 
Reduce the load; 
(iv) 
Storage of materials in a way to avoid heavy or awkward loads to be lifted from the ground; 
and

(v) 
Consider the risk of a moving chair while handling load. Avoid use of a wheeled chair for 
work involving manual handling, particularly on hard floors. 
(d) 
Unpredictable Movement of Loads  
If there is a risk of a load suddenly becoming free (such as when pulling an object that is stuck to 
release it) or moving unpredictably during handling, the handler is at greater risk of injury. This risk 
is increased if the handler's posture is unsuitable.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the use of mechanical aids e.g., trolleys or rest 
tables to safely handle unpredictable movements of loads. 
 
(e) 
Repetitive Movements  
Frequent or prolonged physical effort can give rise to injury, even if the load itself is not particularly 
heavy. Often, repetitive movements are combined with twisting or stooping which increases the 
risk of injury.  
Furthermore, insufficient rest or recovery period will lead to the development of physical and mental 
fatigue. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Effective job rotation in avoiding the onset of fatigue as a result of prolonged use of a 
particular group of muscles; 
(ii) 
Where possible, adopt a flexible approach to timing of work break; and 
(iii) 
Regular short breaks in the work to avoid fatigue than infrequent longer breaks. 
(f) 
Long Carrying Distances  
If a load has to be carried more than about 10 meters, the effort involved in carrying the load is 
likely to predominate over that of lifting it. This will often be the limiting factor in deciding whether 
the load should reasonably be handled manually.  
 
Pushing or pulling can place the handler at risk of injury particularly if it is carried out with the hands 
below knuckle height, above shoulder height or if the action is jerky. The condition of the floor and 
the type of shoes worn by the handler should also be considered, as the risk of slipping can be 
significant. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Use transport aids such as trolleys or barrows; and 
(ii) 
Level the height of the trolley as that of work surfaces from which the load is required to be 
moved. 
7.3.5. RISK ASSOCIATED WITH INDIVIDUAL CAPACITY AND CONTROLS 
The individual capability of the person carrying out the manual handling task shall also be taken into 
consideration.  
 
(a) 
Job Requiring Physical Capability or Strength  
The nature of the job normally carried out by the employee shall also be considered before manual 
handling. A job that is routine for a worker involved in heavy physical work may not be reasonable 
for an office worker not used to that type of activity.

Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Consider age requirements before assigning/performing the manual handling task. Physical 
capability for safe lifting generally reaches a peak in the early 20's, declining gradually during 
the 40's and more markedly thereafter. This may be compensated for, to some extent, by 
greater experience and maturity in older employees. It is generally accepted, that the risk of 
injury during manual handling is greater for those in their teens and in their 50's and 60's 
than for those in their mid-years. 
(ii) 
Use of mechanical aids if necessary. 
(b) 
Individuals with Health Issues  
An individual may have a health issue which could increase the risk associated with manual 
handling work, this should be taken into account in the risk assessment. Such health issues include 
back issue and heart conditions in addition to others that may not be so obviously related. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
  
(i) 
Relocate such individual to an appropriate work area or re-design his current task. 
(ii) 
Consider the suitability of clothing or PPE being worn by the person carrying out the manual 
handling task as clothing should be loose enough to allow free movement but have no parts 
which might snag on the load. This shall be taken into account when making an assessment 
of a manual handling task. The effectiveness of PPE shall not be compromised to make the 
manual handling work easier. 
(iii) 
Use of mechanical aids if necessary. 
7.3.6. RISK ASSOCIATED WITH LOAD AND CONTROLS 
The risk is increased in any manual handling operation if the load has any of the following properties:  
 
(a) 
Heavy Load  
The weight of a load presents a significant risk of injury during manual handling.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Break the load down into smaller packages if this is possible; 
(ii) 
Order materials in a smaller container size if possible; 
(iii) 
Use mechanical assistance; and 
(iv) 
Consider the effect of the frequency of handling because sometimes increased frequency of 
manual handling task can outweigh the benefits of a lighter load. 
Appendix 5 shall be followed for the load limitations for men and women with regards to safe 
manual lifting [Ref. 5]. 
 
(b) 
Bulky Loads  
If load is awkward to handle, there is an increased risk of dropping it, and often adopting the most 
favourable posture for handling of the load is not feasible.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Break the load down into smaller manageable parts if this is possible; and 
(ii) 
Order materials in a way that makes manual handling easier.

(c) 
Difficulty in Grasp  
Loads may be difficult to grasp because, for example, they are wet, slippery or rounded, which can 
pose the risk of dropping the load and associated injury or additional stress on the back. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Eliminate these problems at source if possible; 
(ii) 
Place the load in a container or sling to alleviate the problem; and 
(iii) 
Use suitable PPE such as gloves or handling aids. (e.g. hooks, clamping devices, handles 
etc.) 
(d) 
Unstable Loads or Shifting Contents  
A load may be unstable because it is not rigid, or because the contents are movable and alter the 
center of gravity. Such additional difficulties are likely to have an effect on the shifting of weight of 
the load that may safely be handled.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Minimize the risk of unstable load by effective packaging of objects that are movable during 
handling; 
(ii) 
Use of slings for such packages; 
(iii) 
Minimize the amount of free space in package for containers holding liquids; and 
(iv) 
Use specialized handling aids, combined with effective training.  
(e) 
Intrinsically Harmful Loads  
The load may have characteristics that make it intrinsically harmful. This can include sharp edges, 
rough, hot or cold surfaces. These characteristics may discourage proper grip and safe handling 
techniques and can likely cause an injury.  
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Avoid sharp edges by effective packaging of objects, where possible; 
(ii) 
Place hot or cold load in an insulated container if allowing to reach a reasonable temperature 
before handling is not possible; and 
(iii) 
Use PPE such as protective gloves for handling. 
7.3.7.  RISK ASSOCIATED WITH ENVIRONMENT AND CONTROLS 
The risk is increased in any manual handling operation if the working environment is in any way hostile 
or imposes constraints for example:  
 
(a) 
Insufficient Space  
This includes space constraints (such as, restricted head room) and other obstructions that hinder 
an efficient working posture during manual handling. Furniture, narrow gangways or other 
obstructions can all make handling of loads, without excessive bending or twisting, more difficult.

Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Ensure that the immediate working space and the route to be used during movement of the 
load is clear when planning manual handling; and 
(ii) 
Maintain a good standard of housekeeping to limit the insufficient space related risks. 
(b) 
Uneven, Slippery or Unstable Floor  
Uneven floors will increase the risk of tripping as well as increasing the risk of slipping while pushing 
or pulling and associated risk of injury. Torn or damaged floor coverings can produce similar risks. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Keep the ground surface even and firm where routine handling of loads is to be carried out; 
and  
(ii) 
Clear up the spillages of water, oil, soap, food scraps or other materials immediately.  
(c) 
Variation in Level  
It is often not possible to avoid the movement of materials among different levels within a building 
which increases the risk of injury in manual handling. Carrying materials up or down steps can 
increase the risk of injury. Handling of loads on a ladder is particularly hazardous, since the need 
to maintain a good hold on the ladder may impair proper grip of the load. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Teamwork shall be acquired where heavy or difficult loads are required to be moved among 
different levels and a good lift is not possible; and 
(ii) 
Slide a load rather than lifting it where possible. 
(d) 
Extreme Working Conditions  
Working in extreme working conditions (high/low temperatures, humidity, high wind, dust etc.) can 
increase the risk of injury through rapid fatigue, loss of manual dexterity and impairment of grip 
through perspiration on the hands.  
 
Sudden gusts of wind can make large loads difficult to handle safely, particularly if they are light in weight. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Consider discontinuing the work if weather conditions become unsuitable where work is 
performed externally; 
(ii) 
Maintain comfortable working environments where possible; 
(iii) 
Take into account the factors of heat/cold, humidity, wind and dust while planning for manual 
handling; and 
(iv) 
Use handling aids or team handling to reduce the risks.  
(e) 
Poor Lighting  
Poor lighting can increase the risks of tripping and bumping against unseen obstructions. Poor 
posture may need to be adopted when lifting, as an individual tries to guard against these perceived 
dangers.

7.3.8. OTHER RISK FACTORS AND CONTROLS 
(a) 
Pregnant Women  
Pregnancy has significant implications and manual handling can pose greater risk. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
The pregnant woman shall not be allowed to handle/lift any heavy loads; and 
(ii) 
Manual handling activity plan shall identify the factors such as magnitude of the load, 
distance to be travelled and physical fitness of the pregnant woman. 
(b) 
Team Lifting/Handling  
Where an object is too large or heavy, safe handling may perhaps be accomplished by two or more 
people working together. This, in itself, introduces potential risk. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Discuss the task among the team members before attempting the operation; 
(ii) 
Coordinate closely during the task so that team members do not hinder one another. The 
way that the weight is lifted by an individual team member may vary during the task (e.g. 
when using stairs); and 
(iii) 
Nominate one person to direct the manual handling task. 
(c) 
Handling Aids and Equipment  
Although manual handling aids and equipment are recommended to reduce the risk associated 
with manual handling activities, it can cause additional risks if those are inappropriate for the job, 
not in a good working condition or lack the safety features. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Identify and use the correct type of equipment; 
(ii) 
Provide equipment that is more suitable for the task; 
(iii) 
Ensure that planned preventive maintenance has been carried out; 
(iv) 
Consider changing the wheels/tyres and flooring etc. if those are not smoothly rolling; 
(v) 
Ensure handles are in good working condition and provide firm grip; and 
(vi) 
Ensure that brakes are easier to use, reliable and effective. 
(d) 
Work Organization Factors  
Work organization factor can also influence adversely the conduct of manual handling tasks. Such 
factors include nature of work e.g. repetitive, boring, workload, deadlines, miscommunication. 
 
Typical control measures include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
Change the task to reduce monotony;

APPENDIX 2 
QUANTITATIVE MANUAL HANDLING ASSESSMENT BY NIOSH LIFTING EQUATION 
1. 
Introduction 
The NIOSH Lifting Equation is a tool that can be used to assess the manual handling risks associated with 
lifting and lowering tasks in the workplace. This equation considers job task variables to determine safe 
lifting practices and guidelines. 
The primary product of the NIOSH Lifting Equation is the Recommended Weight Limit (RWL), which 
defines the maximum acceptable weight (load) that nearly all healthy employees could lift over the course 
of an 8 hour shift without increasing the risk of MSD to the lower back. In addition, a Lifting Index (LI) is 
calculated to provide a relative estimate of the level of physical stress and MSD risks associated with the 
manual lifting tasks evaluated. 
2. 
NIOSH Lifting Equation  
LC (51) × HM × VM × DM × AM × FM × CM = RWL 
Where; 
LC = 51 pounds or ~23kg, which represents the maximum recommended load weight to be lifted under 
ideal conditions. 
M = Multiplier, that serves to decrease the load constant and calculate the RWL for that particular lifting 
task. 
H = Horizontal location of the object relative to the body 
V = Vertical location of the object relative to the floor 
D = Distance the object is moved vertically 
A = Asymmetry angle or twisting requirement 
F = Frequency and duration of lifting activity 
C = Coupling or quality of the workers grip on the object 
Lifting Index (LI): Weight ÷ RWL  
3. 
NIOSH Lifting Equation Outputs 
(i) 
RWL, which answers the question: “Is this weight too heavy for the task?” 
(ii) 
LI, which answers the question “How significant is the risk?” 
LI < 1.0 indicates a nominal risk to healthy employees. 
LI = 1.0 or more indicates that the task is high risk for some fraction of the population.  
As the LI increases, the level of lower back injury risk increases correspondingly. Therefore, the goal shall 
be to design all lifting jobs to accomplish an LI of less than 1.0. 
(i) 
The Frequency-Independent Recommended Weight Limit (FIRWL) and the Frequency-
Independent Lifting Index (FILI) are additional outputs of the NIOSH lifting calculator. The 
FIRWL is calculated by using a frequency multiplier (FI) of 1.0 along with the other task 
variable multipliers. This effectively removes frequency as a variable, reflecting a weight limit 
for a single repetition of that task and allows equal comparison to other single repetition

Step 1: Determine Task Variables Needed 
The evaluator should prepare by interviewing and observing workers to gain a complete understanding of 
all required lifting tasks. Selection of the lifting tasks to be evaluated should be based on the most significant 
and demanding manual material handling tasks. If the job requires a wide variety of lifting tasks, a multi-
task evaluation can be performed using a composite of all single-task lifting assessments performed. More 
on that later, but for now let’s focus on single-task assessments. 
To determine the task variables needed: Gather information, interview supervisors, group leaders, and 
workers to determine the origin (start position) and the destination (ending position) of the lifting or lowering 
task, and whether significant control of the object being lifted or lowered is required at the destination of 
the lift. Significant control is a condition of the task that requires precision placement of the load at the 
destination of the lift. For example, when an object is fragile and careful placement is needed to protect the 
object from damage; or, the worker needs to change grip or hold or guide the object at the lifting or lowering 
destination. 
If any of these conditions exist, significant control at the destination is required. You will then need to collect 
all other task variable data at the destination (in addition to the origin) to accurately determine the RWL. If 
significant control is not required at the destination, the only measurement you will need at the destination 
is the vertical location (V) of the hands to determine the vertical travel distance (D) from the origin of the 
lifting task.

SAFE MANUAL HANDLING/LIFTING TECHNIQUE 
1. 
Think before Lifting/Handling 
Plan the lift. Are handling aids/equipment required? What is the target location where the load will be placed? 
Will help be needed with the load? Remove obstructions on the way such as discarded wrapping materials. 
For a long lift, consider resting the load midway on a table or bench to change the grip. 
 
Illustration Purpose only – Image Courtesy HSE UK [Ref. 5] 
 
2. 
Adopt a Stable Position 
The feet should be apart with one leg slightly forward to maintain balance (alongside the load, if it is on the 
ground). The handler should be prepared to move the feet during the lift to maintain stability. Avoid tight 
clothing or unsuitable footwear, which may make this difficult. 
 
Illustration Purpose only – Image Courtesy HSE UK [Ref. 5] 
 
3. 
Get a Good Hold 
Where possible, the load should be kept as close as possible to the body. This may be better than gripping 
it tightly with hands only. 
 
4. 
Start in a Good Posture 
At the start of the lift, slight bending of the back, hips and knees is preferable to fully flexing the back 
(stooping) or fully flexing the hips and knees (squatting). 
 
5. 
Don’t flex the back any further while lifting 
This can happen if the legs begin to straighten before starting to raise the load.

APPENDIX 4 
GOOD HANDLING TECHNIQUE FOR PUSHING AND PULLING  
1. 
Handling Devices 
Aids for pushing and pulling of loads, such as barrows and trolleys, should have handle heights that are 
between the shoulder and waist. Devices should be well maintained with wheels running smoothly. For 
new equipment, ensure that they are good quality. 
1. 
Force 
As a rough guide, the amount of force that needs to be applied to move a load over a flat, level surface 
using a well-maintained handling aid is at least 2% of the load weight. For example, if the load weight is 
200 kg, then the force needed to move the load is 4 kg. If the wheels are not in right position and good 
working condition, the force needed will be much larger. The workers should try to push rather than pull 
when moving a load, provided they can see over it and control steering and stopping. 
2. 
Slopes 
Workers should get help from another worker whenever necessary, if they have to negotiate a slope or 
ramp, as pushing and pulling forces can be very high. For example, if a load of 400 kg is moved up a slope 
of about 5°, the required force is over 30 kg even if wheels are good and surface is smooth. 
3. 
Uneven Surfaces 
Moving an object over soft or uneven surfaces requires higher forces. On an uneven surface, the force 
needed to start the load moving could increase to 10% of the load weight. Soft ground may be even worse 
such as sand. 
4. 
Stance and Pace 
To make it easier to push or pull, workers should keep their feet well away from the load and go no faster 
than walking speed. This will stop them becoming too tired too quickly.
