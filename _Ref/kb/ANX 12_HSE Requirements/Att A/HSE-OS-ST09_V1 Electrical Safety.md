1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy, 
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all personnel who 
work on or near electrical equipment, are fully aware of the hazards involved and have the competence, 
training and awareness to undertake such activities. 
 
Electrical risks are risks of death, electric shock or other injury caused directly or indirectly by electricity. 
The most common electrical risk and causes of injury are: 
 
(i) 
Electric shock causing injury or death. The electric shock may be received by direct or 
indirect contact, tracking through or across a medium, or by arcing; 
(ii) 
Arcing, explosion or fire causing burns. The injuries are often suffered because arcing or 
explosion or both occur when high fault currents are present;  
(iii) 
Electric shock from ‘step-and-touch’ potentials. 
2. 
PURPOSE 
The purpose of this standard is to provide an overall understanding of the activities performed on or near 
electrical equipment and its application within the ADNOC Group. This standard establishes a consistent 
safety guideline to the personnel involved in such activities within the ADNOC Group. 
 
All works related to the activities carried out for ADNOC Group shall be in accordance with the 
requirements of this Standard. 
 
3. 
SCOPE 
This Standard stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors. 
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored. 
 
4. 
LAWS AND REGULATIONS 
The relevant UAE legislations applicable to this Standard includes but not limited to: 
 
(i) 
UAE Federal Law No. 8 (1980), Chapter V:  Safety, Protection, and the Health and Social 
Care of the Employees; Article (91). 
(ii) 
Federal Law No.: 24 of 1999 for the Protection and Development of the Environment. 
(iii) 
UAE Ministerial Order No. (32), 1982. Specifying Preventive Methods and Measures for 
Protecting Workers against Work Hazards. 
ADNOC Group shall ensure that their activities comply with all relevant Federal and Abu Dhabi 
laws and regulations at all times, including any that may be introduced after the publication of 
this Standard.

7.1. 
OVERVIEW 
Electricity has long been recognized as a serious workplace hazards, exposing employees to electric 
shock, electrocution, burns, fires, and explosions. It is well known that human body conducts electricity, 
even at levels as low as 3 milliamperes can also cause injuries in which involuntary muscular reaction 
from the electric shock can cause bruises, bone fractures and even death resulting from collisions or falls.

7.2. 
HAZARDS ASSOCIATED WITH ELECTRICITY 
The primary hazards associated with electricity are as follows: 
 
(i) 
Electric Shock: resulting from current flowing through the body interfering with muscle and 
central nervous functions; 
(ii) 
Electrical Burns: resulting from the heating effect of the current which burns the body tissue; 
(iii) 
Fire: caused by overheating and damage of insulation which can be either due to over 
current or short circuiting; 
(iv) 
Explosion: caused by sparks in a flammable atmosphere; 
(v) 
Arc Flash: Arc flash results from an arcing fault, where the electric arcs and resulting 
radiation and shrapnel cause severe skin burns, hearing damage, and eye injuries; 
(vi) 
Electric Field: Strong radiation can induce current capable of delivering an electric shock to 
persons. 
7.2.1. ELECTRIC SHOCK 
Electrical injury occurs when an electric current runs through a portion of the body, usually from either a 
man-made source or lightning. The outside of the person’s body may appear to have only minor injuries, 
but internal injuries may still be significant. As current enters the body (source), it causes surface to deep 
burns, damages muscle and organs as it passes through the body, and eventually exits at another distant 
point (ground), which causes a second burn or wound. The electrical current may trigger irregular 
heartbeat or stop the heart entirely.

Among man-made sources, Direct Current (DC) tends to throw people from the source after one shock. 
Alternating Current (AC) is more dangerous. AC causes muscle spasms that often prolong contact with 
the power source, which increases the extent of the injury. 
 
7.2.2. ELECTRIC BURNS 
(a) 
Direct 
When an electrical current passes through the human body, it heats the tissue along the length of 
the current flow. This can result in deep burns that often require major surgery and are permanently 
disabling. Burns are more common with higher voltages but may occur from domestic electricity 
supplies if the current flows for more than a few fractions of a second. 
 
(b) 
Indirect 
If while working on live equipment, the system is short-circuited by, for example, an un-insulated 
spanner touching live conductors this will result in a large and sudden current flowing through the 
spanner. This rapid discharge of energy that follows contact with high voltages not only causes the 
rapid melting of the spanner but does so with such violent force that molten particles of metal are 
thrown off with huge velocity. Serious burns can occur if this molten metal contacts people in close 
proximity to the spanner. It is not necessary to have high voltages to melt a spanner in this way, it 
can also occur with batteries with sufficient stored energy such as those used on a forklift truck or 
in an Uninterruptable Power Supply (UPS). There are many experiences of workers suffering injury 
in this way after a spanner falls out of their overall top pocket and lands across the battery terminals. 
 
7.2.3. FIRE 
The principle causes of electrical fires are: 
 
(i) 
Wiring with defects such as insulation failure due to age or poor maintenance; 
(ii) 
Overheating of cables or other electrical equipment through overloading with currents above 
their design capacity; 
(iii) 
Incorrect electrical protection selection or settings; 
(iv) 
Poor connections due to lack of maintenance or unskilled personnel; 
(v) 
Poorly maintained or defective motors, heating and lighting; 
(vi) 
Flammable materials covering electrical equipment which may become hot in normal 
operation. 
7.2.4. ARC EXPLOSION 
The pressure of an arc blast is caused by the expansion of the metal as it vaporizes and the heating of 
the air by the arc energy. This accounts for the expulsion of molten metal up to 10 feet away. In addition, 
the sudden expansion of an arc blast creates loud sounds that can cause hearing damage

7.3.3. RISK ASSESSMENT 
A risk assessment shall be performed prior to the commencement of the activity to determine the 
measures to be taken to implement the control of risk. The risk assessment helps to determine: 
 
(i) 
The severity of an electrical risk; 
(ii) 
Identify the personnel at risk of exposure; 
(iii) 
Determine the sources and processes causing the risk; 
(iv) 
Identify that proper control measures shall be implemented; and 
(v) 
Assess the effectiveness of existing control measures.  
The likelihood of each hazard actually causing harm in a specific situation shall be assessed. As minimum, 
the following shall be used for the assessment: 
 
(i) 
What is the potential impact of the hazard? 
1. 
How severe could the electrical hazards be? For example, direct contact causing 
electrocution, fire or explosion causing burns or death. 
2. 
How many people are exposed to the hazard? 
(ii) 
How likely is the hazard to cause harm? 
1. 
Could it happen any time or would it be a rare event? 
2. 
How often and for how long will exposure to the hazard occur? 
3. 
If exposed to the hazard, how severe will the outcome be based on Risk Assessment 
Matrix? 
Other factors that may affect consequence and likelihood include but not limited to the following: 
 
(i) 
The conditions under which the electrical equipment is used, for example wet conditions 
outdoors or confined spaces; 
(ii) 
Work practices and procedures, for example isolation, to carry out maintenance; 
(iii) 
The capability, skill and experience of relevant workers.

Figure 7.3.2: Hierarchy of Controls 
(a) 
Elimination 
This means removing the hazard or hazardous work practice from the workplace. This is the most 
effective control measure and shall always be considered before anything else. For example, 
conductors and circuit parts in an electrically safe working condition. 
 
If eliminating the risk is not reasonably practicable, then substitution, isolation or engineering 
controls, or a combination of these control measures to minimize the risk shall be considered. 
 
(b) 
Minimization 
(i) 
Substitution: Minimize the risk by substituting or replacing a hazard or hazardous work 
practice with a safer one; for example use of extra low voltage equipment such as battery-
operated tool rather than a tool that is plugged into mains electricity. 
(ii) 
Isolate/Separate: Minimize the risk by isolating or separating the hazard or hazardous work 
practice from personnel; for example, lock out and tag out the source of electrical energy 
during operations or maintenance functions, erect a physical barrier around the work area, 
restrict access to electrical switchboards and electrical equipment rooms. 
(iii) 
Engineering: Engineering controls are physical control measures to minimize risk; for 
example, installing residual current devices to reduce the risk of receiving a fatal electric 
shock. 
(iv) 
Organization/Procedures: This shall only be considered when other higher order control 
measures are not reasonably practicable, or to increase protection from the hazard. These 
are work methods or procedures designed to minimize the exposure to a hazard, for example 
ban the use of electrical equipment during rain, tag and test electrical equipment. 
(v) 
Personal Protective Equipment: PPE is the lowest order control measure in the hierarchy of 
controls. PPE shall only be considered when other higher order control measures are not 
reasonably practicable or to increase protection from the hazard. A minimum of the following 
PPE shall be ensured:  
1. 
Insulating Gloves; 
2.

7.4. 
GENERAL SAFETY REQUIREMENTS 
7.4.1. ELECTRICAL EQUIPMENT SELECTION AND OPERATION 
Equipment shall be properly designed, constructed, installed and maintained so that it does not present 
a risk of electric shock, burns, fire or explosion when properly used. BS 7671 [Ref. 3] describes how 
systems and equipment can be designed, constructed and installed so that they can be used safely. 
 
Some equipment operates at voltages that are so low that they cannot cause a harmful electric shock but 
even at these extra-low voltages an arc can occur, burns can result from overheated conductors, or an 
explosive atmosphere may be ignited. A short-circuited car battery, for example, may cause the 
conductors to overheat and even cause the battery to explode. 
 
The selection shall be suitable for the environment in which it is used, for example cables and equipment 
in heavy industries such as sheet metal works need to be protected against mechanical damage. The 
adverse environmental factors shall also be considered when working on equipment. For example, 
excessively damp or humid conditions will increase the risk of injury because of reduced effectiveness of 
insulation, which may undermine the effectiveness of devices used for isolation, or increase the severity 
should an electric shock occur. Equipment that has corroded may not function as intended. 
 
Certified explosion-protected equipment shall be used in places where there could be potentially 
explosive atmospheres, for example if there has been a leak of flammable gas or build-up of combustible 
dust that could be ignited by an electric spark. 
 
Careful design and selection of electrical equipment shall be considered. For example: 
 
(i) 
Switch disconnectors shall have a locking-off facility or other means of securing them in the 
OFF position; 
(ii) 
Circuits and equipment shall be installed so that all sections of the system can be isolated 
as necessary; 
(iii) 
Switch disconnectors shall be suitably located and arranged so that circuits and equipment 
can be isolated without disconnecting other circuits that are required to continue in service; 
(iv) 
Devices used for isolating circuits shall be clearly marked to show their relationship to the 
equipment they control, unless there could be no doubt that this would be obvious to anyone 
who may need to operate them.

7.4.5. MOBILE GENERATOR SETS 
Mobile generator sets may be required when no suitable permanent mains supply is available. The frame 
and neutral of mobile generator sets shall be connected to earth. The maximum acceptable resistance to 
earth is 4 ohms. 
 
Mobile generator sets shall be provided with over-current devices to protect the distribution switchboard 
and connecting cables. In addition, mobile generator sets with ratings of 50kVA and above shall be 
provided with earth fault protection devices. For large mobile generator sets the protection devices may 
be located in the distribution switchboard; for small units they shall be located on the generator skid. 
Where the protection devices are located at the distribution switchboard, the cables from the generator 
to the switchboard shall be mechanically protected and shall be as short as possible. 
 
Mobile generators sets shall be provided with a means of isolating the generator supply from the 
distribution system. The means of isolation may be installed either at the generator, or at the point where 
the incoming generator supply connects to the distribution switchboard. 
 
7.4.6. WELDING 
Electric welding machines shall be the DC type. AC welding machines may only be used prior to written 
approval. A return cable shall always be connected between the workpiece and the welding machine, 
with the connection to the workpiece being, clean, free from rust and paint and as close as possible to 
the point of work. The return cable shall be an integral part of the machine. Steel structures and pipelines 
shall not be used as return paths. 
 
As a minimum the following precautions shall be considered: 
 
(i) 
The welding machine shall be installed as close as possible to the workpiece; 
(ii) 
The welding machine casing earth terminal shall be connected to the local earth; 
(iii) 
The welding machine frequency generator shall not be operated within 15m of 
communication and/or instrument circuits. Where this is not possible, the operator of the 
facility should be advised of the potential for interference within the circuits and shall agree 
to the placement of the equipment. 
When welding is being performed on a structure or section of piping, the CP shall be isolated and a 
continuity bond shall be provided across the point to be welded. The continuity bond may be omitted if

All the activities related to welding shall be performed in line with ADNOC Cutting, Welding and Allied 
Processes Standard [Ref. 28]

7.4.10. LEADS & EXTENSION CORDS 
Leads and extension cords used outdoors or in industrial environments shall be rubber insulated and

7.4.12. LIGHTNING PROTECTION 
A lightning strike to a steel object will act to raise the potential of the object to a level that may be 
dangerous to personnel. To avoid danger to personnel all large and prominent masses of steelwork shall 
be connected to earth in accordance with BS EN 62305 Protection Against Lightning, parts 1 to 4 [Ref.

Permanent installations such as buildings, vessels, and tanks will generally be protected against lightning

Earth electrodes used in lightning protection systems shall be located away from areas likely to be 
occupied by personnel during lightning activity (e.g. entrances, exits, sheltered areas).

7.5.7. LIVE LINE WASHING PRACTICES 
Portable LL washing equipment are used for cleaning insulators on overhead line circuits and substation 
compounds. The portable washing equipment used for cleaning polluted insulators supporting LIVE HV 
conductors, are dependent on three main factors that determine if the LL washing procedure can be used 
safely which are: 
 
(i) 
Nozzle design, size and type; 
(ii) 
Water pressure available at nozzle; and  
(iii) 
The conductivity of the water to be used for the washing. 
When considering these three factors it is important to be aware that the safety of the person operating 
the portable washing equipment depends on: 
 
(i) 
Avoid large leakage currents in the water jet between the LIVE HV conductors and the nozzle 
the operator is holding; 
(ii) 
Avoiding large leakage currents down the LIVE conductor’s insulator stack which shall avoid 
an electrical flashover near to the operator 
(a) 
Water Pressure at the Nozzle 
The nozzle used can be of several different designs, or size, but it shall be capable of breaking up 
the water into droplets before the water jet comes into contact with an insulator carrying a LIVE 
conductor. Whatever type of nozzle is used for portable LL WASHING equipment, it shall be 
effectively connected to Earth to protect the operator. 
 
This pressure depends on the length of the jet of water required to effectively wash a polluted 
insulator, whilst still allowing the operator to remain at a safe distance from any LIVE HV conductor. 
This safe distance depends on the clearance required from the LIVE conductors at a particular 
system voltage, and the need to keep any leakage current, down the water jet to the nozzle, under 
1mA (the 'threshold of perception'). 
 
The design of the portable washing equipment shall allow for the maximum water pressure that 
can be produced by its pump when fitted with a particular design, and size of nozzle, and also the 
conductivity of the water being used.

7.5.10. WORK WITH PORTABLE (HAND-HELD) ELECTRICAL APPARATUS 
Portable electrical equipment is movable equipment, not permanently connected to the power system, 
being supplied through a flexible cable and plug and socket or similar approved connector. Examples of 
portable electrical equipment are hand lamp, handheld power tool, fixed power tool, moving power tool, 
mobile lighting, fixed lighting, small mobile equipment etc. 
 
Great care shall be taken to ensure that portable equipment is safe as it poses an ever present hazard 
should there be a failure in the insulation or damage to the cable causing the casing to become live or 
exposing live conductors. As a minimum the following shall be considered: 
 
(i) 
Hand-held, tools and equipment shall be designed to operate at rated voltage of 110V (in 
the case of portable inspection hand lamps 24V). Non-interchangeable plugs and sockets 
shall be used to ensure that a tool is connected only to a supply of the correct voltage and 
cannot be connected to a 230/250 V circuit; 
(ii) 
Hand-held, tools and equipment shall be connected to the mains through safety isolating 
transformers having both: 
1. 
An earthed screen between primary and secondary windings; 
2. 
The secondary winding connected to earth in such a way that it’s potential cannot rise 
to more than 55 V with respect to earth. 
(iii) 
Only Class II or Class III portable electrical equipment shall be used for hand-held tools: All 
Class II Hand-held portable electrical equipment should be supplied at 110V ac maximum 
(± 55 V act to earth); 
(iv) 
Electrically operated portable equipment shall not be used in hazardous areas unless the 
use of the equipment is covered by the PTW; 
(v) 
When a residual current device is to be used it shall have a rated residual operating current 
not exceeding 30 mA and an operating time not exceeding 40 ms at a residual current of 
150 mA, as provided by BS IEC 1008 [Ref. 8].

7.6. 
ELECTRICAL ISOLATION (DE-ENERGIZATION) 
All electrical equipment shall be regarded as being live until properly isolated and proved to be dead. 
 
Before carrying out any maintenance, repairs, alterations, cleaning, or testing on electrical equipment the 
equipment shall be isolated (de-energized) from its power source. Isolation is the disconnection from live 
conductors by an open isolator or adequate physical gap from all sources of supply to prevent electrical 
equipment being made live in error. 
 
The operation of switching out a circuit and making it dead from all sources of supply does not by itself 
provide proper isolation of that circuit. 
 
Isolation padlocks (lock out devices) shall be applied to points of isolation for electrical equipment to 
prevent de-isolation or unexpected start-up until the isolation padlock (lock out device) is removed. 
Caution notices (tagout tags) shall also be attached at all points of isolation from live sources of supply, 
to warn personnel not to interfere with such points of isolation, as work or testing may be taking place on 
electrical equipment. 
 
If electrical equipment does not have facilities for applying isolation padlocks (lockout device) at points of 
isolation, additional steps shall be taken to protect from unexpected de-isolation and startup. These steps 
may include one or more of the following: 
 
(i) 
Removal of fuses; 
(ii) 
Racking out of circuit breakers or contactors; 
(iii) 
Disconnecting of circuit conductors. 
 
Before work is done on equipment that has been isolated, test equipment shall be used to verify that the 
circuit elements and equipment parts are deenergized. The test shall also determine whether any 
energized condition exists as a result of inadvertently induced voltage or unrelated voltage backfeed even 
though specific parts of the circuit have been deenergized. The approved test equipment shall be checked 
immediately before and after use to make sure it is working correctly. 
 
All work on electrical equipment that needs energy isolation prior to the start of work shall follow the 
requirements of ADNOC Energy Isolation Standard [Ref. 27].
