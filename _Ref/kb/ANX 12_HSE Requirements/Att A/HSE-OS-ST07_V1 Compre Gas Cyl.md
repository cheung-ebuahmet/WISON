1. 
INTRODUCTION 
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy, 
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all persons who 
undertakes handling, storage, usage, transportation and disposal of Compressed Gas Cylinders, are fully 
aware of the hazards involved and have the competence, training and awareness to undertake such 
activity.  
 
Compressed gas cylinders are frequently used in ADNOC Group sites and can be extremely hazardous 
if misused. Certain precautions shall be taken into consideration while handling compressed gas cylinders 
in order to keep the risk at ALARP level. 
 
2. 
PURPOSE 
This Standard sets out the requirement for all personnel involved in the handling of compressed gas 
cylinders to work safely with minimal risk to personnel, plant and equipment. It specifies requirements for 
safe handling, use and storage of compressed gas cylinders. It applies only to single gas cylinders of 
sizes from 0,5 to 150 l water capacity. 
 
Exclusion: 
 
(i) 
Medical Gas Cylinders used in ADNOC Medical facilities are not covered under this 
Standard.  
3. 
SCOPE 
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates & 
Functions at HQ, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.  
 
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood, 
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

GENERAL 
Compressed gas cylinders shall be handled and used only by properly trained and competent persons.

7.2. 
TYPES OF COMPRESSED GASES [REF. 8] 
There are three major groups of compressed gases stored in cylinders: liquefied, non-liquefied and 
dissolved gases.  
 
In each case, the pressure of the gas in the cylinder is commonly given in units of kilopascals (kPa) or 
pounds per square inch gauge (psig). 
 
Gauge pressure = Total gas pressure inside cylinder - atmospheric pressure. 
 
Atmospheric pressure is normally about 101.4 kPa (14.7 psi). 
  
Note: Compressed gas cylinder with a pressure gauge reading of 0 kPa or 0 psig is not really empty. It 
still contains gas at atmospheric pressure. 
 
7.2.1. Liquefied Gases 
Liquefied gases are gases which can become liquids at normal temperatures when they are inside 
cylinders under pressure. They exist inside the cylinder in a liquid-vapour balance or equilibrium. Initially

cylinder, enough liquid evaporates to replace it, keeping the pressure in the cylinder constant. Anhydrous

7.2.2. Non-Liquefied Gases 
Non-liquefied gases are also known as compressed, pressurized or permanent gases. These gases do 
not become liquid when they are compressed at normal temperatures, even at very high pressure. 
Common examples of these are oxygen, nitrogen, helium and argon. 
 
7.2.3. Dissolved Gases 
Acetylene is the only common dissolved gas. Acetylene is chemically very unstable. Even at atmospheric 
pressure, acetylene gas can explode. Nevertheless, acetylene is routinely stored and used safely in 
cylinders at high pressures (up to 250 psig at 21°C). 
 
This is possible because acetylene cylinders are fully packed with an inert, porous filler. The filler is 
saturated with acetone or other suitable solvent. When acetylene gas is added to the cylinder, the gas 
dissolves in the acetone. Acetylene in solution is stable. 
 
7.3. 
HAZARDS ASSOCIATED WITH COMPRESSED GAS CYLINDERS 
Activities involving the handling of compressed gas cylinders can be extremely hazardous as the 
uncontrolled release of compressed gases can result in serious consequences. A high-pressure cylinder 
can become a lethal missile if the cylinder valve suddenly detaches. 
 
Other associated hazards are related to the contents of compressed gas cylinders, which can be toxic, 
corrosive, or flammable and may lead to injury or fire if released in an uncontrolled manner.  
 
High temperature may also cause a relief device to release the entire contents as the increased pressure 
may lead to the bursting of a rupture disc. As it is conceivable for metal temperatures to reach 81.4 0C in 
UAE while stored in direct sun light, it is therefore essential to store compressed gas equipment under 
shade and securely fixed. 
 
The hazard related to compressed gas cylinders containing oxygen comes from the creation of oxygen 
enriched atmospheres that enhance combustion; e.g. fire involving oils or greases. Some materials that 
do not burn in air could burn when mixed with oxygen. 
 
Compressed gas cylinders are generally bulky and heavy. They can cause serious injury if they drop on, 
fall on, dragged or otherwise strike a person. A full size cylinder may weigh more than 130 pounds. 
Moving a cylinder manually may lead to back or muscle injury as well.  
 
People who handle compressed gas cylinders containing propane shall be aware of the hazard of frostbite. 
Direct contact with either its liquefied form or as a rapidly expanding gas can chill or freeze the skin 
causing what is called a “Frostbite”. Similar direct eye contact can freeze the eye(s) causing permanent 
eye damage or blindness. 
  
Compressed gas cylinders containing acetylene need special attention because of its lower flammable 
limit of 2.5% by volume in air and no clearly defined upper limit. This characteristic makes acetylene gas 
potentially unstable; in either the liquid state or gas under pressure, as it may decompose violently in the 
absence of oxygen or air.

compressed gas cylinders.

7.3.1. OXIDIZING HAZARD  
Any material used in contact with oxidizing gases (e.g. oxygen and nitrous oxide) shall be suitable for 
the service. It is important that equipment used be clean. Oxygen compatible materials shall be used and 
kept free from oils, greases and other contaminants. Deviation from this requirement can cause violent 
reactions and incidents. 
 
The oxygen concentration in work areas, other than hyperbaric chambers, shall not exceed a volume 
fraction of 23.5%. The use of a detection and alarm system should be considered for detection of oxygen 
leakage or of an increase in concentration. 
 
Where oxygen concentration is found to exceed 23.5% and/or an uncontrolled leak is present, personnel 
shall be evacuated from the area immediately. Where clothing has become saturated with oxygen, 
personnel shall be removed from the oxygen source and from potential ignition sources and the clothing 
removed. 
 
7.3.2. FIRE AND EXPLOSION HAZARD 
Fire and explosion are the primary hazards associated with flammable gases, oxygen and other oxidizing 
gases. Flammable gases can be ignited by static electricity or by a heat source, such as a flame or a hot 
object. Oxygen and other oxidizing gases do not burn but will support combustion of organic materials. 
Increasing the concentration of an oxidizer accelerates the rate of combustion. Materials that are non-
flammable under normal conditions may burn in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere. 
 
7.3.3. ASPHYXIATION HAZARD 
All gases except oxygen and air can cause asphyxiation by displacing the ambient oxygen necessary to 
sustain life. They can escape into the atmosphere undetected and quickly reduce the concentration of 
oxygen below the level necessary to support life. The use of oxygen monitoring equipment is strongly 
recommended for enclosed areas where these gases are being used. 
 
7.3.4. TOXIC HAZARD 
Many compressed gases are toxic or very toxic. They could cause various health problems depending 
on the specific gas, its concentration, the length of exposure and the route of exposure (inhalation, eye 
or skin contact). Contact between the skin or eye and liquefied gases in liquid form can freeze the tissue 
and result in a burn-like injury. 
 
Chemical poisoning is one of the primary toxic hazards. Even in very small concentrations, brief exposure 
to these toxic gases can result in serious poisoning injuries. Symptoms of exposure may be delayed. 
 
7.3.5. CORROSIVE HAZARD 
Some compressed gases are corrosive in nature. They can burn and destroy body tissues on contact. 
Corrosive gases can also attack and corrode metals.  
Corrosive gases can chemically attack various materials, including fire-resistant clothing. Some gases 
are not corrosive in their pure form but can become extremely destructive if a small amount of moisture 
is added. Corrosive gases can cause rapid destruction of skin and eye tissue. Common corrosive gases 
include ammonia, hydrogen chloride, chlorine and methylamine.

7.3.6. PRESSURE HAZARD 
All compressed gases are hazardous because of the high pressure inside the cylinders. Gas can be 
released deliberately by opening the cylinder valve, or accidentally from a broken or leaking valve or from 
a safety device. Even at a relatively low pressure, gas can flow rapidly from an open or leaking cylinder. 
 
There have been many cases in which damaged cylinders have become uncontrolled rockets or 
pinwheels and have caused severe injury and damage. This has happened when unsecured, uncapped 
cylinders were knocked over causing the cylinder valve to break and high pressure gas to escape rapidly. 
Most cylinder valves are designed to break at a point with an opening of about 0.75 cm (0.3 inches). This 
design limits the rate of gas release and reduces cylinder velocity. This limit may prevent larger, heavier 
cylinders from “rocketing” although smaller or lighter cylinders might take off. 
 
Poorly controlled release of compressed gas in chemical reaction systems can cause vessels to burst, 
create leaks in equipment or hoses, or produce runaway reactions. 
 
7.4. 
RISK MANAGEMENT PROCESS 
7.4.1. HAZARD IDENTIFICATION 
The first step in managing risks associated with the handling of compressed gas cylinders is to identify 
all hazards (Refer to Section 7.3) that could potentially cause harm to people. These shall be identified 
by: 
(i) 
Conducting a walk through assessment of the workplace; 
(ii) 
Observing the work and talking to workers about how the activity is carried out; 
(iii) 
Inspecting plant and equipment used during the activity; and 
(iv) 
Reviewing incident reports. 
7.4.2. RISK ASSESSMENT  
Group Companies shall ensure risk assessments are conducted for all activities involving the handling of 
compressed gas cylinders to help: 
 
(i) 
Identify which workers are at risk;  
(ii) 
Types of dangerous events that could be triggered by the hazards. For e.g.; decomposing 
acetylene cylinder = fire and explosion. 
(iii) 
Severity of the incidents, such as fatalities, destruction of assets, damage to the environment; 
(iv) 
Assess the effectiveness of existing control measures and decide for each remaining hazard 
whether the risk is ALARP; and 
(v) 
Identify, as necessary, what additional control measures shall be implemented to achieve 
ALARP. 
The likelihood of each hazard actually causing harm in a specific situation shall accordingly be assessed, 
probing the following: 
 
(i) 
What are the conditions under which compressed gas cylinders handling activities are 
carried out? 
(ii) 
How often and for how long will exposure to the hazard occur? 
(iii) 
If exposed to the hazard will the outcome be severe, moderate or mild? 
(iv) 
What is the likelihood of harm being caused?

Elimination

not reasonably practicable, then substitution, isolation, engineering and procedural control 
measures, or a combination of these control measures to minimize the risk shall be considered.

Substitution:

Contamination can occur when foreign substances, other gases, process materials or fluids enter 
the cylinder through the valve. If contamination has occurred or is suspected, the personnel making 
use of the cylinders shall identify and clearly mark the cylinder and shall notify the gas supplier of 
the details on the contamination. It shall be the responsibility of the personnel to ensure that the

(e) 
Misuse 
Gas cylinders shall not be used as roller, supports or for any purpose other than to deliver the gas 
content. 
 
(f) 
Electrical Circuits 
Cylinders shall not be placed where they become part of an electrical circuit. When gas cylinders 
are used in conjunction with electric welding, they shall not be used for earthing (grounding) of 
electrical equipment. Arc welding near gas cylinders should be avoided. The use of a cylinder to 
test the arc performance shall not be allowed. Observing these precautions will prevent the cylinder 
from being arc burned. 
 
(g) 
Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) 
Cylinders and accessories with ferritic (magnetic) materials shall not be used in the proximity of 
magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) equipment. There is the risk of personal injury / fatality and 
equipment damage from such cylinders being magnetically attracted by the unit. 
 
(h) 
Temperature Limits 
Gas cylinders shall not be intentionally exposed to temperature above 650C. Never apply a flame 
or heat directly to any part of a gas cylinder or allow it to come in contact with an electrically 
energized system. In the event that the cylinder has been accidently heated above these 
temperatures, it shall be segregated, and the supplier shall be notified.  
 
In case of fire, compressed gas cylinders shall be removed safely from the heat source in order to 
prevent the internal pressure from exceeding the compressed gas cylinder bursting pressure. 
 
If removal of compressed gas cylinder is not possible due to fire radiation heat or storage site 
locations and limitations, cylinders shall be cooled by using firewater. 
 
Avoid knocking or jarring acetylene gas cylinders. Acetylene gas is very sensitive to shock and can 
decompose violently by polymerization (pressures above 1.5 bar) which will generate heat and 
may result in an explosion. 
 
(i) 
Precautions for Acetylene Gas Cylinders 
Acetylene cylinders exposed to fire need special care as they are known to explode days after 
exposure due to internal slow pressure build up. 
 
Excessive heat or fire will cause fusible metal pressure relief device to melt allowing acetylene to 
escape. Cylinders may rupture violently if sidewalls are exposed to direct flame impingement. If a 
flame is extinguished and acetylene continues to escape, an explosive re-ignition could occur. 
Internal decomposition can occur in cylinders exposed to fire.

7.5.7. STORAGE OF COMPRESSED GAS CYLINDERS 
(a) 
General 
The storage areas for compressed gas cylinders shall be cool dry, well ventilated and equipped 
with compartments, or dividing walls of 1.5 meters (5 feet) high which have a fire resistance of 30 
minutes. 
 
Compressed gas cylinders shall not be placed where they may become part of an electrical circuit 
or a grounding path. 
 
Compressed gas cylinders shall not be stored in an enclosed space or the place where the humidity 
is high, e.g. a vessel hold, unless the storage space is equipped with an oxygen enrichment and 
deficiency detection system that activates visual and audible alarms both inside and outside the 
storage area. Compressed gas cylinders shall be covered in with plastic covers when kept in high 
humidity atmosphere.

1. 
Inert gases are asphyxiants, chemically inactive, odorless, tasteless and colourless, 
and include argon, helium, xenon, neon, krypton and nitrogen. Also, carbon dioxide is 
an asphyxiant; 
Note: Nitrogen normally is considered an inert gas because it does not become 
reactive except at high temperatures and pressures and with catalysts. 
2. 
Inert gases can cause asphyxiation by displacing the ambient oxygen necessary for 
life. Self-contained breathing apparatus or masks attached to an air line with 
breathable air shall be worn in areas containing an oxygen-deficient atmosphere. 
3. 
Because inert gases in sufficient quantities can cause asphyxiation, storage areas 
shall be well ventilated. Only persons trained, qualified and using self-contained 
breathing apparatus with adequate backup should respond to an inert gas leak or 
enter a storage area where gas could be present. Shut off the leak if doing so presents 
no risk; then ventilate the area. 
(v) 
Storage of Gas Cylinders for Oxidizing Gases 
1. 
Oxidizing gases, including oxygen, are non-flammable gases, but in the presence of 
an ignition source and a fuel, they can support and vigorously accelerate combustion. 
Common oxidizing gases include chlorine, fluorine and nitrous oxide. Materials that 
normally will not burn in air can burn in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere. Oxygen is 
extremely reactive with organic materials such as oil, grease or tar if ignited by flame, 
impact or some other energy source. Materials that do burn in air will burn more 
vigorously and at a higher temperature in an oxygen-enriched atmosphere; 
2. 
Oxidizing gas equipment shall be cleaned to remove grease, oils and other 
contaminants using cleaning agents that are oxygen compatible. Any material used in 
contact with oxidizing gases shall be suitable for this type of service. Valves, piping, 
fittings, regulators and other equipment used in oxygen service shall be of a material 
and pressure rating compatible with oxygen; 
3. 
The oxygen concentration in work areas, other than in hyperbaric chambers, shall not 
exceed a volume fraction of 25 %. If it is suspected of exceeding 25 % and an 
uncontrolled leak is present, personnel shall be evacuated from the area immediately. 
Where clothing has become saturated with oxygen, personnel shall be removed from 
the oxygen source and from potential ignition sources and the clothing removed; and 
4. 
Oxidizing gases shall be stored separately from flammable gas cylinders where 
required by relevant regulations. Oxidizing gases shall not be stored with combustible 
materials (especially oil, grease, wood or paper). A minimum separation distance and 
non-combustible barrier as specified in applicable relevant regulations shall be 
maintained.

7.5.9. DISPOSAL OF COMPRESSED GAS CYLINDERS 
The following methods shall be considered in order to dispose an empty cylinder: 
 
(i) 
Maintain manufacturer labels and label the cylinder as “empty”; 
(ii) 
Cylinders shall be returned to the authorized supplier or directly to the vendor; 
(iii) 
Cylinders procured through contractors shall be send back to the same contactor for disposal; 
(iv) 
If a cylinder is encountered that does not have a manufacturer label, contact the vendor for 
advice on disposal and / or identifying the manufacturer through stamp marks on the cylinder; 
(v) 
Unidentified gas cylinder shall not be buried in garbage collection bins, they shall be tagged 
and disposed of to the authorized vendors. 
7.6. 
EMERGENCY RESPONSE 
The following responses shall be pursued in case of an emergency: 
 
(i) 
Emergency response and first aid shall be in place to deal with compressed gas injuries, 
such as air or foreign particle injection; 
(ii) 
Provision shall be in place to immediately shut down air compressors, isolate gas cylinders 
etc. in the event of an emergency; 
(iii) 
Contact details for external emergency services and relevant on site personnel shall be in 
place; 
(iv) 
Evacuation routes and emergency assembly points; 
(v) 
For toxic gases leaks, the cylinder shall be removed to an isolated, well-ventilated area, but 
only if this is reasonably practicable while maintaining personal safety. A general evacuation 
of the building may be necessary and have the cylinder approached only by trained 
emergency response personnel wearing protective apparel and self-contained breathing 
apparatus (SCBA).

The main audit deliverable is a formal and structured report for the attention of GC Management and the 
respective ADNOC Directorate.
