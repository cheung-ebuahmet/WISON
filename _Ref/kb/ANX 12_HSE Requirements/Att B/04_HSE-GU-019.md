1. INTRODUCTION 
Working in the heat is one of the high HSE risks for  workers, especially during summer. In a hot 
environment, workers can develop heat related illnesses such as heat cramps, heat exhaustion 
and heat stroke – all considered as preventable health conditions.  
 
It has been recognised that the conventional tools for conducting quantitative risk assessment for 
hot environments i.e. WBGT, ISO Heat Stress, Heat Stress Index, are not valid and useful for the 
Gulf region. The UAE government enforced a ‘midday break rule’ during summer which is based 
on the administrative controls aiming to relieve workers during a fixed time period in summer. 
The lack of valid tools for risk assessment of hot environment and a scientific approach were the 
main challenges for managing risk of heat stress.  
 
The Thermal Work Limits (TWL) – a scientific quantitative method of managing risk of heat stress -
was introduced and validated by the Health Authority Abu Dhabi in 2008 for the UAE. In 2014, the 
TWL got included in the ADNOC Code of Practice V3-02, 2014, as a requirement for managing risk 
of heat stress. 
 
This document provides guidance on implementation of TWL in order to achieve compliance with 
ADNOC Code of Practice for managing risk of heat stress. 
2. PURPOSE 
- 
Provide guidance for minimising risk of heat stress for workers in a hot environment 
- 
Implement Thermal Work Limits (TWL) as a tool for managing thermally stressful 
environments 
- 
Compliance with ADNOC Code of Practice.  
3. SCOPE AND APPLICATION 
- 
The scope of this guideline includes all GASCO Sites, Pipelines Division and Major Projects. 
- 
The guideline is applicable on workers, i.e. GASCO workers, contractors, sub-contractors, 
shutdown workers, and visitors who are required to do job in hot environments e.g. outdoor, 
indoor, confined space, etc. 
- 
For implementing TWL, workers should be considered as self-paced, healthy, well hydrated 
and acclimatized  to the working environment.

Procedures and practices are in place to enable the requirements to be achieved in

- 
Following safe work practices that reduce or prevent occupational heat exposure; 
- 
Attending relevant training required by the company HSE Training Programme; 
- 
Wearing PPE properly, as instructed and provided by the company; 
- 
Observing rules pertaining to thermal work limits and work management; 
- 
Notifying supervisor when conditions, practices, or equipment are changed that may result in 
potential heat exposure; 
- 
Following guidelines and advice given for hydration during acclimatisation and thereafter as 
required to carrying out tasks safely; 
- 
Immediately reporting symptoms and signs of heat related illness; and 
- 
Participating in the health surveillance programme upon advice of supervisor.

RISK CONTROL 
7.1.1 
Engineering Controls 
Engineering controls should aim at: 
- 
Reducing the metabolic heat production by the body by providing automation and 
mechanization of tasks to minimize the need for heavy physical work and the resulting build-
up of body heat; 
- 
Reducing radiant heat emissions by shielding, covering and/or insulating hot surfaces with 
low emissivity material to reduce heat exchange between the source of heat and the work 
environment; 
- 
Reducing 
humidity 
and 
temperature 
by 
applying 
ventilation, 
air 
conditioning, 
dehumidification and elimination of open hot water baths, drains and preventing steam 
leaks; and 
- 
Providing suitable rest shelters (Appendix-5). 
7.1.2 
Administrative controls  
Administrative controls should aim at: 
- 
Ensuring that personnel are acclimatized to conditions before embarking on full workloads; 
- 
Alternating work periods with adequate rest periods and establishing a work/rest regimen 
that permits workers to set their own work pace in hot environment; 
- 
Ensuring that adequate rest areas are available that meet the recommended standard; 
- 
Encouraging workers for drinking water by appointing a ‘Waterman’; 
- 
Implement administrative control measures as defined for each TWL risk zone  
- 
Provision of adequate supply of cool drinking water; 
- 
Adequate emergency response plan and first aid arrangements; and 
- 
Training of supervisors and workers on prevention of heat stress.

7.1.3 Personal Protective Equipment 
Following PPE may be considered for workers who have high risk of developing heat stress due to 
their nature of job e.g. firefighters and auxiliary team, welders, etc. 
a) Circulating Air Systems  
This type of cooling system uses compressed air circulating directly around the body from a 
supplied air system. These systems should only be used in work areas where physical 
movement and climbing are not required. 
b) Circulating Liquid Systems 
These systems attempt to supplant sweat evaporation as the principal mode of heat 
dissipation. They are worn in close contact with the skin and the coolant, usually water, 
transfers heat from the skin to a heat sink, usually ice.  
c) Cooling Garments 
Commercially available cooling vests may be used as additional measures for prevention of 
heat stress. Such cooling garments can be provided to workers members  
Technical guidance on selection of proper PPE can be obtained from GASCO HSE Division.  
7.1.4 
Midday Break Rule 
- 
The UAE Ministry of Labour issues the decree for midday break for workers working under 
the sun during summer. 
- 
According to the rule, workers should not perform non-emergency jobs under the direct sun 
between 12.30 – 15.00hrs starting from June 15 until September 15. 
8. HEALTH SURVEILLANCE 
- 
The recommended tests for health surveillance are visual check of the urine colour (self-
assessment test) and the Dehydration Test (urine specific gravity test). 
- 
The Urine Colour Chart should be displayed in toilet facilities and communicated to workers 
for self-testing. 
- 
The Dehydration Test should be performed randomly for workers performing jobs in hot 
environment. 
- 
The Dehydration Test (urine specific gravity test) should be conducted by a suitably trained 
and qualified healthcare provider e.g. nurse, paramedic. 
- 
The Dehydration Test should be performed for workers involved in workplace incidents to 
assess if heat stress was a causative factor. 
Appendix-7 and Appendix-8 provide the Urine Colour Chart and the methodology for conducting 
the Dehydration Test.

APPENDIX-1 TWL INSTRUMENT AND MEASUREMENT PROCEDURE 
A. INSTRUMENT FOR TWL 
The recommended instrument for measuring TWL is the ‘Scarlet TWL-1S’ heat stress monitor. The 
instrument automatically calculates TWL by measuring the dry bulb temperature, the wet bulb 
temperature, the globe bulb temperature and the wind speed and accommodates for clothing 
factors, i.e. standard and specialised PPE. It includes following features: 
1. Real-time measurement of TWL, TWL data logging and data analysis by using PC supported 
software, provided by manufacturer. 
2. TWL dependent backlight corresponding to colours of TWL risk zones (green, amber and red). 
3. Pager for alerting about the high risk, that works at a maximum distance of 200m from the 
TWL monitor (optional). 
B. METHODS OF TWL MEASUREMENTS 
a) Instant TWL Measurement 
The instant TWL measurement is suggested for area monitoring to determine the ‘hot zones’. 
b)  Continuous TWL Monitoring 
 
Continuous TWL monitoring (i.e. 8 hours continuous monitoring/recording) should be 
performed when work is carried out indoor or under a shade (e.g. confined space, tank, 
basements, workshops, etc.). 
 
Continuous TWL monitoring is not recommended for outdoor work where instrument is 
exposed to direct sunlight.  
c)  Intermittent TWL Monitoring 
 
Intermittent TWL monitoring (i.e. instrument is kept at room temperature after taking 
readings at intervals) should be performed when work is carried out under the direct sun. 
 
The instrument should not be left under the direct sun for more than 15 minutes as the 
equipment sensors get heated up resulting in false reading.  
C. STANDARD OPERATING PROCEDURE FOR TWL-1S HEAT STRESS MONITOR 
 
Once the ‘Scarlet TWL-1S’ heat stress monitor is turned on and set up as per the instructions 
given in the manual, the instrument is ready for measuring the four environmental 
parameters and computing a TWL value. 
 
The instrument should be exposed to the environment to be checked for TWL for 10 minutes 
to allow instrument to get acclimatized before taking the first reading. 
 
The TWL measurement should be taken in environment, which should be the same or similar 
to the environment where workers are performing job. For example, if workers are 
performing job under the direct sun, then the TWL measurement should also be taken under 
the direct sun.

D. IMPLEMENTATION OF TWL 
 
STEP - 1 
 
 A walk-through survey should be conducted to identify ‘hot zones’ (red and yellow TWL zones). 
Attention should be paid on identification of sources of heat, surfaces emitting radiant heat, and 
air movement at the places where workers performing the job. The Site Quantitative Health Risk 
Assessment Report (QHRA-2010) can be used as a reference for identifying hot zones. 
 TWL measurement should be taken during the period of the day when workers are required to be 
performing job and at the time when ambient temperature is recorded as high (i.e. worst case 
scenario).

 Review should be made for all four parameters of TWL i.e. dry temperature, humidity, radiant 
heat and air speed to identify contributory factors causing the TWL reading low (i.e. less than 115 
W/m2). 
 When practicable, actions should be taken aiming to correct the contributing factors; For 
example: 
- 
High dry temperature – Put mist fan or air cooler; 
- 
High humidity – Increase air flow by putting a fan; 
- 
High radiant heat – Decrease exposure by putting insulation between workers and source; 
replace corrugated metal roof with concrete or wood; and 
- 
Low air velocity – Put fan. 
 
STEP – 2 
 
 All works, where risk of heat stress is identified, should be monitored by using TWL monitor. 
 All efforts should be made to correct the factors causing low values of TWL. 
 If, despite of all efforts, the TWL readings indicate medium or high risk, administrative controls 
should be implemented as per the Appendix 2. 
 Frequency and method of TWL monitoring should be determined with the help of Table 1. 
 If TWL monitor cannot be used in a certain area, a similar nearby location can be used for 
measuring TWL.

APPENDIX-4: PREVENTION OF HEAT STRESS IN FIREFIGHTERS AND AUXILIARY TEAM  
 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should have valid fitness to work medical certificate. 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should be acclimatised. 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should be trained to recognising signs and symptoms of heat 
stress and applying controls. 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should start the working day well hydrated and follow the 
recommended guidelines for hydration (see Appendix-11). 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should immediately report to supervisor for any health 
conditions or use of any medications that can increase the risk of heat stress, or interfere 
with the ability to perform assigned tasks. Supervisor should seek medical advice on workers 
fitness to work before assigning a heavy task. 
 
Firefighters and auxiliary team should follow the work-rest and rehydration regimen as given 
in Appendix 2.

APPENDIX-6: ADMINISTRATIVE CONTROL MEASURES 
I. 
Acclimatisation 
 
Acclimatisation should be applicable to new workers and those who have returned from

 
During acclimatisation, workers should be assigned with tasks so that the body heat 
tolerance is gradually increased to the level of full work performance without adverse health 
effects of heat exposure. 


Hydration 
 
Workers should be adequately hydrated during acclimatisation and continue drinking water



APPENDIX-8: DEHYDRATION TEST [URINE SPECIFIC GRAVITY (SG) TEST] 
The Dehydration Test is used to determine the level of hydration of a person working in hot 
environment. The urine specific gravity (SG) is tested in a urine sample to assess the level of 
hydration. The test should be used for monitoring purposes to check workers’ hydration levels and 
encourage them to drink more fluids to prevent heat related illnesses.  
 
The Dehydration Test must be performed by a suitably trained medical professional. 
 
The Dehydration Test must be conducted when: 
- Anyone  is reported  sick or is suspected for heat related illness; 
- Anyone  is working in areas with TWL less than 115W/m2;  
- Un-acclimatised workers are working during acclimatisation period. 
 
Acceptable SG levels are: 
- 
At the start of the shift - ≤1.015 
- 
Other times and end of shift - ≤1.029 
 
Test results should be recorded and reported to management when actions are required to 
prevent heat related illnesses. 
 
Workers with the result of urine SG for more than 1.020 at the beginning of shift and more 
than 1.030 at other times should not be allowed to work and should be advised to drink 
water. 
 
Repeat SG test should be performed after one hour.  
 Refer to Clinic if SG is not corrected after the repeat test.

APPENDIX-11: PREVENTION OF HEAT-RELATED ILLNESS GUIDELINE 
 
Before you start work 
 
Build up tolerance slowly to the heat and work, if you are not used to working in heat or if you have been off work for a week or more. 
 
Plan your work to perform the heaviest work at the coolest part of the day. 
 
Wear light, loose-fitting, breathable clothing, such as cotton. Take extra care when wearing personal protective clothing or equipment, such as a 
respirator. 
 
Drink plenty of cool water before starting work. 
 
Consult the doctor if you feel sick any time or have a history of high blood pressure, diabetes, kidney problems or fever or taking medication. 
 
Learn the warning signs that indicate that your body may be overheating or low in fluid. 
 
Learn what to do if you or anyone working with you shows signs of heat illness. 
 
During work 
 
Drink at least 2 litres of water every 2-3 hours. Keep a two-litre water container with you and refill it during at each break. 
 
If approved electrolyte drink is available, use it under supervisor’s instruction. 
 
Drink enough to urinate at least three times a shift. 
 
Check colour of urine. Your urine should be pale yellow in colour; if it is dark you need to drink more. 
 
Slow down if you are feeling overheated, or are getting very tired.  
 
Use the buddy system, with people working in pairs.  
 
Work in the shade. Take frequent, short breaks in cool, shaded areas to allow the body to cool down. 
 
Avoid tea, coffee and fizzy soft drinks. These beverages contain caffeine that makes the body lose water and increases the risk of heat related illnesses. 
 
Look for symptoms of heat exhaustion and heat stroke for yourself and other workers. 
 
Inform your supervisor if you feel unwell on the job. 
 
After work 
 
Observe the basic rules of personal hygiene. Regular washing and wearing clean clothes can help prevent skin irritation and rushes. 
 
Eat proper meals, including fruits and vegetables. Avoid eating large meals before working in a hot environment. 
 
Add a little extra salt to your meals in hot weather.  
 
Get plenty of sleep. 
 
Consult the doctor if you feel sick. 
 
The midday break rule must be followed as directed by the UAE government.

APPENDIX-13: USE OF ORAL REHYDRATION SOLUTION (ORS) GUIDELINE 
 
While water remains to be the main source of fluid, an Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) may be 
required to replace lost salts and water. Sports and energy drinks are high in sugar contents and 
often more acidic and therefore not recommended for daily consumption by workers. 
 
What is ORS? 
Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS) is a mixture of salts and sugar (in a specific ratio) that helps to 
prevent and treat loss of water and salts from the body.  
 
What type of ORS should be used? 
The recommended ORS is one which contains 4% or less sugar and sodium, potassium and chloride.  
 
How much ORS should you use in a day? 
You can drink one glass of ORS (150 – 200) and one glass of water alternatively every 15-20 minutes. 
The idea is to take 2 – 3 litres of fluid in 2 hours. The total quantity of ORS should not exceed 2 litres 
in a day. 
  
When should you use ORS? 
Use of ORS is recommended if: 
 
You have been away from hot working environment for more than 10 days; 
 
You are performing moderate to heavy work in hot environment (day or night) for more than 
one hour; 
 
You have developed symptoms of heat-related illness;  
 
You have history of heat-related illness in past; or 
 
You are not suffering from chronic illness (e.g. diabetes, high blood pressure, kidney disease). 
 
When should you avoid ORS? 
Use of ORS is not recommended if: 
 
You are working in hot environment for more than 10 days without developing any symptoms of 
heat-related illness; 
 
You are performing light work in hot environment; 
 
You do not have symptoms of heat-related illness; 
 
You do not have history of heat-related illness in past; 
 
You are suffering from chronic illness (e.g. diabetes, high blood pressure, kidney disease) or on 
medication; 
 
You are on salt-free diet; or 
 
You spend most of the time in the office environment. 
 
Important: If you have any health problem or on medication, please seek medical advice before 
starting use of ORS.
