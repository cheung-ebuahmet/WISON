1. INTRODUCTION
ADNOC Life-Saving Rules are established to protect the lives of all ADNOC employees and contractors
who are involved in work activities that have high potential of HSE incidents.
Adherence to the Life-Saving Rules represent a commitment by all ADNOC employees and contractors
to always follow these rules, at all times, without exception. They complement the embracing of ADNOC’s
100% HSE and “I am the difference” programs, which means that we will always find the time to do it
safely or not do it at all.
Key Messages of the Life-Saving Rules include the following:
(i) The Life-Saving Rules are here to keep you safe;
(ii) Following our Life-Saving Rules is your responsibility;
(iii) You speak up and act whenever the Life-Saving Rules are broken; and
(iv) You have the support from your leaders to enforce compliance with our Life-Saving
Rules.
The Life-Saving Rules do not replace the need to adhere to ADNOC HSE Policy and any of the other
individual ADNOC HSE Standards, they rather reinforce safety requirements for the most common work
activities that have the highest potential to cause serious injuries and/or fatalities, and that require
additional focus and strict adherence at all times. There are no exceptions!
2. PURPOSE
This document serves to provide the framework for ADNOC’s Life-Saving Rules and its implementation
across ADNOC and Group Companies. It outlines the purpose and scope of each of the Life-Saving
Rules and provides guidance to all employees and contractors at all levels in the organisation to reduce
the potential for fatal incidents associated with work activities.
The following are the key characteristics of the Life-Saving Rules
(i) Aimed purely at life-saving;
(ii) Clear and straightforward;
(iii) Simple to understand and communicate;
(iv) Task-level based;
(v) Proactive;
(vi) Actionable, observable, & auditable; and
(vii) For the employees & contractors.
ADNOC will continue to monitor, record, and report all the incidents across the organization, and will
cross-reference these incidents against the Life-Saving Rules.
Effective implementation of the Life-Saving Rules will help to ensure that everyone can return home safely
to their families and friends at the end of each work day.
The term Life-Saving Rules cannot be changed in any way and shall not be abbreviated in its use across
the organization in written or verbal form. It is important to keep the emphasis and focus at all times on
the three words ‟Life”, ‟Saving” and ‟Rules”. The use of any abbreviation will diminish the importance of
these three words.
Ultimate authority for the content of ADNOC Life-Saving Rules collaterals and key messages is remaining
with ADNOC Group HSE Function.

7.1. BYPASSING SAFETY CONTROLS
Many serious incidents occur when safety controls are either ignored, poorly implemented/maintained, or
intentionally or unintentionally bypassed/overridden.
Safety-critical controls include:
(i) Equipment (such as fire and explosion protection and mitigation systems, guards, interlocks
[sensors, logic solvers/PLC, and end devices], lock-out/tag-out devices, relief valves, critical
controls & alarms; safety-critical monitoring equipment; and all fire protection and lifesaving
equipment) whose purpose is to prevent a fatality or other major accident; limit the
consequences of a major accident; or whose failure could cause or contribute substantially
to a fatality or other major accident.
(ii) Procedures and safety critical tasks instructions that if not performed correctly or at the right
time could result in a fatality or other major accident.
(iii) Safety-critical equipment and procedures are typically identified as safeguards (controls)
in various risk assessments such as Job Safety Analysis (JSA); Hazard Identification
(HAZID); Hazard & Operability (HAZOP); Layers of Protection Analysis (LOPA); and Bow
Ties. It is therefore important that the relevant risk assessment be referred to, to help ensure
that all safety-critical equipment and procedures are clearly identified.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Bypassing Safety Controls:
(i) Understand what equipment and procedures are safety-critical, and if unsure speak to your
supervisor and/or HSE representative (or refer to the relevant risk assessment and/or
COMAH report);
(ii) Authorization for bypassing or overriding a HSE system shall only be given by a designated
competent person (area authority) in accordance with the governing site procedures (such
as Permit to Work [PTW] or isolation certificates);
(iii) Alternate prevention and mitigation measures must be assessed, authorized and
implemented, if required, to ensure that risk is adequately managed during the
bypass/override;
(iv) All bypasses/overrides shall be documented and communicated to all concerned/affected
personnel;

7.2. CONFINED SPACE
Incidents involving confined spaces continue to result in multiple fatalities across a wide range of
industries (oil & gas, petrochemical, marine, etc.) despite there being well developed management
practices for confined spaces now in-place for many years across these industries.
A Confined Space is defined as ‟An enclosed or substantially enclosed volume where there is a
reasonably foreseeable risk of serious injury to personnel entering the volume from hazardous
substances or conditions within the volume or nearby.” Many incidents involving Confined Spaces result
from firstly not recognizing what constitutes a Confined Space.
A confined space, such as a vessel, tank, pipe, cellar, pit, or a trench/excavation can contain:
(i) Flammable/explosive gas/vapours; or a
(ii) Toxic or asphyxiating atmosphere; or
(iii) Other dangers such as: energy releases; lack of oxygen; exposure to hazardous chemicals;
things that can fall on you or crush you; or that you can fall from or onto/into.
Authorized access keeps you safe.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Confined Space:
(i) Understand what constitutes a Confined Space and if unsure speak to your supervisor
and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Ensure a valid Permit to Work (PTW) is raised and approved;
(iii) All PTW controls are in place;
(iv) Obtain the necessary authorization before entering a Confined Space;
(v) Understand and follow all of the PTW controls for entering a Confined Space; and
(vi) STOP confined space work at any point of time where the conditions change or there is
reason to believe that work cannot proceed safely, or if any of the controls associated with
the confined space work are felt to be ineffective (including ineffective supervision).

7.3. DRIVING
Managing Motor Vehicle Accidents (MVA) remains one of the largest challenges for all large oil & gas
companies, and MVA continue to have a large contribution to overall employee fatalities.
The driver and passengers must take responsibility for each other’s safety, for example by ensuring all
occupants are wearing a seatbelt, and the driver is not distracted or fatigued.
Fitness for duty means assuring that an individual can complete a task safely and without unacceptable
risk to themselves or others. This includes not being under the influence of drugs and alcohol.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Driving:
(i) Understand your responsibilities as a driver or a passenger, and if unsure speak to your
supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) As either the driver or as a passenger, understand and follow the expectations related to:
the use of seatbelts; following the speed limits (and all other road safety rules); not using
phones or other devices and avoiding other distractions; ensuring fitness of the driver; and
following the journey management requirements;
(iii) Ensure that the vehicle is equipped with the required safety features and is maintained in
safe working order;
(iv) While driving in adverse condition, such as fog, it is required to that the speed of the vehicle
is lowered, and low-beam headlights are turned on. If the fog is heavy and it becomes thick
safely and completely pull the vehicle off the road;
(v) In case of rainstorm, slow down the vehicle at the first sight of rain or drizzle. Turn on the
low-beam headlights. Heavy rainfalls can reduce visibility to zero. Thereby, pull over and
wait for the rain to subside or until visibility is restored; and
(vi) STOP the driving activity if at any point of time any of the safe driving rules are not felt to be
followed, or if any of the controls associated with the driving activity are felt to be ineffective
(including ineffective supervision).

7.4. ENERGY ISOLATION
The are many forms of residual or stored energy that need to be removed and isolated before work can
be undertaken safely, including those associated with: electricity; mechanical/movement; chemical;
heat/cold; and pressure. All of these different sources of energy shall be isolated before work begins.
Energy isolation separates people from hazards such as: hazardous chemicals; electricity; pressure; and
energised equipment. Energy isolation also provides protection from potential energy sources (such as
positioning valves to prevent tanks filling with materials due to gravity). Any stored energy (such as
hydraulic or pneumatic power) shall also be dissipated before the work starts.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Energy Isolation:
(i) Understand when energy isolation needs to be applied/implemented, and if unsure speak to
your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Ensure a valid Permit to Work (PTW) is raised and approved, if required, including all relevant
isolation certificates and registers;
(iii) Verify that all isolations are implemented before starting work;
(iv) There is a process in place to document and communicate the status of isolation between
shifts and different workgroups;
(v) Ensure that isolation integrity is regularly checked and maintained at all times during work; and
(vi) STOP work at any point of time if there is any concern related to the extent of the energy
isolation, or if any of the controls associated with the isolation of energy sources are felt to be
ineffective (including ineffective supervision).

7.5. HOT WORK
Hot Work is defined as “any work activity which will produce a source of ignition (sparks, heat, or a naked
flame), or any work on live electrical equipment or the use of non-intrinsically safe equipment which will
potentially produce a source of ignition.” (It also covers activities such as smoking.)
Hot Work includes any work that creates the potential for an ignition source that is performed in an area
which has the potential for the presence of hydrocarbons or flammable materials (a ‟hazardous area”*).
Ignition sources are open flames or sources of heat that could ignite materials in the work area, such as:
welding; grinding; smoking; torching; (un)loading of hazardous materials (static electricity); internal
combustion engines; chemical reactions; batteries; static electricity; non-classified electrical equipment;
etc.
* Hazardous areas are defined in the UK Dangerous Substances and Explosive Atmospheres
Regulations 2002 (DSEAR) as ‟any place in which an explosive atmosphere may occur in quantities such
as to require special precautions to protect the safety of workers”.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Hot Work:
(i) Understand what constitutes hot work and what work can generate a potential source of
ignition, and if unsure speak to your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Understand what constitutes a hazardous area;
(iii) Confirm that a valid approved Permit to Work has been issued for any hot work that you are
involved with in a hazardous area;
(iv) Smoke only in approved designated areas (and do not carry matches or lighters where not
allowed);
(v) Do not carry portable electronic equipment (such as mobile phones, tablets, smart watches,
laptops) into hazardous areas without an appropriate permit;
(vi) Ensure that the presence of flammable gas is continually monitored; and
(vii) STOP work if there are any changes to the work environment, including other incompatible
work activities occurring or to be undertaken in the same area (Simultaneous Operations
[SIMOPS]), or if any of the controls associated with the hot work are felt to be ineffective
(including ineffective supervision).

7.6. LINE OF FIRE
According to the IOGP data on fatalities over the period 2008 to 2017, the largest number of
industry fatalities were by far associated with a ‘line of fire’ hazard. The intent of the Line of
Fire Rule is to engage individuals to actively look for and avoid ‘line of fire’ hazards.
Whilst the other Life-Saving Rules focus on specific work activities, this Life-Saving Rule is
intended to raise personal situational awareness of ‛struck-by’ and ‛caught in-between’ hazards.
‛Line of fire’ hazards are not always obvious or constant, and can be introduced as a work
activity progresses.
At all times individuals need to continually monitor their surroundings and position themselves
to avoid being in the ‛line of fire’. This includes ensuring you are visible to vehicle drivers and
equipment operators.
Individuals need to recognize when they create a ‛line of fire’ hazard and put others, or
themselves, in the ‛line of fire’.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Line of Fire:
(i) Understand what constitutes a potential ‘line of fire’ hazard, and if unsure speak to your
supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Be aware of the potential for movement hazards that can result in a possible ‛struck-by’ or a
‛caught in-between’ accident;
(iii) Do not cross barriers and stay clear of movement hazards (heavy equipment) or lifting
activities;
(iv) Try to anticipate the unexpected and position yourself, and others, away from possible ‘line
of fire’ hazards; and
(v) STOP work at any point of time if the requirements for ‛line of fire’ protection may be
compromised, or if any of the controls associated with the work activity are felt to be
ineffective (including ineffective supervision).

7.7. SAFE MECHANICAL LIFTING
Mechanical lifting is a common workplace activity across all of ADNOC’s facilities, and loss of control
during a lift can result in an unstable lifting device, or a dropped, or swinging object during a lift, and this
can have serious consequences for plant/equipment and for personnel.
A suspended load is an object that is temporarily lifted and hangs above the ground, from where it can
fall on you, or swing and crush you.
Lifting operations need to be carefully planned and performed by competent personnel using certified
equipment that has been inspected and is fit for the specific lifting activity.
To protect people around suspended loads and any mechanical lifting operation, access must be
controlled through physical barriers and exclusion zones.
When mechanically lifting people (e.g. man-riding, personnel baskets, personnel transfer, mobile
elevated work platforms), the equipment must be specifically designed and certified for lifting people.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Safe Mechanical Lifting:
(i) Understand what your specific responsibilities are with respect to safe mechanical lifting,
and if unsure speak to your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Follow lifting procedures at all times for lifts involving portable, fixed, and transportable lifting
equipment;
(iii) Ensure that a lifting plan is prepared, and any Permit to Work requirements (where
applicable) are followed;
(iv) Ensure that all lifting equipment and lifting gear is inspected and certified;
(v) Ensure that lifting equipment is only operated by competent personnel;

7.8. TOXIC GAS
Toxic gas in the context of ADNOC’s operations will typically refer to hydrogen sulphide (H2S), although
it can also cover other toxic gases such as chlorine (Cl2). (Note - typical hydrocarbon gases, such as
methane or ethane, are not considered ‘toxic’, they are considered to be asphyxiates. Also note that H2S

property, while for its toxicity it is considered under Toxic Gas Life-Saving Rule)
Toxic gas environments (zones) shall be clearly demarked within any facility. When entering a toxic gas
zone specific precautions must be followed, including carrying a personal toxic gas detector and an
emergency escape breathing apparatus.
If you are authorized to enter a toxic gas zone you must:
(i) Understand what a toxic gas is and what constitutes a toxic gas zone, and if unsure speak
to your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Turn on your personal toxic gas detector and carry an emergency escape breathing
apparatus;
(iii) Understand what to do if the detector activates;
(iv) Know how to don the emergency escape breathing apparatus;
(v) Know how to don a Self-Contained Breathing Apparatus (SCBA) if you are required and
deemed fit to work in such an environment; and
(vi) Be aware of wind direction and your escape route to a point of safety throughout operation
in the risk area.

(i) Be aware of the location of toxic gas environments in your workplace;
(ii) Only enter a toxic gas zone with appropriate approval;
(iii) Carry an appropriate gas detector and respiratory protection whenever required;
(iv) Follow the toxic gas response plan in the event of a toxic gas release; and

7.9. WORK AUTHORIZATION
The Permit to Work (PTW) system is what helps to ensure that required work activities are undertaken
safely, with appropriate approval authority and controls. A PTW system describes the steps you need to
take to stay safe during an authorized work activity. It will typically cover such activities as: cold work; hot
work; excavation; confined space entry; work at height; mechanical lifting; use of radioactive sources; etc.
Work authorization is more than just a person-in-charge (the ‛area authority’ and the ‛permit issuer’)
signing the PTW form. It involves confirmation of all preparation has been undertaken and that it is safe,
and also having the appropriate authorization to either start, resume, or hand-over a task.
The persons-in-charge of the work confirm that it is safe to start, that controls are in place & effective and
the task can be performed as planned.
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Work Authorization:
(i) Understand the overall Permit to Work process and your role in it, and if unsure speak to
your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Ensure that you have an approved Permit to Work before commencing work;
(iii) Comply with all of the requirements specified in the Permit to Work, and ensure that
everyone working under the permit is also aware of the requirements;
(iv) Be aware of the potential for simultaneous activities, or associated work, which would impact
on the safe undertaking of either activity; and
(v) STOP work at any point of time where the conditions change or there is reason to believe
that work cannot proceed safely, or if any of the controls associated with the work activity
are felt to be ineffective (including ineffective supervision).

7.10. WORKING AT HEIGHT
Whenever work has to be carried out at height (1.8 meters or higher) it is necessary to provide the worker
with a safe means of access to the work and during the work. This is normally achieved by a purpose-
built fixed platform (with handrails and toe-boards) or by certified scaffolding (a ‘safe-working platform’ -
either permanent or temporary). [Ref 21]
Working at height outside a protected area (such as an elevated work area not enclosed by hand rails)
requires the use of approved fall protection equipment secured to an approved anchor point. Other
considerations for working at height include such as: use of ladders; work over water; rope access; floor
openings; access hatches; and inspection pits. (Note – any floor openings must always be protected with
physical barriers to prevent falls.)
Preventing objects from falling from height and using physical barriers below the working area keeps you
and people working below you safe.
(Note - A ‛protected area’ is an area such as an elevated work area or platform not enclosed by hand
rails and working in these areas requires the use of approved fall protection equipment secured to an
approved anchor point.)
What you need to do to ensure that you follow the Life-Saving Rule for Working at Height:
(i) Understand what constitutes a ‛protected area’ and a ‛safe-working platform’, and if unsure
speak to your supervisor and/or HSE representative;
(ii) Any ‘safe-working platform’ is fit-for-purpose (hand rails, toe-boards, with a safe working
load defined) and all scaffolding is certified;
(iii) Ensure that you use fall protection equipment at all times when not working on a ‘safe
working platform’;
(iv) Ensure that the fall arrest system is properly anchored;
(v) Maintain tie-off at all times when using fall protection equipment;
(vi) Secure all loose equipment, materials and tools when working at height; and

7.11. HOW TO COMMUNICATE & USE THE LIFE-SAVING RULES
There are various ways in which the Life-Saving Rules can be used in your day-to-day work activities,
and all Managers and Supervisors are encouraged to use the following techniques for maintaining the
visibility of the Life-Saving Rules across ADNOC and its Group Companies.
Toolbox talks & Safety Meetings
Toolbox talk and routine safety meetings provide an excellent opportunity to keep the Life-Saving Rules
in the forefront of worker’s minds before they start work for the day, and to refresh on a regular basis.
One obvious question to ask is ‟Can we learn from incidents that involved a Life-Saving Rule not being
followed?”, and this must be highlighted with worker groups to avoid similar issues to arise again.
Pre-job planning
During pre-job planning is an excellent opportunity to identify all of the relevant Life-Saving
Rules that apply to the planned scope of work. This will help to ensure that the associated JSA
and risk assessment adequately covers the relevant hazards involved with the work activity.
The questions listed below can assist with this process:
(i) Are we doing any work today involving any Life-Saving Rule(s)?
(ii) How can we make sure that we follow the Rule(s) from start to finish?
(iii) What needs to be in place for this to happen?
(iv) Is everything in place, and is it in good working condition (fit for purpose)?
(v) What could possibly change during the undertaking of the work that can place people at
higher risk?

Table 1: Life-Saving Rules (LSRs)
1. LSR Violation Clauses
Examples where individuals committed HSE non-compliances/violations under this Category are
illustrated below:
1.1. Work Authorization
a. Did not obtain relevant PTW or Certificate(s) for specific task as per procedure.
b. Appropriate JSA or Formal Risk Assessment - where indicated - not conducted or
available.
c. Did not identify the hazards; risks were not assessed.
d. Identified controls were not in place.
e. Work conducted without a valid PTW.
f. Did not conduct the TBT for the job activity at site.
g. Did not re-validate the permit to reassess the changes in work conditions/scope.
h. Work-site supervision as specified on the PTW not adhered to.
1.2. Safe Mechanical Lifting
a. Did not select and use suitable and certified lifting equipment for lifting the load.
b. Did not use used certified lifting equipment, tools and tackles.
c. Trained and competent lifting crew (i.e. rigger & banksman) were not available.
d. Did not agree on communication means amongst lifting crew.
e. Did not prepare/have a Lifting Plan for critical lifts.
f. Lifting activity not in line with the lifting plan (i.e. Load, Location, etc.).

1.3. Confined Space (CSE)
a. Entered a confined space without a valid PTW and/or CSE Certificate.
b. Did not appoint/available CSE Observer for confined space work activity.
c. Did not maintain an Entry/Exit Log or Register while entering the Confined Space.
d. Did not conduct gas test to ensure safe working conditions.
e. Did not carry out continuous gas testing, though identified.
1.4. Toxic Gas
a. Did not identify the toxic gas environment.
b. Did not carry H S personal monitor while working in the toxic gas environment.
2
c. Did not wear H S personal monitor suitably (near breathing zone) to the coverall.
2
d. Did not ensure safe working conditions before entering confined spaces.
e. Did not carry suitable PPE such as EEBA/SCBA/SAR for toxic gas environments.
1.5. Energy Isolation
a. Did not ensure isolation requirements (Process, Electrical, Instrument, Mechanical, etc.).
b. Isolating Operator did not isolate as per Isolation Certificate requirements prior to
handing it over.
c. Did not verify all locks, disabling devices and isolation tags, prior to authorizing the work.
d. High Voltage Isolations conducted by Unauthorized Electrical Person.
e. Did not carry out electrical Isolation activities using PPE & Safety protection tools.
1.6. Driving
a. Drivers did not plan journey utilizing ADNOC Onshore Journey Management Plan.
b. Driver did not possess valid certification ADSD.
c. Driving a vehicle which was not inspected or fit for purpose.
d. Driver and occupants in a moving vehicle did not wear seatbelt.
e. Drivers did not comply with road safety rules and exceeded allowable speed limits.
f. Driver used mobile phone for texting or talking while driving.
1.7. Bypassing Safety Controls
a. Defeat/Override of HSE critical equipment and systems without authorization.
b. Did not clearly label the overrides, did not communicate and log.
c. Did not undertake risk assessment prior to overriding/disabling safety critical equipment.
d. Did not reinstate safety critical equipment after overriding/disabling condition is over.
e. Did not seek authorizations from appropriate level for by-passing.

APPENDIX 3
1.8. Line of Fire
a. Did not ensure proximity hazards/surroundings are free from hazards.
b. Did not identify/mark the exclusion/no entry zone.
c. Entered the exclusion/restricted zone.
d. Did not wear suitable clothing near the rotating equipment.
1.9. Working at Height
a. Did not inspect the scaffolding, not tagged by competent scaffolding inspector.
b. Scaffolding was not designed & built by competent scaffold erector.
c. Did not carry/use fall prevention equipment for work where required.
d. Did not secure tools & tackles used at heights appropriately to avoid dropped objects.
1.10. Hot Work
a. Did not identify and control ignition sources.
b. Did not ensure storage of flammables suitably.
c. Did not ensure gas test to ascertain suitable work environment for hot work.
d. Did not carry out continuous gas monitoring though identified.
e. Carried out Hot Work without a Hot Work Permit.
f. Carried out Hot Work without protecting surrounding areas.
