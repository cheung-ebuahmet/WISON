1. INTRODUCTION
This Standard provides an overview of biological hazards (recognition, evaluation, and control) as it
affects employees and contractors of ADNOC Group.
The Standard outlines how to recognize biological health hazards in ADNOC workplaces; how biological
hazards can be assessed; the interpretation of field data, and how to control worker’s exposure to
biological hazards aimed in mitigating unnecessary biological risks to health.
2. PURPOSE
This Standard provides valuable information to ADNOC Group and their Contractors in recognition,
evaluation and control of biological agents that may be present in the workplace.
Activities that are carried out by the ADNOC Group and several of their contractors involve complex
interfaces and reactions between processes, plant & machinery, and people. In many cases, such
interactions can directly or indirectly present scenarios or conditions that pose risks to the health and
wellbeing of personnel at work.
This Standard provides a framework and detailed guidance on:
(i) Various kinds of biological agents and their effects on the human body;
(ii) Evaluating the health risks associated with biological hazards (mentioned above) present in
the working environment;
(iii) Controlling the health risks posed by biological agents by utilizing the hierarchy of controls;
and ;
(iv) Management of Legionella bacteria in hot and cold water systems.
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates &
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood,
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7.1. OVERVIEW
A biological agent is a microorganism, cell culture, or human end parasite which may cause infection, allergy,
and toxicity or otherwise create a hazard to human health. Some microorganisms are also capable of transferring
genetic material as they break down or interface with the human organs or body fluids. Biological agents can be
categorised as any of the species shown in Figure 7.1.1 below.

7.2. BIOLOGICAL AGENTS & HEALTH EFFECTS
Many biological agents are present in nature and can be found in living organisms which bring beneficial
effects. However, in some cases, they can have adverse effects on human life and well-being.

7.2.3. SPECIFIC ILLNESSES & BIOLOGICAL AGENTS
(a) Legionella and Legionnaires’ Disease
Legionnaire’s Disease is one of a group of diseases collectively known as legionellosis. There are
thirty-seven different strains of bacteria associated with legionellosis with the most virulent being
Legionella pneumophila. Infection with this bacterium can result in pneumonia and other potentially
life-threatening effects. It is normally contracted by inhaling Legionella bacteria that are either
transported on aerosols or in droplets left behind after water has evaporated.
Legionella organisms are widespread in natural water sources but can also colonize man-made
recirculating water systems such as water storage tanks, calorifiers and air conditioning systems.
The bacteria proliferate in water at temperatures between 20°C and 45°C, with optimum growth
taking place at 37°C. The bacteria are dormant below 20°C and do not survive above 60°C.
Legionella is a hazardous biological agent with potentially severe impacts on personnel and local
communities.
(b) Bloodborne Pathogens
These are hazardous biological agents that can be transmitted through blood and include Human
Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV), as well as the Hepatitis B & C viruses.
(c) Food and Waterborne Pathogens
Food and water normally contain a variety of biological contaminants and can produce illness if the
contaminants are pathogenic or toxic. A few examples of illness caused by food and waterborne
pathogens are:
(i) Acute gastroenteritis (bacterial, viral or toxic)
(ii) Hepatitis A
(iii) Typhoid fever (Salmonella infection)
(iv) Gut infections by parasites
(d) Vector-Borne Pathogens
Pathogenic microorganisms, especially viruses and parasites, may be transmitted to humans
through the bite of insects such as mosquitoes, flies, ticks and mites. Examples of illnesses are:
(i) Malaria, Dengue, Yellow fever, Viral encephalitis (from mosquito bites);
(ii) Newcastle and Lyme disease; Meningitis (from tick bites);
(iii) Toxoplasmosis and Trichomoniasis (from bird ticks);
(iv) Sand fly fever (from flies).
(e) Others
These are hazards that are caused by biological agents present in the workplace, although they
are not necessarily work-related or caused by an occupational activity. Adverse health effects are
usually the consequence of the reaction of toxins or allergens in the body. Examples include:
(i) Bites and stings from poisonous animals, reptiles or insects (e.g., snakes, scorpions, lizards,
or spiders, etc.);
(ii) Allergic reaction caused by contact with certain plants (e.g., grass & tree pollen, poison ivy,
oleander);

7.3. EVALUATION OF BIOLOGICAL HAZARDS EXPOSURE
7.3.1. QUALITATIVE ASSESSMENT
ADNOC Group and Contractors must carry out risk assessments where there is the likelihood of an
employee being exposed to biological agents of any type stated above in Section 7.2. The target of the
risk assessment is to identify what actions must be taken to ensure that the risks to health are eliminated
or minimized. The ADNOC Group and Contractors must also recognise that the scope of the risk
assessment is wider than the simple task of taking samples, since in some cases these may not be
needed. The targets of the risk assessment must be to [Ref. 2]:
(i) Identify where there may be a risk from exposure to biological agents and who may be at
risk;
(ii) Provide a reliable estimate of types and potential extent of exposure to biological agents;
(iii) Identify actions need to be taken to control exposure, based on the hierarchy of control; and
(iv) Profile groups of personnel who are at high, high-medium or medium risk for inclusion in a
health surveillance programme.
The framework for carrying out risk assessments is detailed in ADNOC OHRM Standard [Ref. 13].
Severity ratings for various biological hazard groups can be found in Table 7.2.2 of this Standard, while
Likelihood ratings to account for existing control measures can be found in Table 7.2.1 of this Standard.
In that respect, assessments must be carried out by competent personnel and the information collected
during the assessment must be reliable and reflective of current plant and operating conditions. The
assessment must be recorded, and must contain a plan showing prioritised actions for dealing with the
high, high-medium or medium risks, as well as timed schedule of actions/deliverables and responsibilities
for closing out the actions.
7.3.2. QUANTITATIVE ASSESSMENT
Unlike other potentially hazardous agents in the workplace, quantitative assessment of exposure to
biological agents poses a distinct challenge to the IH/OH SME. The main reasons behind this are [Ref.
4]:
(i) Pathogenic biological agents can be hazardous at extremely low concentrations, while other
species only pose adverse health effects at several higher orders of magnitude;
(ii) Certain agents are extremely resilient while others are easily degraded in the sampling
process;
(iii) Certain fungal spores are easily identified and counted, while many strains of bacteria are
difficult to distinguish;
(iv) Sensitive & specific analytical methods are available for quantification are not available for
all biological agents;
(v) Biological aerosols do not conform with the size-selective criteria that apply when sampling
for respirable or inhalable dust particles;
(vi) During sampling and analysis, biological agents are under stress and this can lead to
appreciable loss of viability. To avoid that loss, sampling for biological agents can only run

for short periods [typically a few minutes] and this in turn introduces considerable

(vii) Selection of sampling media, storage & transportation temperatures, extraction, growth

(viii) Competitive organisms may inhibit the growth of each other due to the production of toxins;

7.4.4. ADMINISTRATIVE SYSTEMS
This category is similar to that described in Section 7.4.3, except that it formalizes the procedures and
systems that must be implemented and regularly maintained to ensure effectiveness and continuity of
control. Examples of administrative strategies are listed below [Ref. 2]:
(i) All personnel must be provided with awareness and information (preferably in appropriate

Break the Chain; Safe Handling Procedures; and the Controls that the Company has in place

(ii) Registration of all workers who are potentially exposed to hazardous biological agents as

contents;

APPENDIX 2 MANAGEMENT OF LEGIONELLA
1. Introduction
Legionella represents a group of bacteria that are commonly found in damp environments. They occur
naturally in rivers, lakes and sometimes in soil. They can also proliferate in some artificial water systems
such as hot water supply systems, the cooling systems of some industrial processes and air conditioning
units [Refs. 2 & 5]. Legionella is one of the main biological agents that can potentially affect workers in all
aspects of process and non-process-related activities within ADNOC Group and their Contractors.
Exposure to Legionella can lead to Legionnaires’ disease or legionellosis. The disease can occur through
inhalation of Legionella bacteria, either in tiny droplets of water (aerosols), or in droplet nuclei (the particles
left after the water has evaporated) contaminated with Legionella, deep into the lungs. There is evidence
that the disease may also be contracted by inhaling Legionella bacteria following ingestion of contaminated
water by susceptible individuals. Person-to-person spread of the disease has not been documented.
Initial symptoms of Legionnaires’ disease include high fever, chills, headache and muscle pain. Patients
may develop a dry cough and most suffer difficulty with breathing. About one third of patients infected also
develop diarrhoea or vomiting and about half become confused or delirious. Legionnaires’ disease can be
treated effectively with appropriate antibiotics.
The incubation period for the disease is typically 3 to 6 days. Not everyone exposed will develop symptoms
of the disease however; some may only experience a mild flu-like infection. Certain groups of people are
known to be at higher risk of contracting Legionnaires’ disease. Men are more susceptible than women,
as are those over 45 years of age, smokers, diabetics and people with cancer or chronic respiratory or
kidney disease.
Legionella pneumophila is also responsible for a short feverish form of the illness without pneumonia,
known as Pontiac fever. Its incubation period is between 2-3 days. Another species of Legionella,
Legionella micdadei, is responsible for a similar form of the illness without pneumonia called Lochgoilhead

2. Abundance
Legionella bacteria can survive in a wide variety of environmental conditions, but water temperatures
between 20°C to 60°C are ideal for its growth. The organisms can remain dormant in cool water [< 20°C]
and multiply only when water temperatures reach a suitable level. Temperatures may also influence
virulence; for instance Legionella bacteria held at 37°C have greater virulence than the same Legionella
bacteria kept at a temperature below 25°C.
Legionella bacteria also require a supply of nutrients to multiply. Sources can include, for example,
organisms within the water system such as algae, amoebae and other bacteria. The presence of sediment,
sludge, scale and other material within the system, together with biofilms, also play an important role in
harbouring and providing favourable growth conditions for the bacteria.
Legionella bacteria commonly encountered in environmental sources may eventually colonise
manufactured water systems. Examples of such water systems are:
(i) Those incorporating a cooling tower;
(ii) Those incorporating an evaporative condenser;
(iii) Hot and cold water systems; and
(iv) Other plant and systems containing water which is likely to exceed 20°C and which may
release a spray or aerosol (i.e. a cloud of droplets and/or droplet nuclei) during operation or
when being maintained.
3. Sources of Legionella
This applies to the control of Legionella bacteria at any facility where there is work activity or premises
where water is used or stored and where there is a means of creating and transmitting water droplets which
may be inhaled, thereby causing a reasonably foreseeable risk of exposure to Legionella bacteria.
A water system includes all plant/equipment and components associated with that system, (e.g. all
associated pipe-work, pumps, feed tanks, valves, showers, heat exchangers, quench tanks, chillers etc.).
It is critical that the system is considered as a whole and not in isolation.
Dead legs and parts of the system used intermittently must also be included as part of the system since
they can create particular problems with microbial growth going unnoticed. Once brought back on-line they
can cause heavy contamination, which could disrupt the efficacy of the water treatment regime.
Humidifiers and air washers, spa baths and pools, car/bus washes, wet scrubbers, indoor fountains and
water features must also be considered.

Control Measures [Ref. 8]
Based on the level and extent of risks identified from the risk assessment, the ADNOC Group Maintenance
and HSE departments must determine what control measures are necessary to reduce risks to ALARP. In
that respect, there are several options along the hierarchy of controls that can be applied, as demonstrated
in the following subsections.
The hierarchy reflects the fact that eliminating and controlling risk by using physical engineering controls
and safeguards is more dependable than relying solely on systems of work:
Eliminating risks: e.g. by substituting a hazardous biological agent with something less/non-hazardous,
e.g., using a non-toxigenic strain of a biological agent when carrying out laboratory quality control (QC)
tests;
Controlling risks at source: by using engineering controls and giving collective protective measures priority,
e.g., using a microbiological safety cabinet when work could create an infectious aerosol, or using needle
safety devices to prevent and control needlestick injuries; and
Minimizing risks by designing suitable systems of working: e.g. having an effective hand hygiene policy in
place in laboratory or healthcare settings. This option also includes the use of personal protective clothing
and equipment (PPE), but PPE should only be used as a last resort after considering elimination or tackling
at source.
