1. INTRODUCTION
For the purposes of this Permit to Work standard, work can be described as an activity that comprises
one or more tasks. It is implicit that the work has been agreed as necessary, having been through the
planning stage of the work management process. The Permit to Work System is concerned with
authorizing and managing this work and related risk involved.
The issue of a Permit does not, by itself, make a job safe. Those preparing for the work can only achieve
that, together with those supervising and executing the work. In addition to the PTW System other
precautions may need to be taken, e.g. in relation to Plant Operations and Worksite Preparation phases
and monitoring of conditions or atmospheres. All levels in the organization must clearly understand both
the PTW System their roles within it and be assessed as competent to perform those roles.
A Permit is used for controlling and coordinating work to establish and maintain safe working conditions,
ensuring that all foreseeable site hazards are considered, and the appropriate mitigation efforts are
defined and implemented prior to commencement of work until completion. It is a written agreement
between the person issuing the Permit and the person receiving the Permit that clearly documents, at a
minimum, the following elements:
(i) A specific work scope;
(ii) All known worksite conditions via supporting documents;
(iii) Any necessary preparations;
(iv) All foreseeable worksite hazards, precautions and controls relating to the work scope;
(v) Equipment, systems, buildings and work areas required for the work scope;
(vi) Any other affected parties and required communications including identification and control
of concurrent activities;
(vii) Requirements for personal protective equipment to be used to safely complete the work
scope;
(viii) Verification that the site and or equipment have been prepared for the work scope;
(ix) Revalidation over change of shift or in cases of suspension;
(x) Provision for recording that site has been checked as left in a safe condition and that
equipment may be reinstated or left safely isolated and that the permit is closed/cancelled.

2. PURPOSE
The PTW System provides a formal and controlled process that identifies and communicates hazards
and risks associated with planned work or breakdown work. The process ensures that appropriate
precautions and control measures are implemented so that the work can proceed and be completed
safely.
The purpose of this standard is to ensure that a well-defined Permit to Work (PTW) system is implemented
across all ADNOC Group Companies. This would ensure a safe working environment by providing
management control over the various hazardous work activities. It is important to note that a Permit to
Work is not a permission to carry out hazardous work. It is an essential part of a standard that provides
instructions on how to carry out any potentially hazardous work safely and in a managed and controlled
way.
The PTW System achieves this by ensuring that:
(i) Types of work covered by the PTW system are clearly defined and authorized at the
appropriate level of responsibility;
(ii) Work is categorized according to risk and, based on the risk category, appropriate types of
Permits with supporting Certificates (e.g. Confined Space Entry, Excavation, Diving,
Isolation, etc.) are used;
(iii) An appropriate level of planning is applied and sufficient time allowed for proper
development of the Permit documentation and work-site preparations to ensure that all
measures are in place for the safe execution of work activities and that all the involved
personnel are made fully aware of the requirements at each stage;
(iv) Persons authorized to plan, prepare, approve, and carry out work activities are trained and
competent;
(v) An appropriate level of Job Safety Analysis is carried out for all work activities;
(vi) Appropriate control measures are identified to meet ALARP, authorized at an appropriate
level and implemented as specified on the Permit;
(vii) The Permit and supporting Certificates are issued prior to the commencement of work
activities and that people responsible for the operation of the area are aware of all the work
activities being conducted;
(viii) Work sites and equipment are adequately prepared and safe before a Permit is issued and
before work commences including the implementation and verification of appropriate
controls such as isolations, etc.;
(ix) The personnel executing the work fully understand the scope of work, limitations of the
Permit and its associated HSE controls requirements and agree to comply fully with all permit
conditions;

(x) All work activities are carried out safely in accordance with the conditions of the Permit with
monitoring in place to ensure the safety of all involved personnel (including in the
surrounding area and / or in other affected operations);
(xi) Worksite is returned to a safe condition on suspension and / or completion of work;
(xii) A time validity is applied to each Permit, limiting the duration of the work with formal

(xiii) PTW System includes a formal hand-back procedure to ensure that any part of the plant

(xv) Activities exempted from the PTW process are identified, assessed and approved.

shall be consulted rather than taking risks.
3. SCOPE
This document shall be applicable to all ADNOC group companies, employees, contractors, facilities
(including interface areas), activities and services without exception.
Contractors shall be responsible for ensuring that all activities performed within ADNOC operating
facilities in the course of carrying out their contractual requirements are managed in accordance with the
requirements of this document and reported, when applicable, to the contract holder.
In cases, where work is to be carried out on non-ADNOC facilities/greenfield locations, work may be
managed under an alternate Safe System of Work. Where Contractor Safe System of Work is
implemented, Contract Administrator shall ensure that it is compliant with ADNOC requirements.
This standard shall be used and read in conjunction with other WMS standards. WMS Framework
standard contains governing principles such as custodianship, deviations, document retention, training
and competence assurance which are applicable to all WMS standards.
4. LAWS AND REGULATIONS
There are no specific laws and regulations applicable to this standard.

6. ROLES AND RESPONSIBILITIES
In this section, all roles within the Permit to Work process are described together with their specific
responsibilities and accountabilities.
For proper harmonization of the PTW process across ADNOC Group, all Businesses should adopt the
common terminology for naming of roles and understanding of their responsibilities and accountabilities.
The ultimate success of a PTW system depends on the competence and awareness of the people
carrying out the activities. If personnel do not have a sound understanding of permit requirements and
their own responsibilities, the system will have a limited effect on maintaining a safe operation and
preventing accidents.

7.1.2. PTW EXEMPT WORK
Certain activities might be exempted from the PTW process. PTW Exempt activities shall meet all the
below mentioned criteria:
(i) routine activities (including maintenance) conducted on a regular basis; and
(ii) activities which are low risk in nature; and
(iii) single discipline/ trade with no involvement of other disciplines/ parties; and
(iv) are completed in less than a shift; and
(v) have no impact on operations.
These tasks can be conducted under standard approved procedures or job safety checklist or work
practices without the issuance of a permit. For PTW exempt work. the Performing Authority (PA) shall
develop a JSA Level 2 i.e. Hazard Management Form (HMF) or Formal Risk Assessment in line with the
approved Standard Operating Procedure (SOP), SMP or Work Instruction for the subject work activity.
Asset Owners of respective business shall carefully control the process of exempting work activities.

business which do not require PTW. The following are mandatory requirements:
(i) The list shall be approved in writing by Asset Owner.
(ii) The list shall be reviewed and approved on an annual basis.
(iii) The list shall be readily available at designated areas namely Permit Control Facility, Control
Room, etc.

Dynamic Risk Assessment Process
Once the job commences, it is important to monitor the worksite for any changes that might prevent the
job from being completed safely. Dynamic Risk Assessment is defined as the continuous process of
identifying hazards and assessing risks arising due changing circumstances of an operational
environment followed by taking action to eliminate or reduce risk.
In some circumstances the dynamic risk assessment activity can be informal, and occurs without any
discussion or documentation.
e.g.: a worker notices debris in a walkway and clears it away without incident.
However, in certain circumstances, dynamic risk assessment shall require suspending the work and
formally assessing the situation

sequence of the task changes.
(a) Supervision

(i) All work party members shall follow the HSE precautions and check conditions at regular

(b) Stopping the Job
Many incidents happen when conditions at the worksite change, when conditions are not as

(a) Re-validation Rules:
If the work activity requires shift change, effective handover between incoming and outgoing
personnel at shift handover and crew change is critical to ensure continuity is maintained to prevent
adverse impacts on safety.
Shift Handover(s) can be one of the most vulnerable times for the PTW system. The failure to pass
on information, or the correct information, has been shown to be the cause of many accidents.
There shall be sufficient overlap to allow proper review and discussion of all permits on the facility.
At shift change, all incoming personnel shall, at the earliest practicable opportunity, become
acquainted with the status of all ongoing and planned work.

APPENDIX NO. 4. CERTIFICATE GUIDANCE: CONFINED SPACE ENTRY (CSE)
Introduction
A confined space means an enclosed or partially enclosed space that is not designed or intended primarily
to be occupied by a person but is intended (or is designed) to be at normal atmospheric pressure while
any personnel are in the space. A confined space is determined by the hazards associated with a set of
specific circumstances and not just because work is performed in a small space.
Confined spaces pose dangers because they are usually not designed to be areas where people work.
Confined spaces often have poor ventilation which allows hazardous atmospheres to quickly develop,
especially if the space is small. The hazards are not always obvious and may change from one entry into
the confined space to the next.
Examples of confined space include ducts, Culverts, Tunnels, Boreholes, Bored piles, Manholes, Shafts,
Excavations and Trenches, Sumps, Inspection and Under-machine pits, Cofferdams; (b) Freight
containers, Ballast Tanks, Ships’ engine rooms and Cargo holds; (c) buildings, building voids; (d) some
enclosed rooms (particularly plant rooms) and compartments within them; (e) enclosures for the purpose
of asbestos removal; (f) areas used for storage of materials that are likely to oxidise (such as store rooms
for steel chains or wood pellet hopper tanks); (g) unventilated or inadequately ventilated rooms and silos;
(h) structures that become confined spaces during fabrication or manufacture; and (i) interiors of
machines, plant or vehicles.
The risks of working in confined spaces include:
(i) Loss of consciousness, impairment, injury or death due to the immediate effects of airborne
contaminants (note that toxic substances such as H2S may be released when sludge or
other material inside the space is disturbed or may build up as a result of the chemical
materials being used to carry out work);
(ii) Fire or explosion from the ignition of flammable contaminants (examples include trapped
hydrocarbons, pyrophoric scale, etc.) in oxygen enriched environment;
(iii) Being trapped or asphyxiated by inwardly converging walls or by a floor which slopes
downward and tapers to a smaller cross-section;
(iv) Configuration and egress / access presents difficulties for rescuing and treating an injured
or unconscious person;
(v) Asphyxiation resulting from oxygen deficiency or immersion / engulfment in a free-flowing
material such as grain, sand, fertilizer, catalyst, plastic pellets, water or other liquids;
(vi) Health effects arising from heat /cold stress or from noise generated through use of
equipment, amplified by reflection off hard surfaces and resulting in hearing loss or tinnitus
(note excessive noise may also prevent workers hearing warning signals or distract workers
from their work); and
(vii) Contains any other recognized serious safety or health hazard.

Safe Management of Confined Space Entry
Preparation of a confined space shall be ensured for safe entry. It might include any or combinations of
the following activities, isolation from energy sources or live process lines, depressurizing, draining,
washing out and ventilating or providing an inert atmosphere.
(i) Isolation: Confined spaces shall be positively isolated from harmful substances and energy
sources. All process lines connected to the confined space should, where possible, be
disconnected and/or blinded. An Isolation Certificate shall be used. Refer to WMS Standard
- Energy Isolation.
Any driven equipment installed on or in a confined space shall be positively isolated from
every source of motive power. For electrically driven equipment, switchgear shall be locked
in the isolated position. Suitable means of positive isolation shall be devised for other types
of motive power, e.g. instrument air, power gas, hydraulic power. Refer to WMS Standard -
Energy Isolation.
(ii) Safe atmospheric conditions for working: Temperatures inside confined spaces should
be reduced to a maximum of ambient temperatures. Where this is not possible, work/entry
duration shall be reduced. Please refer to ‘ADNOC standard of Occupational Health’ for
more details.
A safe atmosphere must be ensured, as far as reasonably practicable, during work in a
confined space. A safe atmosphere in a confined space is one that:
1. Has a safe oxygen level (>19.5% and < 23.5%);

2. Is free of airborne contaminants or any airborne contaminants are in concentrations

3. Any flammable gas or vapor in the atmosphere is at concentrations below 5% of its
LEL.
The above values are minimum standards; Group Companies can apply stricter measures
if required. A safe atmosphere can be achieved within the confined space using methods
such as cleaning, purging and ventilation.
Ventilation of a confined space with fresh air, by natural, forced or mechanical means, may
be necessary to establish and maintain a safe atmosphere and temperature for as long as
anyone is in the confined space.
Testing and monitoring the atmosphere in a confined space is a routine part of
determining appropriate control measures. Any air monitoring in a confined space should be
carried out by a competent person using a suitable, correctly calibrated gas detector. It may
be necessary to test the atmosphere for:
1. Oxygen content;
2. Airborne concentration of flammable contaminants;
3. Airborne concentration of potentially harmful contaminants (for example, hydrogen
sulphide and carbon monoxide).
As a minimum, initial testing should be done from outside the confined space by inserting a
sample probe and/or portable gas detection device at appropriately selected access holes,
nozzles and openings. Because contaminants can settle at different levels, each part of the
confined space should be tested – side to side and top to bottom. For example, some gases
(such as hydrogen sulfide) are heavier than air and in unventilated areas will settle to the
bottom of the space, while other gases (such as methane) are lighter than air and will collect
at the top of the space. Testing should be carried out on a sufficient number of points to
accurately reflect areas of the space that is likely to be accessed.
Re-testing and continuous monitoring of the air may be necessary if the risk assessment
indicates that conditions may change due to the work being done or the disturbance of
hazardous material in the confined space.
Refer to Atmospheric Test Record Form for further requirements related to Atmospheric
Testing.

confined space contains a safe oxygen level or safe levels of airborne contaminants, then
appropriate respiratory protective equipment must be provided. The respiratory protective
equipment should be provided and worn in situations where there is no exposure standard
for a substance or where the substance is present in an unknown concentration.

to the bottom of the space, while other gases (such as methane) are lighter than air and will collect at the
top of the space.
Testing should be carried out on a sufficient number of points to accurately reflect areas of the space that
is likely to be accessed. Re-testing and continuous monitoring of the air may be necessary if the risk
assessment indicates that conditions may change due to the work being done or due to disturbance of
hazardous material in the confined space.
The conditions should also be continuously monitored, for example, when forced ventilation is being used
or when the work activity itself could give rise to changes in the atmosphere. The frequency of subsequent
testing shall be stipulated on the Atmospheric Test Record Form based on the Risk Assessment. A
confined space located within Hydrogen Sulphide classified area shall be monitored continuously.
While work is being carried out in a confined space, it is required to ensure that the concentration any
flammable gas, vapor or mist in the atmosphere of the space is less than 5% of its LEL, so far as is
reasonably practicable. If it is not reasonably practicable, and the concentration of any flammable gas,
vapor or mist in the atmosphere of the confined space:
(i) Is equal to or greater than 5% but less than 10% of its LEL—the person must ensure that
any worker is immediately removed from the space unless a suitably calibrated, continuous-
monitoring flammable gas detector is used in the space;
(ii) Or is equal to or greater than 10% of its LEL—the person must ensure that any worker is
immediately removed from the space.
(iii) Where a flammable atmosphere may exist in a confined space and there is a risk of fire and
explosion, all ignition sources in the vicinity must be eliminated.

APPENDIX NO. 9. CERTIFICATE GUIDANCE: IONIZING RADIATION
Introduction
Ionizing radiations, such as X-Rays generated by different types of equipment and Gamma rays which
are generated by radioactive isotopes (example Iridium-192) are widely used for Non-Destructive testing
of various welds, pressure vessels, steel structures and so forth and it is important to understand that the
material tested does not retain any radioactivity once testing is completed.
For all practical purposes, harmful radiations like X-rays and Gamma rays emitted by radioactive sources
have certain properties which must be understood. Even though rays have the ability to penetrate the
body but cannot be perceived by any of the five senses. Rays can be absorbed and scattered by matter,
travel in straight lines at the speed of light, ionize gases, affect photographic film emulsions and, by far
the most important, are harmful to living cells. Radiation dosages are cumulative and do not dissipate for
a period of time.
Safe Management of Ionizing Radiation Activities
(i) Radioisotopes with high radioactivity and radio toxicity are to be placed in sealed sources to
mitigate leakage of the isotope itself. Sealed sources completely enclose the radioactive
material, which is also permanently bonded or fixed to a capsule or matrix designed to
prevent its release under the most severe conditions of normal use and handling.
(ii) The following controls should be used either stand-alone or combined to minimize an
uncontrolled dose to personnel:
1. Distance: Radiation intensity decreases inversely with the square of the distance from
the source. The safe distance for unshielded radiation source is based on a maximum
permissible dose rate.
2. Time: Radiation exposure rate depends upon exposure time and hence less time
results in a lower exposure rate.
3. Shielding: When adequate distance protection is not practicable, shielding materials
such as lead, steel, or concrete are commonly used to reduce radiation.
In addition, the following precautions are applicable to all radiographic activities.
(iii) Reduce unavoidable radiation exposure to the shortest time;
(iv) Maintain maximum distances while still keeping direct visual contact of radiation areas;
(v) Consider all hazards involved in the work, such as mechanical, electrical, chemical,
biological, and fire;
(vi) Keep radioactive containers covered when not in use;
(vii) After working with radioactive materials, personnel should wash their hands and check
hands and shoes for contamination;

(ix) Ensure that radiation exposure record keeping is available;

(xi) Eating, drinking, smoking, food storage, in any area posted for the use and/or storage of
radioactive materials is to be prohibited;
(xii) Storage or use of radioactive materials takes place only in approved areas.
