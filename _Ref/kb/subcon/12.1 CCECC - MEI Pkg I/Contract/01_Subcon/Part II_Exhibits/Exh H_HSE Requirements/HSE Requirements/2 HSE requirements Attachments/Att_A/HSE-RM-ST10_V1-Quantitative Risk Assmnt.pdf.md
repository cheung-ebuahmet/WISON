1. INTRODUCTION
Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) is a technique for the evaluation and integration of hazard frequency
and consequence to derive a numerical estimate of risk.
In the context of facility and activity risk assessment, QRA shall be applied when:
 High-risk or Major Accident Hazards have been identified for a facility or operation; QRA shall be
required to demonstrate tolerability and ALARP in support of a project or facility COMAH Report.
 There is potentially high risk of exposure to workers or the public (e.g. sour field developments,
drilling activities close to populated areas, etc.).
The main objectives of a QRA are:
 quantifying the overall risk associated with a facility or activity;
 identifying the main contributors to the overall risk;
 identifying measures to reduce the overall risk and analyzing their effectiveness;
 where a number of alternative options are available, determining the relative safety and cost
effectiveness of the alternatives;
 assisting in the identification of new alternatives;
 demonstrating compliance with ADNOC risk tolerability criteria and assisting in the demonstration
and achievement of ALARP to all stakeholders;
 increasing awareness of hazards, scenario-based escalation and mitigation; and
 as an aid to communication to the workforce and third parties of their impact on, and exposure to,
risk.
2. PURPOSE
The purpose of this Standard is to provide an overall understanding of the QRA process and its application
within the ADNOC Group. This Standard establishes consistent requirements for planning, conducting
and documenting QRA studies for the ADNOC Group.
All QRA studies carried out for ADNOC Group shall be in accordance with the requirements of this
Standard.
Requirements for carrying out QRA studies at various stages of a project are provided in ADNOC HSE
Impact Assessment (HSEIA) Standard [Ref. 1]. Recommendations from audits or incident investigations
may also initiate a QRA study.
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to the ADNOC Group and its
Contractors.
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood,
implemented, complied with and monitored at all times.

7.1. QRA LIFE CYCLE
The QRA is an integral part of the ALARP process for both project activities and operations.
QRA process starts at concept stage. In some cases, coarse/preliminary QRA may be carried out during
concept stage especially where several options have been identified. This allows a comparison of the
relative risk levels from the different options to support the decision making process.
Comprehensive QRA shall be performed during the FEED Stage of the project. This QRA shall be later
updated during the EPC (Detailed Engineering) Stage based on newly established information/data. EPC
QRA also forms the basis for the operations and forms part of HSEIA dossier.
For existing facilities, where QRA is not available, it shall be carried out at the first available opportunity
(earliest) and update the HSEIA Dossier as per ADNOC HSEIA Standard [Ref. 1].
As part of COMAH/HSEIA update, QRA shall be reviewed to determine if significant changes to facilities,
barriers in place, manning, building functionality or occupancy, etc. are observed or carried out. QRA
study shall be updated based on these changes to ensure assessment reflect the updated changes.

7.3. HAZARD IDENTIFICATION
All potential Major Accident Hazards associated with the facility or operation shall be taken forward for
QRA assessment. The potential hazardous event is usually called the 'top event'. Examples of such top
events are:
1 Hydrocarbon leaks from process equipment, storage facilities, risers or pipelines;
2 Blowouts during drilling, production and work-over/wireline;
3 Road/ Marine/Air transport accident;
4 Collisions with visiting or passing vessels; and
5 Extreme environmental loads.
Hazard identification workshops such as HAZID, HAZOP, etc. along with document reviews are
conducted to identify the Major accidental hazards associated with project facilities Generally, two types
of hazards are seen, process and non-process.

Process hazards generally consist of onshore/offshore process facilities, storage facilities,
piping/pipelines, drilling activities, etc. whereas non-process hazards consist of occupational hazards,

Reference should also be made to accidents and incidents that have occurred in the oil and gas industry

7.3.1. IDENTIFICATION OF HAZARDOUS INVENTORIES
For ADNOC operations and facilities, and the oil and gas industry, the majority of hazardous events
involve releases of hydrocarbons, flammable or combustible or toxic materials. When conducting QRAs,
such hazardous events shall be defined in terms of the isolatable sections and their operating conditions.
Isolatable sections are defined and bounded by the location of the following:
1 Emergency Shut-Down Valves (ESDVs);
2 Emergency Depressurisation Valves (EDPVs).
Isolatable sections shall be further divided into various failure cases based on the locations, process
parameters, operating modes, etc. to capture appropriate risk profile and release events. These isolatable
sections and failure cases shall be clearly marked on the P&ID and attached to QRA Report and shall be
reviewed and approved as part of milestone requirements.
On detection of a hydrocarbon release, fire or manual initiation, these valves close to limit the quantity of
hydrocarbons that can feed an incident. Level control valves, pressure control valves and non-return
valves are not considered to provide positive isolation and are not used to define isolatable sections.
Inventory analysis shall be carried out for these isolatable sections based on the static inventory based
on the volume and dynamic inventory considering shutdown duration.
7.3.2. IDENTIFICATION OF POTENTIAL CONSEQUENCES
The development of the top event into a serious incident depends on the effect of safety systems,
prevailing environmental conditions, actions by personnel and presence of ignition sources. QRA studies
use event trees to model the chronological series of events. Event trees provide a systematic method to
ensure all potential outcomes as a result of a specified top event are identified. Development of credible
accident scenarios using event trees thus provides a structure to the conceptual and physical escalation
scenario analysis. Refer to Figure 7.3.1 for example.

7.5. FREQUENCY ASSESSMENT
7.5.1. GENERIC LEAK FREQUENCIES
Failure frequencies and event frequencies are determined for each isolatable section/failure cases
identified and associated events. Generic failure rates/ frequencies for various components are available
in various historical accidental databases such as OGP, Lastfire, etc. These frequencies in conjunction
with part count hole size distribution are used to determine the failure frequencies.
When historical data is not available, or only available for facilities operating in different circumstances, it
is necessary to use engineering judgement or rely on the opinion of experts to interpret data for
comparable equipment in order to make a best estimate. Such judgements should not be too optimistic
and should be clearly recorded and formalized as part of assumption register.
It is recommended that all generic leak frequencies used in the assessment from various databases are
documented in the assumption register, reviewed and approved prior to finalizing frequencies
assessment.
7.5.2. FAILURE FREQUENCIES
Failure frequencies are frequency of potentially hazardous events estimated from historical databases
(generic failure frequencies) combined with part count information for associated isolatable section and
failure scenarios (e.g. length of pipelines, number of valves/flanges, etc.).
Where the hazardous events being assessed involve releases of hazardous material from the isolatable
inventories, a parts count shall be undertaken to determine the number of potential leak sources of
hazardous process fluids within each isolatable section. The frequencies of loss of containment from each
leak source shall be taken from validated industry databases and combined with the parts count to
generate a failure frequency of leaks from each section. Part count methodology which shall be adopted
for determining failure frequencies is available in Appendix 1.
The leak frequency data should be apportioned by hole size by modelling the distribution of leaks across
a range of hole sizes divided into bands, where each band is represented by its hole diameter. These
values should be used as equivalent hole diameters when conducting the consequence and should also
be used to derive the failure frequency of each leak size from each isolatable section & failure case.
Part count sheets and summary of the total failure frequencies and various hole size frequencies shall be
made available as part of the QRA Report.

7.6.1. SOURCE TERM MODELLING
An important input to physical effects calculations is the source term, i.e. the rate at which hazardous

and toxic materials can be limited by ESD systems, in which case the release rate will decline with time.

discharge and how much is entrained by the gas. Unless the consequence modelling package can model

7.7. IMPACT ASSESSMENT
Exposure to various consequence identified as part of physical effect modelling can lead to major injury
or fatalities. As part of impact assessment, vulnerability of humans to the consequences of major hazard
events at onshore and offshore installations, primarily those producing and/or processing hazardous
fluids are established. The focus is on fatality criteria as QRAs generally address fatality risks, however
injury thresholds can also identified where appropriate. The impact assessment is generally expressed
in terms of lethality which is fraction/percentage of the exposed population who would suffer fatality on
exposure to a given consequence level. Typically probit which is a function that relates lethality to the
intensity or concentration of hazardous effect and the duration of exposure.
Generally following consequence are considered as part of impact assessment.
 Fire Scenarios (radiation levels)
 Explosions (blast overpressure)
 Toxic Gases (toxic gas exposure)
 Smoke (toxic fumes exposure)
Fire Scenarios:
Humans are vulnerable to fire in the following ways:
 Engulfment by the fire
 Thermal radiation from the fire (outside the fire)
 Inside a building that is exposed to fire/radiation

Marine Transport Risk Assessment
Marine transport risk is determined using the FAR rates. The FAR (fatalities per 1E8 exposed Hrs) where

FAR = 30 + (26/Transit Time Per Journey in Hrs)
This method is very high-level assessment but adopted for typical QRA report based on the guidelines
given in OGP Report 434 – 10.
Assumptions related to marine transportation are available in Appendix 1 of this standard.
Aviation Risk Assessment – Helicopter Transport
Aviation risk assessment includes determination of risk for helicopter and fixed wing transport risk
associated with facilities under consideration. Following methods are recommended.
Individual risk (IR) per journey = In-flight IR + Take-off & landing (TO/L) IR
In-flight IR = Accident frequency in-flight (per hour) × Flight time (hours) × Probability of fatal accident ×
Probability of death in fatal accident
TO/L IR = Accident frequency in TO/L (per flight stage) × No of flight stages per journey × Probability of
fatal accident × Probability of death in fatal accident
Assumptions related to aviation risk assessment are available in Appendix 1 of this standard.

7.13. BUILDING RISK ASSESSMENT
A BRA is carried out to determine risk levels at each building and to identify measures to eliminate, control,
and mitigate these risks. Detailed fire and explosion assessment for building is carried out as part of the
FERA study. In addition to these impacts, the effect of other MAH such as toxic, smoke impairment, etc.
shall be studied as part of the BRA. These impacts shall be studied for the Residential Buildings,
Accommodation Buildings and Industrial buildings which are either occupied or used post emergency for
EER measures. The overall summary of risk assessment shall be made available in QRA Report.
All industrial buildings shall be studied if they are impaired due to 0.5 LFL. Building is considered to be
impaired if the frequency of 0.5 LFL reaching the building is more than 1E-04 (risk based) or releases
from reasonably worst-case scenarios can reach the building (consequence based). In such events,
appropriate risk reduction measure shall be proposed (e.g. shutting down of electrical equipment,
personnel within these buildings shall be evacuated to safe location, designing the building as gas tight,
etc.)
Occupied industrial buildings shall be studied if they are impaired due to toxic gas AEGL 2 value. Building
is considered to be impaired if the frequency of AEGL 2 reaching the building is more than 1E-04 (risk
based) or releases from reasonably worst-case scenarios can reach the building (consequence based).
In such events, personnel within these buildings shall be evacuated to safe location (muster and/or TR).
If a building is required to be occupied in the event of emergency, in such cases, buildings shall be
designed as TR and meet the TRIA requirements as outlined in EERA Standard.
The BRA shall include the following:
1 Maximum credible flammable gas and toxic gas hazard (concentrations) at each building;
2 Toxic Concentration for each building at the 1E-4 per year frequency
3 Building Risk for occupants from all MAHs including fire, explosion, smoke, flammable and toxic
gas (assuming an individual is present in the building for 40 hrs/week, 52 weeks/year during EPC
stage as data may not be available, where manning data is available data should be utilised);
4 IRPA for maximum exposed person for each building.

7.16. SENSITIVITY ANALYSIS
Risk assessment studies are conducted based on the various assumptions made or outlined during
studies. These assumptions though are based on various databases, previous history, knowledge, know-
how of the facilities, needs to be tested to ensure that the risk lies within the ALARP region.
As part of QRA report, it is therefore required that sensitivity assessment is carried out for the key
assumptions. The new risk results are then compared with ADNOC risk acceptance criteria to confirm
that the risk lies within the Tolerable regionor ACCEPTABLE region.
It is recommended that QRA team and SME from the GC CHSE select the assumptions which needs to
undergo sensitivity test. Some of the assumptions can be determined at early stages of QRA which can
be formalized as part of assumption register. Sensitivity analysis is not necessary to be conducted for
projects/ facilities such as small brownfield modifications, wellhead tower, well pad areas, pressure
reducing stations, etc. where risk profile is understood and well within the tolerable region. Whereas for
greenfield projects, large facilities, offshore complexes, etc. sensitivity cases shall be carried out in
consultation with GC CHSE.
Typical assumption which can undergo sensitivity test/assessment are as below:
 Duration of ESD, BDV, etc.
 SEP Values for fire,
 Congestions levels for blast assessment,
 Part Counts – Number of flanges, piping length, etc.
 Ignition Probabilities
 Manning & Occupancy Factors
 Etc.
The sensitivity results shall be clearly presented in the form of LSIR, IRPA and PLL. Where large
workforce or public is involved F-N Curve shall also be produced.

7.17. ALARP DEMONSTRATION
ALARP demonstration is required when the calculated Individual Risk Per Annum (IRPA) for various
worker group lies within tolerable region as per ADNOC risk acceptability criteria. The QRA shall provide
a clear demonstration that the risk is or will be reduced to ALARP in accordance with ADNOC HSE Risk
Management Standard [Ref. 3].
ALARP process starts by identification of the Major Risk Contributors to given worker group or at a
location. These major risk contributors are further drilled down to determine the top contributors to risk.
Risk reduction measures are identified against these contributors to reduce the risk.
While identifying these risk reduction measures hierarchy of controls shall be maintained. Once these
major risk contributors are identified and risk reduction measures are highlighted, an ALARP workshop
shall be conducted to discuss and brainstorm these risk reduction measures or any additional risk
reduction measures.
All applicable recommendations measures shall be subjected to ALARP demonstration using CBA
approach as outlined in ADNOC HSE Risk Management Standard [Ref. 3].

7.20. QRA TEAM COMPETENCY
The QRA team shall consist of lead engineer and support engineer(s) and shall have following
competencies as minimum.
 QRA Lead shall be well aware & have detailed technical knowledge of various models &
assumptions which are used in the industry for performing these activities and shall have
experience of 10 years or more in performing risk assessment & QRA studies.
 QRA engineers shall have minimum experience of 5 years in modelling performing activities.
QRA lead shall be responsible for technical quality, QA-QC, regulatory compliance etc. QRA is a highly
technical discipline exercise and parts of some assessments, such as detailed explosion modelling, can
be beyond the abilities of general risk analysts and specialist expertise shall be sought.
Some of the issues that should be considered while determining the competence requirements are as
below.
Persons using consequence models should:
1 be familiar & have technical understanding of the models and understand its basic assumptions;
2 know when the model is being used outside its range of validity;
3 understand when it is no longer valid to represent a range of possible incidents by a single idealised
scenario; and
4 understand the sensitivities of the model and how these relate to reality.
Persons determining the frequency or probability of events, including the production of fault trees and
event trees, should:
1 understand the level of detail required to fulfil the goal of the study;
2 understand the laws of probability including Boolean algebra;
3 be able to interact with engineers and operators to obtain a good understanding of how the systems
they are considering work and how they can fail;
4 have a good general understanding of past incidents;
5 have knowledge of the available base data and its limitations; and
6 be able to distinguish results that are not statistically significant.
Persons calculating or interpreting risks should:
1 understand the various forms of risk presentation and the associated criteria and their meaning;
2 be familiar with background risk levels;
3 understand the ALARP principle;
4 understand the limitations caused by uncertainty;
5 know how to draw out important practical conclusions that result in reduced risk; and
6 understand the application of cost benefit analysis.
