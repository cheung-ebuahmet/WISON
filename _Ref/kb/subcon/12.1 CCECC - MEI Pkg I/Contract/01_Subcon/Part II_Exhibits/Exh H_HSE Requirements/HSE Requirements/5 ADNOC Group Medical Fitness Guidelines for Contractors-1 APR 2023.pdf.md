2. INTRODUCTION
2.1 ADNOC operates across the entire hydrocarbon value chain, through a network of fully integrated
businesses, with interests that range from exploration, production, storage, refining and distribution, to
the development of a wide range of petrochemical products.
2.2 ADNOC has played an integral role in Abu Dhabi’s economic development; managing, producing and
preserving the Emirate’s hydrocarbon reserves on behalf of the Abu Dhabi Government. ADNOC is the
principal catalyst of growth in the Emirate and has had a positive and lasting impact on the quality of
millions of lives.
3. PURPOSE
3.1 These Guidelines are intended to serve as guidance on effective and efficient approaches to promote and
protect the health and safety of ADNOC Group contractors in line with ADNOC operating values and
cultural attributes.
3.2 The purpose of this Guidelines is:
3.2.1. to provide improved access to comprehensive occupational health services to ADNOC Group
contractors.
3.2.2. to ensure continued delivery of quality healthcare services to the contracted workforce enabling
them to maintain the highest possible degree of physical, mental health and social wellbeing; and
3.2.3. to ensure that ADNOC Group occupational health performance is in par with the best international
industry standards available.
4. SCOPE
4.1 The Guidelines shall apply to all contractors working or applying to work for ADNOC Group.
4.2 The fitness criteria set out in the Guidelines are the minimum requirements for occupational health
assessments/evaluation in ADNOC Group and should not be considered as all inclusive. Additional
criteria may be included based on risk assessment as determined by the nature of the job and its location.
4.3 The fitness criteria shall also be applicable to assessments which occur at different stages of employment
within the ADNOC Group. This includes pre-employment/pre-placement, pre-enrolment, specific jobs
assessments, periodic medical assessment, referrals for an occupational health assessment for
continuing fitness for work, transfers, service extension, end-of-service and disability assessments (see
specific sections below).
4.4 Where fitness criteria are not described (for a specific job type) in these Guidelines, comparable industry
standards and/or international best practice for equivalent jobs can be referred to as criteria for
assessment.
5. USE OF THE GUIDELINES AND DECISIONS ON FITNESS FOR WORK
5.1 Occupational Medical assessments are either required by law or performed to fulfil ADNOC’s legal, social
and economic responsibilities towards safeguarding the health and safety of its personnel and operations.
5.2 The Guidelines should hence be used as a guidance document for Occupational Medical assessments
and are not meant to represent a programmed inclusion or exclusion of an individual worker from a
specified job.
5.3 Accordingly, decisions on fitness for work shall not be made solely on the presence of a diagnostic label.
Various other interrelated factors shall also be taken into consideration including those that can affect the

5.5.2. The individual

5.5.3. The job
5.5.3.1. The nature of the work, and the work tasks;
5.5.3.2. The extent of exposure to hazards and risks for the job, plus the extent of preventive

reduction of risks where feasible (the ALARP principle). Where there is residual risk,
then suitability of individuals (for such work) will be assessed taking into account other
related factors such as possible aggravation of ill-health following continuing exposure
to residual risks;
5.5.3.3. The location of the job: In the oil and gas industry there are special factors that are
unique to offshore work (such as on rigs, platforms, on board ships, and on islands)
where evacuation for emergency care if indicated, can be difficult, time consuming and
costly;
5.5.3.4. On-shore work in ‘remote’ areas: For occupational health considerations the main factor
is not necessarily designation of a work location as ‘remote’ for administrative and pay
reasons, but ease of access to these locations and ease and speed of transport for any
emergency secondary or tertiary care. The harsh desert environment in some ‘remote’
areas may also not be conducive for individuals with pre-existing health conditions,
especially during the hot summer months where ambient temperatures can exceed 50
degrees Centigrade, and sandstorms are a regular occurrence;
5.5.3.5. Town based jobs (including jobs at headquarters and at industrial sites such as
Mussafah industrial city): These are locations often close to where individuals live, and
therefore with ease of access to medical facilities (including availability of family and
community support). They are also in proximity to hospitals providing secondary and/or
tertiary care services;
5.5.3.6. Decisions on fitness for work should specify:
5.5.3.6.1. Suitability for the job (taking into account the job tasks/specifications), and

7. OCCUPATIONAL HEALTH SERVICES
7.1 Pre-Employment Medical Assessment
7.1.1. Pre-employment medical assessments shall be conducted for all recruits to ensure that they are
fit for the job and tasks to which they will be assigned. The main objective is to make sure that
the individual is fit to perform job tasks safely and effectively (without risk to their own or others’
health and safety) and that they do not have clinical conditions that can be aggravated by their
work. Fitness for work should be judged against the general fitness criteria as described in these
Guidelines and against additional requirements (if any) for that particular job. This principle shall
also apply to other medical assessments outlined in the subsequent (following) sections.

7.3 Change of Job Medical Assessment
7.3.1 Change of job medical assessments shall be carried out at all times where the individual is
assigned to a new job and/or to a new work environment. In this regard the change of job medical
assessment has special relevance where the new job and work environment can pose significant
risk to the health and safety of the contractor and others in the workplace.
7.3.2 The fitness for work shall be assessed against the general fitness criteria described in this
Guidelines and where there are specific requirements for the particular job, against the additional
requirements for that post.
7.4 Post Sickness or Return to Work Assessment
7.4.1 Return to work medical assessments shall be performed for individuals who have been off work
due to sickness or injury (both occupational and non-occupational) for more than two (2) calendar
weeks continuously. The examining physician should evaluate the fitness to work based on the
report of treating physician with due consideration on the ability of the individual to perform job
tasks.
7.4.2 The post-sickness return to work medical assessment of all offshore workers is mandatory
regardless of the duration of sick leave.

7.5.1 End of service medical assessments are conducted to assess if individuals are entitled to special

7.6 Extension of Service beyond Normal Retirement Age
7.6.1 Extension of service medical assessments are carried out during particular instances when
company management requires continuation of a worker’s employment beyond ADNOC’s normal
retirement age.
7.7 Remote Area and Offshore Visit Medical Assessment
7.7.1 Contractors requiring remote area/offshore visits should have a valid fitness to work certificate.
They shall hence undergo additional tests depending on the location or the purpose of the visit.
7.7.2 Simple medical screening procedures (e.g. physical examination, vital signs, visual acuity, history)
may be adopted for individuals undertaking short-duration visits (<24hrs) to remote and offshore
locations (Furnace et al, 2008). For those undertaking a remote/offshore area visit longer than 24
hours, complete medical examination shall be conducted in accordance with these Guidelines.
7.8 Health assessments at Company Management Request
7.8.1 Medical assessments at management request can be conducted for example, for an individual
7.8.1.1 With prolonged sickness absence, or frequent spells of sickness absence, especially
where work performance has been affected;
7.8.1.2 Where there are health and safety concerns; and
7.8.1.3 Where there is evidence that drug/alcohol misuse at work has occurred.
7.8.2 Medical assessments requested by the management shall be carried out by an Occupational
Medicine Physician.
7.9 Alcohol and Drug Screening
7.9.1 Drug and alcohol screening shall be completed on designated candidates based on the technical
work condition or job type. It may also be performed on personnel performing HSE Critical Roles
including fire fighters, divers, seafarers, radiation workers, emergency responders etc. based on
the risk assessment and/or at the discretion of the concerned ADNOC Group to which the
individual is affiliated. All such requests shall be processed in accordance with UAE legislations,
UAE Labor Law, applicable Department of Health (DOH) standards and ADNOC policies and
procedures.
7.9.2 In general, applicable provisions of the Oil and Gas Contractor Drug and Alcohol Testing
guidelines (2016) - International Association of Oil & Gas Producers shall be applied for alcohol
and Drug Screening.

7.10 Occupational Health Surveillance and Biological Monitoring
7.10.1 Risk related health surveillance is conducted in conjunction with the Occupational Health Risk
Assessment (OHRA) and implemented for similar exposure groups (SEG) with extreme, high and
medium occupational health risks, where such risks cannot be reduced at source.
7.10.2 The frequency and content of occupational health surveillance and biological monitoring will
depend on the risk of exposure, the extent of control of hazards, compliance with preventive
measures, and on the regulatory and legal requirements.
7.10.3 The procedures for occupational health risk assessment, occupational health surveillance and
biological monitoring shall be in accordance with applicable ADNOC standards on occupational
health management.
7.10.4 UAE Labor Law also requires six (6) monthly medical assessments for individuals who are at risk
of exposure to a list of prescribed agents.
7.11 Disability Assessment
7.11.1 Disability assessments are carried out to assess the extent of disability in accordance with
applicable regulations and guidelines (such as the AMA Guide on Evaluation of permanent
impairment). Such assessments shall provide the basis for determining the disability benefits (if
payable).
7.11.2 Disability is a physical or mental impairment that has a substantial and long-term negative effect
on someone’s ability to do normal daily activities. Substantial is more than minor or trivial. Long-
term means 12 months or more.
7.11.3 The below are eligible for disability assessment:
7.11.3.1 Employees after work related injuries;
7.11.3.2 Employees after general accidents
7.11.4 Disability assessment can be conducted by:
7.11.4.1 AMA Guides Impairment Rating certified physicians; or
7.11.4.2 Trained or experienced physicians based on the AMA Guides Impairment Rating.
7.11.5 The suitable time to conduct a disability assessment is after reaching Maximum Medical
Improvement (MMI) or after one year from the incident (UAE Labor Law).
7.12 Disability Assessment after Work-related Injuries and Diseases
7.12.1 Disability assessments will be done by Occupational Medicine Specialist after the injured worker
has reached maximum medical improvement (MMI). The request will be sent from the employing
company for an impairment rating and then sent to the employing company for compensation.
7.12.2 The injured worker shall not be entitled to a compensation for the injury or disability:
7.12.2.1 If it is proven in the investigations of the competent authorities that the worker
deliberately injured himself with the intention of committing suicide or of obtaining
compensation;
7.12.2.2 If the worker at the time of the incident was under the influence of drugs or alcohol;

7.14 Occupational illness Investigation
7.14.1 Cases of suspected or confirmed occupational illnesses should be investigated in association
with health, environmental, safety and occupational hygiene specialists. Any relevant preventive
measures should be considered and implemented as appropriate.
7.14.2 The investigations shall be carried out in accordance with applicable ADNOC HSE Standards.
7.15 Occupational Illness Reporting
7.15.1 Suspected cases of occupational illness shall be reported to a nominated competent person (with
the respective company) who will consider each case and coordinate appropriate follow-up. The
company shall collate their occupational health statistics and provide appropriate reports to
ADNOC. Guidelines on reporting are detailed in ADNOC HSE Standards 2019 HSE OH ST04.
7.16 Immunization
7.16.1 The Department of Health Abu Dhabi (DOH) mandates immunization for workers in specific jobs
(e.g., food handlers, barbers, healthcare workers, sewage workers). Where such jobs are relevant
within ADNOC Group, the occupational health service provider will facilitate such immunizations.
Relevant immunizations and travel advice can also be provided for individuals required to travel
abroad for company business, or for specific jobs (e.g. fire fighters, emergency response team
members), as indicated. Please also refer to the specific section on Vaccination.

8. MEDICAL RECORDS AND CONFIDENTIALITY OF INFORMATION
Medical records shall be kept strictly confidential and access to such records should be given only to
authorized persons who require access to such information and records for patient care purposes. Medical
records / health data retention and disposal practices (regardless of the media – paper, electronics, and
films) shall be carried out with the utmost care and diligence and shall at all times be in accordance with
applicable ADNOC data retention policies as well as local and federal regulatory requirements.
9. MEDICAL ASSESSMENT
9.1 General Provisions
9.1.1 At pre-employment assessment, any existing condition that may have considerable impact on the
health and safety of the individual, co-workers and/or third parties, or the organization will be
generally unacceptable. This will apply especially for chronic conditions other than those that are
acute (see sections on specific conditions).
9.1.2 For individuals who are deemed fit at pre-employment and hired, but subsequently develop an
illness or disability (work-related or otherwise), efforts will be made to accommodate and retain
these individuals where feasible.
9.2 Medical Assessment Contents
9.2.1 Medical assessments shall be carried out by the attending physician. Female candidates should
be examined by a female physician. Where a female physician is not available, assessments can
be performed by a male physician in the presence of a female chaperone e.g., a female nurse.
Written consent of the candidate should be obtained prior to physical examination.
9.2.2 Physical examination results should be documented in the appropriate medical exanimation form.
Minimum standard tests for specific job types are provided in Appendix-B. Additional tests (e.g.,
stress-ECG, spirometry, etc.) may be performed as determined by the examining physician based
on the clinical indications.

10. GENERAL FITNESS CRITERIA
10.1 Head & Neck Disorders
Individuals with abnormalities preventing the effective wearing of protective devices (e.g. helmets,
earmuffs, self-contained breathing apparatus [SCBA], and respirators) shall not be accepted for work
where the use of such protective equipment is mandatory.
10.2 Eyes
Visual acuity adequate to permit the individual to mobilise and work safely is essential and shall be
assessed at each medical examination. Corrected visual acuity should be sufficient to perform normal
work duties. Individuals with acuity of at least 6/60 will normally meet this requirement. For individuals
with an uncorrected acuity less than this the examining physician should satisfy himself by practical testing

ADNOC Classification: Internal
that they have a sufficient acuity to effectively mobilise in case of emergency without the use of corrective
spectacles (Furnace et al, 2008).
Higher standards of visual acuity are required for specific roles, including drivers, crane and forklift
operators, divers, emergency response workers and seafarers. These standards are provided in the
relevant sections of this Guidelines.
Individuals who require lenses to meet the visual standards should be encouraged to carry at least two
pairs in the event of emergency mobilisation offshore and at Remote Locations.
10.2.1 Monocular vision:
Can be accepted for employment, provided the minimum standard of vision acuity is met,
appropriate adaptation to the loss of binocular vision is demonstrated, and the risks associated
with the job are as low as reasonably practicable (Furnace et al, 2008). Adequate eye protection
should be provided and used to protect the monocular eye, as necessary.
Diplopia, if persistent, is not suitable for jobs based offshore and at Remote Locations. For town-
based office jobs diplopia is acceptable, provided the individual demonstrates adequate
adaptation to the work environment and can perform routine tasks. Appropriate specialist advice
should be obtained, as necessary.
10.2.2 Visual fields:
Should be full and unrestricted for specific jobs such as crane operators, drivers, divers and those
required to work at heights (Furnace et al, 2008).
10.2.3 Colour Vision:
Individuals with colour vision deficiency shall be assessed (e.g. by a trade test) for the ability to
perform tasks requiring colour discrimination. Fine colour discrimination is required for seafarers
performing lookout duties, electrical workers, fire fighters, emergency response team members
and divers. Individuals with colour vision deficiency can be considered for jobs not requiring fine
colour discrimination.
10.2.4 Glaucoma:
Which is adequately controlled and has not compromised visual acuity may be acceptable, but
specialist referral and a temporary period of restricted duties may be required.
10.2.5 Corneal dystrophies:
As a general rule, individuals with progressive corneal dystrophies are not suitable for offshore
and remote area work. Individuals with successful corneal transplants, preserved visual acuity
and good long-term prognosis can be accepted for indoor work. Tasks with a potential risk of eye
injuries should be avoided.
10.2.6 Retinal disorders:
As a general rule, individuals with progressive retinal disorder and poor prognosis are not suitable
to work in offshore and remote areas.
10.3 Ears
The functional impact on the individual’s ability to hear and communicate, as well as any impact on
balance, must be carefully assessed in addition any underlying pathology. Pure tone audiometry (bone
and air conduction) is the standard test used for assessing hearing ability.

10.3.2 Chronic suppurative ear infections
Normally acceptable, if adequately treated. Particular care must be taken in respect of food
handlers.
10.3.3 Disorders of the tympanic membrane
For example dry perforations and grommets and the middle ear require further assessment.
Individuals with tympanic membrane perforation, chronic ear canal or middle ear disease will be

Organic or functional neurological disorders causing, or likely to cause, any significant defect of
consciousness, cognitive function, muscular power, balance, mobility, sensation or coordination should
be carefully assessed in regard to work capability and safety.
In assessing individuals with psychiatric disorders, the examining physician should consider the potential
for exacerbation of the condition by the social and physical environment unique to offshore and Remote
Locations. The examining physician should make clinical assessment of the functional effect of symptoms
such as mood, memory, concentration, agitation, psychosomatic symptoms, behavioural disturbance and
side effects of medication (Furnace et al, 2008).
10.4.1 Headache:
Individuals with tension headaches and migraines without incapacitating complications are
normally suitable for employment. Severe cases of headaches should be carefully evaluated by
the examining physician and referred for specialist assessment before a certificate of fitness is

10.5 Respiratory System Disorders
The workplace may cause respiratory disease(s) or exacerbate pre-existing lung disease. It is important to
try and identify the specific workplace factor(s) that cause or aggravate respiratory disorders, since further
reduction or removal of such exposures, where feasible, may enable the affected worker to continue in their
current job. Respiratory disease(s) can limit functional capacity and the ability to perform specific tasks such
as HSE Critical Roles, e.g., fire fighters, emergency response workers, and other jobs requiring the
mandatory use of breathing apparatus. In such instances, the presence of respiratory disorders and
coexisting disease(s) may be incompatible with the safe performance of these jobs, in which case
discontinuation or redeployment is indicated. Assessment of the respiratory system can often be made on
clinical grounds alone, although special tests e.g., spirometry, chest X-rays and blood tests may provide
additional support for confirming the diagnosis. Where special investigations are unavailable to the
examining physician, these tests may be requested through referral to a pulmonologist/respiratory physician
following which a report should be obtained for any follow-up action by the examining physician.
10.5.1 General Fitness to work requirements
In assessing the impact of respiratory disease on an individual’s ability to work offshore and
remote areas, the occupational physician must consider the following:
10.5.1.1 Exercise tolerance with specific reference to general mobility (including climbing stairs),
ability to perform normal job functions, ability to respond to emergency situations and
successfully take part in evacuations;
10.5.1.2 Risk of developing sudden life-threatening complications and likelihood of successful
medical evacuation;
10.5.1.3 Ability to take part in survival training including exposure to smoke filled environments,
wearing smoke hoods and BA equipment, helicopter underwater escape training and

10.5.1.4 Consideration of potential exposure to respiratory irritants and sensitizers.

10.5.3 Lung Function testing for Offshore Personnel
All offshore personnel who travel by helicopter to an offshore installation are required to be trained
in the use of CA-EBS (Emergency Compressed Air Breathing System).
This means that all offshore personnel must undergo a lung-function examination prior to
participating in a training course to establish that there is no increased risk in following the training.
A lung-function examination must be conducted for every offshore examination whereby a
minimum of a FEV1, FVC and FEV1 / FVC ratio and flow-volume curve is performed. The case
history must also be well discussed to check for the presence of (old) lung problems such as:
pneumothorax, asthma, COPD, medication etc. Participation in a CA-EBS “in-water” training is
only possible if the participant has undergone the lung-function examination prior to the training,

personnel is medically unfit for participation in the CA-EBS “wet” training but is otherwise
medically fit for working offshore, this must be indicated. This allows them to participate in a CA-
EBS “dry” training and they can thereby meet the requirements for the trainings-criteria for
offshore work.

10.5.5 Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)
It is characterized by chronic obstruction of lung airflow that is not fully reversible. The more
familiar terms ‘chronic bronchitis’ and ‘emphysema’ are no longer used but are now included
within the COPD diagnosis (WHO; NICE, 2010). The relevant symptoms are exertional
breathlessness, chronic cough, regular sputum production, frequent winter ‘bronchitis’ and
wheeze. The condition should be assessed with particular consideration given to the degree of
impairment, the prognosis, comorbidities (e.g., asthma, anaemia, obesity), family history,
smoking status, functional work capacity and workplace factors, and any mandatory requirement
for use of respiratory protective equipment (e.g., SCBA, filter type respirators).
A diagnosis of COPD is indicated by the occurrence of persistent airflow limitation i.e., FEV1/FVC
ratio<70%. (FEV1 – Forced expiratory volume in 1 second; FVC – Forced vital capacity).
In general, individuals with mild COPD and well-controlled symptoms can be considered fit for
employment. However, such individuals should not be involved in firefighting and emergency
response work and other tasks requiring the mandatory use of a breathing apparatus.
Moderate COPD with well-controlled symptoms is suitable for employment in locations with easy
access to secondary care.
Cases of severe COPD are unacceptable at pre-employment assessment. For in-service
individuals who develop COPD, there may be limited scope for alternative jobs. Regular review
(e.g., annually) may be appropriate for assessment of the health status and continuing fitness for
work.
10.5.6 Chronic restrictive pulmonary diseases
It includes interstitial fibrosis, extrinsic allergic alveolitis, silicosis and asbestosis. The severity of
these conditions shall be a key factor in deciding on fitness for work. Where necessary, the
examining physician should have the knowledge of the job tasks and work environment and
obtain the advice of a pulmonologist/chest physician regarding progression of disease, treatment,
and prognosis. Where occupational exposure to agents causing pulmonary fibrosis (e.g.,
asbestos, silica) is suspected, an appropriate occupational health risk assessment and
investigation is indicated to determine the source(s) of exposure and implement preventive
actions accordingly.
10.5.7 Pneumothorax
The occupational physician will carefully review the detailed medical report from the
pulmonologist or treating consultant, status of individual’s health and his tasks as well as control
of any underlying medical condition before issuing a fitness decision.
10.5.7.1 Spontaneous pneumothorax (primary or secondary): can return to town-based jobs or
sites with secondary care in non-manual roles following complete recovery.
10.5.7.2 Traumatic pneumothorax can be considered for remote area and offshore work and
would be dependent on the severity and prognosis.

10.5.8 Pulmonary tuberculosis (PTB)
Where active and open, is not acceptable for employment until successfully treated and certified

Especially those that are highly infectious or with high mortality, such as some of the newly

away from the workplace until full recovery has occurred and the case has been confirmed as

10.6 Cardiovascular System and Peripheral Circulation Disorders
Cardiovascular diseases (CVD), including stroke and hypertension are a major cause of morbidity and
mortality in working populations. CVD can affect (a) fitness for work due to limitation of working capacity
or (b) the risk of sudden incapacitation, e.g. cardiac arrest, stroke, and (c) the need for emergency
evacuation from offshore and Remote Locations. Hence, it is important to evaluate at pre-employment
and subsequent medical examinations the prognosis of any CVD, the effect on the individual’s working
ability and the potential for sudden incapacitation. The examining physician should also assess the
candidate’s mobility, ability to perform essential job tasks (e.g. use of SCBA for fire fighters) and respond
to emergency situations.
Where appropriate, specialist advice should be obtained. Regular review should be carried out to ensure
continuing fitness for work.
10.6.1 Congenital heart diseases such as atrial or ventricular septal defects, resolved spontaneously or
surgically without any residual complications, are generally suitable for employment. Other
congenital cardiac abnormalities are generally unsuitable for employment. Employment of such
cases would depend up on the severity of the condition, the success of treatment and any residual
effects.
10.6.2 Valvular heart diseases
Applicants detected at pre-employment with significant functional or symptomatic abnormality of
any of the heart valves are generally unacceptable for employment.
Cases of valvular heart diseases detected at pre-employment or for current personnel that have
been surgically treated, or those that are mild and non-progressive can be considered for
employment in town-based jobs or locations with ready access to secondary care. Such
individuals should have a good prognosis, no limitations of physical activity and no complications,
e.g. heart failure or intractable arrhythmias. The work tasks should not cause any limitations or
effects on physical fitness that could result in heart failure.
Individuals with prosthetic valves (mechanical or bioprosthetic) on anticoagulant treatments are
unacceptable for work in remote and offshore locations.
In all cases of valvular heart diseases detailed cardiology report must be obtained in order to fully
understand the condition, its hemodynamic defect and impacts upon exercise tolerance.
10.6.3 Hypertension that is uncomplicated and well-controlled with or without medication is generally
acceptable for employment. Hypertension that is complicated or uncontrolled should be assessed
as to the likelihood for improvement with good prognosis following treatment and the ability to

associated with mechanical and/or electrical dysfunctions which exhibit inappropriate ventricular
hypertrophy or dilatation and are due to a variety of causes, often genetic. (Ramrakha & Hill).
Cardiomyopathies are generally not compatible with remote area and offshore work, or for
firefighting and emergency response duties. Town-based office work may be acceptable but
should be subject to assessment of the degree of impairment, prognosis and physical capability
for work performance.
10.6.6 Cardiac hypertrophy and dilatations shall be assessed in conjunction with the underlying disease
and potential for functional impairment and limitation of working capacity. Appropriate referral
should be considered for evaluation of cardiac functional capacity. Individuals with good
prognosis, preserved cardiac functions, without limitations of physical activity and without
complications (e.g. heart failure or intractable arrhythmias) may be considered fit for employment.
10.6.7 Cardiac arrhythmias and conduction defects, if symptomatic, should be referred for expert cardiac
opinion. Such cases are not acceptable for employment in remote and offshore areas.
Asymptomatic occasional, non-recurrent arrhythmias (especially those detected by ECG alone
without any other accompanying clinical features) are generally acceptable for employment if they
do not cause sudden incapacitation or if no treatment is required. Individuals with a history of
arrhythmia with potential life-threatening complications (e.g. sick sinus syndrome, Wolf-
Parkinson-White syndrome, ventricular extrasystoles and tachycardia) should be assessed as to
the likelihood of sudden incapacitation, and the ability for work performance. Work in locations
with ready access to secondary care is generally acceptable.
10.6.8 Individuals with pacemakers and implanted cardioverter defibrillators (ICD) are not acceptable for
employment. In-service individuals can be acceptable for town-based jobs or locations with ready
access to secondary care. Appropriate specialist advice should be obtained, as necessary.

10.6.8.2 Written clarification by the treating cardiologist or manufacturer of the pacemaker or
ICD indicating the risk of exposure of the pacemaker or ICD to electro-magnetic fields.
10.6.8.3 Report by the treating physician confirming that the individual is free of syncopal attacks
and has no significant risk of syncope or other complications following insertion of the
pacemaker or ICD.
10.6.9 Aortic aneurysm, dissection, and occlusion are not acceptable for employment. For in-service
individuals, these conditions are not compatible with remote area and offshore work or for
firefighting and emergency response duties. For town-based office work, this can be considered
subject to expert cardiac opinion regarding assessment of the degree of impairment, prognosis
and likelihood of complications (e.g. rupture, bleeding, stroke, haemorrhagic shock), and physical
capability for work performance.

10.6.12.1 A normal heart’s ejection fraction is between 50-75% (however, heart failure can still

10.6.12.3 An ejection fraction between 40 - 50% is borderline and may result from a previous

Individuals with haematological disorders should be carefully assessed as symptoms associated with

likelihood of frequent absenteeism, and emergency medical evacuation from offshore and remote sites.

carried out to ensure continuing fitness for work.

mechanisms are suitable for employment (Smedley and Kaczmarski, 2013). With identification
and treatment of the cause of anaemia, there should be no need for restrictions on work activity
or location.

Digestive system disorders can limit the capacity to perform job duties and result in frequent and long-
term sickness absence due to recurrent symptoms, treatment regimes, or surgical intervention. Symptoms
can be aggravated by the unique work environment, shift work, hazardous exposures, psychosocial
factors or the nature and quality of food in remote and offshore locations. The examining physician should

Where appropriate, the examining physician should obtain specialist advice. Regular review should be

prognosis before being placed especially in offshore and remote areas.

treated. For those with chronic and recurrent pancreatitis in remission, the length of remission
and the severity of frequency of relapses will influence the decision to place an individual
especially for offshore and Remote Locations.
10.8.9 Cholelithiasis, if associated with severe or recurrent symptoms, requiring special diet or
medication is unacceptable until satisfactorily treated and reassessed.
10.8.10 Liver cirrhosis with an increased risk of oesophageal varices or ascites, or where continuing

with ease of access to secondary care may be considered.

10.8.13 Hepatitis C is caused by the Hepatitis C virus (HCV). Acute HCV infection is defined as the
presence of HCV within the 6 months of exposure. Spontaneous clearance of acute HCV infection
usually occurs within six months of infection, although >50% of infected persons can remain as
chronic carriers (WHO, 2016). Untreated chronic HCV can result in liver cirrhosis, liver failure and
hepatocellular carcinoma. HCV is spread predominantly by percutaneous or mucosal exposure
to infected blood and body fluids. At risk jobs are those as described for Hepatitis B above.

be a marker of past or recent HCV infection. The presence of HCV RNA indicates active infection
(ref. WHO 2016; Getchell, 2013).

10.9 Genitourinary System Disorders
A history of short-term genitourinary disorder will usually present no difficulties for offshore or remote site
work. Chronic or recurrent genitourinary disease should be carefully assessed with attention to
compromised renal function, acid-base and electrolyte imbalance, and other indicators of renal
insufficiency. Further investigations that are required but not available to the occupational physician
should be done in consultation with appropriate clinical specialists.
Employment of individuals with progressive and chronic renal disease with impaired renal function is
unacceptable for safety-critical jobs, work requiring strenuous physical effort, and especially those in
outdoor work areas (e.g. offshore, remote) with exposure to harsh environmental conditions. These
include fire fighters, emergency response team members, heavy duty vehicle drivers, and crane
operators.
10.9.1 Haematuria
Gross haematuria can indicate serious underlying disease, including malignancy, and warrants
thorough investigation before placing the individual in work or returning an in-service individual to
work. Macroscopic haematuria should be referred for further assessment and not certified fit for
work until full investigations and any treatment required completed successfully.
Asymptomatic, microscopic haematuria can be an incidental finding that by itself is not necessarily
abnormal. It is common in young individuals when it is rarely associated with any pathology, but
it is a better predictor of malignancy in older individuals, especially men over 40years of age
(NHS, 2008). Transient microscopic haematuria (<1+) (e.g. associated with exercise,
menstruation, viral illness) should be tested after 1 week and certified fit for work if the result of
repeat test is negative or <1+.
Recurrent microscopic haematuria (≥1+) should be referred for further investigation and not
certified fit for work until investigated clinically and any treatment, if required, completed
successfully. Individuals with very low likelihood of serious underlying condition(s) would
generally be fit for employment.

10.9.7 Renal function assessment
Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) can be used as an indicator of early renal damage.
However, it is not as useful in those who have significant muscle mass (such as body builders)
or individuals with muscle-wasting diseases. eGFR may also be affected by drugs such as
gentamicin, cisplatin or cefoxitin. eGFR together with the albumin/creatinine ratio (ACR) can be
used to assess renal function in individuals with a history of renal dysfunction (e.g. secondary to
diabetes, hypertension, recurrent renal calculi). Referral for specialist assessment is
recommended based on abnormal eGFR and ACR in accordance with applicable international
guidelines (Lamier et al, 2013; NIHCE, 2014).

severity, treatment, or prognosis of skin disorders. Affected individuals must inform the occupational

powder-free varieties can be part of a long-term strategy for prevention of latex allergy (NHS Plus,
2008).
Irritant contact dermatitis can usually be treated and prevented. Referral to a dermatologist may
be appropriate to assess whether contact dermatitis is allergic or irritant, to evaluate the degree
of impairment, and to determine prognosis. The ability to perform certain job tasks could require
discussion between the occupational health practitioner and the dermatologist. Jobs with
exposure to skin irritants include drilling and maintenance crew, cleaners and construction
workers. For in-service individuals, relocation should be considered where contact with the
causative agent cannot be avoided despite adequate control measures in place.
10.11.3 Psoriasis controlled by topical medications is acceptable for most jobs. Severe psoriasis
requiring hospitalization, systemic treatment and immunosuppressant therapy should be carefully
assessed, especially matching the compatibility of offshore and remote work requirements with
treatment regimes. Severe cases include those with frequent exacerbations, a history of
childhood psoriasis and involving a large area of the skin (more than 40% of skin surface being
affected) (Ferriday & Foulds, 2013). These severe cases should be referred to a dermatologist
regarding treatment regimes, duration and prognosis.

10.11.4 Photodermatoses (immunologically mediated, chemical-induced; genetic; or photoaggravation

inducing photosensitivity (e.g. chemicals, medication) can be avoided or prevented, such

where needed for job suitability and skin protective measures used to prevent direct skin exposure
to sunlight.
10.11.5 Disorders of pigmentation (e.g. vitiligo) may be complicated by exposure to direct sunlight. This
is an important consideration especially where most of the work is performed outdoors. Vitiligo
can present in localised or generalised forms. Individuals with vitiligo have less protection against
direct sunlight. In most cases, such individuals can work outdoors provided appropriate skin
protection is used (e.g. long sleeve coverall, head and neck covers, sunblock creams). Where
the risk of exposure is unavoidable and the disease is extensive, with poor prognosis despite
treatment, such individuals should be advised against employment outdoors. Jobs with primarily
indoor work activities and minimal exposure to sunlight can be accepted.
10.11.6 Severe acne, widespread and especially nodulo-cystic forms are not suitable for work outdoors,
due to the risk of frequent exposure to the hot and humid environment contributing to
exacerbations. Deployment to remote areas and offshore work can be considered following
successful treatment. Town-based postings are generally acceptable. Some acneiform lesions
may result from exposure to chlorinated compounds and oil-based products. In such cases, the
occupational history is important in improving prospects for recovery and prevention.
10.11.7 Infectious skin diseases are important for certain jobs. Food-handlers in particular should be
free of infection, e.g. staphylococcal infection of the hands. Cases of recordable and reportable
skin infections (e.g. scabies) should be notified to the DOH in accordance with the DOH
Notification system.
10.12 Musculoskeletal System Disorders
In assessing disorders of the musculoskeletal system, a careful assessment shall be made of the impact
on the individual’s functional capacity, not only to complete their intended work, but also activities which
are an essential part of offshore and remote area work. This may include emergency mobilisation (e.g. by
helicopter), ability to move up and down flights of stairs, climbing ladders easily and repetitively as
necessary, emergency response and other related drills. Functional assessments of mobility should be
supplemented where necessary by a specialist report on severity, treatment and prognosis.
Irrespective of pathology, all musculoskeletal disorders should be assessed with a focus in the following
(Furnace et al, 2008):
(a) Locomotor function;
(b) Balance and co-ordination;
(c) Stability of joints and risk of subluxation and dislocation;
(d) Ability to wear personal protective equipment; and
(e) Side effects of medication.

meet the mobility requirements of the work location. Appropriate arrangements should be made
for such individuals in the case of an emergency (Furnace et al, 2008).
10.13 Malignant Neoplasms
Individuals with cancer who have been treated successfully with full recovery and are asymptomatic can
be returned to their usual work duties. Those who are undergoing treatment and in remission can be
considered for return to work, depending on the condition, the treatment plan, prognosis, work
requirements and the ability to perform the full range of job tasks. Individuals with overt malignant disease
with poor prognosis are unlikely to seek or continue in full-time work. Where appropriate, the examining
physician should obtain specialist advice. Regular review should be carried out to ensure continuing
fitness for work.
In all cases of malignancy, the examining physician should assess the following, especially for work in
offshore and remote environment:
10.13.1 On-going medical treatment and possible side effects, and whether it can be delivered
effectively;
10.13.2 The likelihood of and significant complications (e.g. haemorrhage, seizure or loss of
consciousness; and
10.13.3 The likelihood of sudden incapacitating illness requiring effective medical intervention.
Fire fighters and emergency response team members with malignant disease that is newly diagnosed,
untreated, or currently being treated, or under active surveillance due to an increased risk of reoccurrence
or concerns over safe performance of essential duties are generally not suitable for employment.

10.14 Infectious Diseases
Minor infectious disease without the risk of spread is likely to be acceptable for remote area and offshore
work. However, all individuals should be restricted from remote and offshore work during the active stages
of an infectious disease if there is significant risk of spread within the working community.
Individuals suffering from chronic communicable disease should be assessed to determine (Furnace et
al, 2008):
(a) The risk of transmission to others;
(b) Any effects of the condition which may adversely affect the individual’s ability to perform their
duties or effectively participate in emergency evacuation; and
(c) The requirements of long-term therapy and side effects of such therapy.
Catering staff should undergo special examination to exclude acute or chronic disease involving
gastrointestinal tract, chest, ear, nose, throat and skin due to the risk of food-borne spread of the disease.
Routine screening for tuberculosis during periodic medical assessment is not required unless indicated.
10.14.1 Immunodeficiency Virus Infection (HIV) All candidates shall be screened for HIV at pre-
employment medical assessment. Expatriates tested positive for HIV will be rejected from
employment in line with the UAE regulations for the residency visa requirements.
10.15 Obesity and Risk Management
Overweight and obesity are defined as abnormal or excessive fat accumulation that may impair health
(WHO, 2014). Obesity can adversely impact on the individual’s ability to carry out job tasks effectively
and safely. This is particularly important in occupations that involve significant physical effort and in
offshore and Remote Locations where prompt access to medical or emergency facilities can be limited.
In assessing fitness for work, consideration should be given to mobility issues, the ability to perform
essential job tasks, and respond adequately to emergency situations. Obesity is associated with an
increased risk of morbidity (e.g. hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, heart diseases, stroke) and
workplace accidents (Safe work Australia 2007, Gu et al 2016).
Body mass index (BMI) is the commonly used tool to categorise overweight and obesity in adults. It is
defined as body weight in kilograms divided by the square of the height in meters (kg/m2) (WHO, 2014).

10.16 Pregnancy and Maternity at Work
Female workers are increasingly being employed in remote and offshore locations. Therefore there
should be provisions for pregnant and lactating employees and consideration of factors that may be
detrimental to pregnancy and breast feeding (Coomber & Harris, 2013). The requirements of UAE Labor
Law should also be taken into consideration when assessing fitness to work of pregnant and lactating
women.
Conditions to be considered in assessing the fitness to work of a pregnant worker include:
Workplace factors:
10.16.1 Shift work, heavy manual work (especially where it involves poor postures and ergonomic factors);
10.16.2 Exposure to agents such as physical, chemical and biological hazards (e.g. teratogens,
asphyxiants, heat, noise, vibration); and
10.16.3 Ease of access to toilet facilities.
Individual factors:
10.16.4 Experience of symptoms common in pregnancy e.g. nausea, vomiting, fatigue with consideration
of frequency and severity of the symptoms;
10.16.5 Clinical conditions that can affect pregnancy outcome e.g. a history of previous obstetric
complications, pre-eclamptic toxaemia, and other general medical conditions such as
hypertension, diabetes, cardiac disease, and renal disorders;
10.16.6 Mobility at work; and
10.16.7 Compliance with the use of personal protective equipment and emergency escape procedures,
where applicable.
A reduction of working hours for a pregnant worker can be considered by the examining physician in
accordance with current regulations and company policies.

11.2 Divers
Professional divers should be medically and physically fit to undertake diving work. The assessing

types of diving, diving physiology/pathophysiology, fitness to dive and the unique work environment.

11.2.4 Diving Fitness Post-COVID-19 Infection:
COVID-19 infection can have significant effects on many of the body’s organs and systems which,
in turn, have implications for a diver's fitness to dive. The extent to which these effects may persist
is not yet clear and there is no international consensus as to when it is safe to return to diving.
Even asymptomatic and mild infections may result in underlying abnormalities that may only
become apparent at times of physical demand, such as when diving. Possible residual effects on
the pulmonary (lungs), cardiac (heart) and clotting systems are of particular importance in fitness
to dive.
As a result, any diver who have been diagnosed with or suspected of having COVID-19 infection,
should consult with an Approved Medical Examiner of Divers (AMED) prior to returning to diving.

Seafarers with normal colour vision can perform lookout duties (Maritime & Coastguard Agency,
2015). Engineering staff (e.g. engineers, electro-technical officers) should be able to correctly
recognise colour coding on cables, pipes and display screens (Maritime & Coastguard Agency,

Colour vision in seafarers has to be tested to ensure that all applicants who pass can carry out
visually demanding, colour-related lookout duties with the same accuracy as those with normal
trichromatic vision, while at the same time there should be no denial of career opportunities to
those who can safely carry out such duties.

The protocol used in these guidelines is in line with the requirements in international conventions,
uses the Ishihara test as initial screen, with access to confirmatory testing for those who fail the
initial screening.

The Ishihara isochromatic plate test, when used as a screening test, has the advantage of ease
of use and low cost, but the test lacks specificity if sensitivity is to remain high and also fails to
diagnose accurately the class of deficiency involved.

Because of these characteristics neither Ishihara nor other pseudo-isochromatic plate-based
tests can be optimized for use as a single definitive test for fitness to undertake navigational
lookout duties, which will ensure suitability without denying some of those tested employment. It
is for this reason that a second ‘confirmatory’ test is needed for those who do not achieve the
criterion set for the Ishihara.

The recommended second confirmatory test is the CAD (Colour Assessment and Diagnosis) test,
developed by City University in conjunction with the UK’s Civil Aviation Authority (CAA). This
measures the severity and type of colour vision loss, and reliably detects congenital deficiency.

11.3.5 Hearing Standards
The hearing capacity for seafarers should be an average of at least 30 dB (unaided) in the better

11.8 Fitness to Wear Respiratory Protection
Respiratory protection equipment (RPE) is designed to prevent or limit the exposure of persons to
dangerous substances that may be inhaled. Personal protective equipment such as RPE are meant to be
a measure of last resort in the hierarchy of controls for workplace exposures. It is used when it is not
reasonably practicable to control exposures to hazardous substances by other means, e.g. where work
is to be carried out in a confined space, or work under emergency conditions (firefighting, rescue).
Respiratory Protective Equipment (RPE) includes:
(a) Air Purifying Respirators (e.g. mechanical filter cartridge, chemical filter cartridge, mechanical and
chemical cartridge, gas masks, positive air pressure respirator [PAPR]);
Document No: AHQ-PTCS-GSST-GMSF-GID-004-R01-23 Page 60 of 89

ADNOC Classification: Internal
(b) Air Supplying Devices; and
(c) Combination of air Purifying and Air Supplying.
Effective protection by RPE is possible only when:
(a) RPE is suitable for the task;
(b) RPE is suited to the wearer and the environment;
(c) RPE is in good condition;
(d) RPE is worn correctly by someone who has been trained to do so; and
(e) The wearer is medically fit to wear RPE.
RPE should be fit tested (qualitatively and/or quantitatively) whenever a respirator is newly supplied or
where a worker is required to wear a respirator for the first time. There should be consideration of different
sizes and types of respirators taking into account facial shape, size and presence of beard and
moustache. A worker shall be trained on the proper maintenance and fitness of respirator and comply
with the company facial hair policy for RPE wearers.
11.8.1 Periodicity of Medical Assessment
For individuals requiring the use of self-containing breathing apparatus (SCBA) as part of a
specific duty or as a requirement of the job type (e.g. fire fighter, emergency response team
member, confined space entry, diver), a fitness to wear respiratory protection medical
assessment shall be carried out at the pre-employment stage, and thereafter annually or when:
11.8.1.1 An individual reports medical signs or symptoms that can affect the ability to use a

11.8.1.2 Observations made during fit testing and program evaluation indicate a need for re-

11.8.1.3 A change in workplace conditions (e.g., physical work effort, protective clothing, and

11.8.2 Fitness Criteria

would impair the ability to wear respiratory protection.

11.9 Fitness for Working at Heights
11.9.1 Definitions
Working at Heights is work in which there is a risk of an individual falling from any height from,
thru, into, or onto a place or structure 1
A place is ‘at height’ if a person could be injured falling from it, even if it is at or below ground
level 1
Working at height means work in any place where, if precautions were not taken, a person could
fall a distance liable to cause personal injury. Extra controls (e.g., body harness) shall be placed
when working at height is greater or equal to 1.8 meters 2
11.9.2 Risk Factors
External factors such as wind, heat stress, structural instability and worker factors should all be
considered along with control requirements as they can be the cause of falls leading to injury,

Other external factors that should be considered and included in a safe system of work are: heat
stress and dehydration of workers working at heights and vertigo. 3

12. HEALTH SURVEILLANCE
12.1 Health surveillance is the process of periodic monitoring of the health and well-being of the workforce.
The objectives for conducting such monitoring include:
12.1.1 To protect the workforce and prevent occupationally related diseases. This is especially if the
exposures are not easily controlled, and there is uncertainty regarding the levels of exposure to
workplace hazards. Health surveillance is useful if early departures from health can be detected,
and if such effects are reversible on removal of the affected individuals away from further
exposure. This should proceed alongside additional measures to eliminate or control hazardous
exposures;
12.1.2 To detect treatable non-occupational conditions early so that the health status can be improved
through appropriate treatment and prevention;
12.1.3 To monitor individuals for unhealthy lifestyle habits e.g. alcohol consumption or cigarette smoking
or unhealthy diets, with a view to providing health promotion advice and counselling and to
improve general health. A healthy workforce also benefits the organization;
12.1.4 Where it is required by legislation, e.g. UAE Labor Law and ADNOC standards, for periodic
reviews of workers exposed;
12.1.5 For the safety of co-workers and third parties; and
12.1.6 To avoid where possible costly, time-consuming and difficult medical evacuation for workers
located in offshore and Remote Locations.
12.2 Health surveillance requires an assessment of an individuals health, using clinical, physiological or
laboratory procedures to detect any significant abnormalities against set ‘norms’ or by comparisons
against established guidance values.
12.3 Where the health surveillance programme coincides with periodic medical assessment, it should be
performed as part of periodic medical assessment.

16.5  APPENDIX E: VACCINATION PROGRAM:

16.5.1  Benefits of workplace vaccinations:

16.5.1.1  Reduction in absenteeism due to vaccine preventable diseases restricts/alleviates the
financial loss and improvement in work productivity. Influenza vaccination has broader
merits: even nonvaccinated people stand to benefit, as the transmission of infection is
restricted.  The  economic  benefits  of  polysaccharide  23-valent  (PPSV23)  and/or
conjugate pneumococcal vaccine 13-valent (PCV13) in adults, the elderly, and at-risk
groups have been evaluated in many studies.

16.5.1.2  During the peak season, flu causes high rates of absenteeism and low productivity
among affected individuals. A study that evaluated the costs and benefits of influenza
vaccination showed that vaccination is associated with not only a 23% reduction in the
absence rate but also a 30% improvement in lost working days and related expenses.
A study conducted among 1022 employees in a Malaysian petrochemical company
revealed that the total number of days of sick leave (i.e. absenteeism) was 72 in
vaccinated vs. 473 in nonvaccinated employees, whereas the total number of days of
reduced productivity was 148 in vaccinated vs. 432 in nonvaccinated employees.

guidelines  for  adult  vaccination  are  the  WHO  guidelines,  the  Advisory  Committee  on
immunization  Practices (ACIP) guidelines from CDC and ECDC and Immunisation  against
infectious disease - 'The Green Book' UK Government.

16.5.3.4 Immunization Recommendations for Disaster Responders
16.5.3.4.1 Tetanus: In accordance with the current CDC guidelines, responders
should receive a tetanus booster if they have not been vaccinated for
tetanus during the past 10 years. Td (tetanus/diphtheria) or Tdap
(tetanus/diphtheria/pertussis) can be used; getting the Tdap formula for
one tetanus booster during adulthood is recommended to maintain
protection against pertussis. While documentation of vaccination is
preferred, it should not be a prerequisite to work.
16.5.3.4.2 Hepatitis B: Hepatitis B vaccine series for persons who will be
performing direct patient care or otherwise expected to have contact
with bodily fluids.
