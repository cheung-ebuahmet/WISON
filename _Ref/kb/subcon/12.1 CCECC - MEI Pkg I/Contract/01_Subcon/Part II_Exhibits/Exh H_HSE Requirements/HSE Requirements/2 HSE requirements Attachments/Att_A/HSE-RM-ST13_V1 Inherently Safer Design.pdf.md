1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. INHERENTLY SAFER DESIGN (ISD) CONCEPT
Inherently Safer Design (ISD) describes a broad, philosophical approach to process safety in which the
focus lies upon the designing of processes and products in such a way as to eliminate hazards completely
or sufficiently minimize their magnitude so as to eliminate the hazard and negate the need for elaborate
safety systems incorporated in the design. This hazard elimination or reduction is achieved through
means which are inherent to the process and, as such, are permanent and inseparable from it.
Besides elimination or reduction of a hazard, ISD applies to the layers of protection in place within a
system such that the strength of a layer of protection can be improved by features that are permanent
and inseparable from that layer.
ISO 17776 “Petroleum and natural gas industries - Offshore production installations - Major accident
hazard management during the design of new installations” [Ref. 2] outlines a general requirement for
the application of ISD for all offshore facilities. The ISO 17776 standard requires that ISD be implemented
to eliminate any credible MAHs or reduce their potential consequences through the application of design
measures inherent to the design, permanent and inseparable from the installation.
1.2. ISD WITHIN HAZARD MANAGEMENT
ISD forms a vital part of a broader approach to risk control and hazard management in the process
industry and represents the theoretical ideal approach to the control of hazards. Hazard management
can be broadly divided into three primary principles:
 Prevention – Measures taken to eliminate or reduce hazards at source or to reduce the likelihood
of a hazard being realised.
 Control – Measures taken to keep hazards within the design envelope, be it through containment
or control systems or reactions to events that could result in an incident.
 Mitigation – Measures taken to respond to a hazard once an incident has occurred either to bring
it back under control or limit its effects.
ISD represents the primary method for the implementation of the prevention category of hazard
management and is preferable to Control and Mitigation measures due to its status as an inherent safe
component of the design and does not rely on other measures. In the Hierarchy of Controls, as shown in
Figure 1.2.1, the most effective forms of protection are Elimination, Substitution and Isolation, all of which
come under the scope of ISD.

1.3. SYSTEMATIC APPROACH TO ISD
As a fundamental philosophy for the assurance of process safety, it is of vital importance that ISD is
considered across all process facilities. This standard outlines a formalised and systematic approach to
the consideration of ISD throughout the design of all ADNOC facilities and assets.
The concept of the ISD Process, as outlined in this standard, is to turn the attention of designers and
operators of ADNOC assets towards the elimination and reduction of hazards at their source, reducing
the risk passed on to operations and fundamentally developing a better integrated combination of reduced
residual risks, risk prevention, risk control and risk mitigation measures.
The systematic ISD Process centres on the completion of ISD Review Workshops as a medium through
which to combine expertise within ADNOC and its clients to identify options by which Major Accident
Hazards (MAHs) can be eliminated or have their risk reduced through ISD, as opposed to through other
forms of control. The ISD Process includes a semi-quantitative analysis of MAH risks, followed by an
iterative analysis of Risk Reduction Measures (RRMs) culminating in a report including a clear set of
design guidelines and actions to be implemented to maximise the inherent safety present in the assessed
design/facility. The ISD Process is relevant throughout the lifecycle of all ADNOC projects, with specific
focus on its use in the Conceptual, Pre-FEED and FEED Stages.
1.4. OBJECTIVES OF THE ISD PROCESS
The objectives of ISD as applied to all ADNOC projects and facilities is to eliminate MAHs or reduce the
severity or likelihood of their associated consequences to such an extent as to remove the need for
elaborate safety systems and procedures. Beyond this, ISD also applies to layers of protection and the
improving of the strength of such barriers through the means of features that are permanent and
inseparable from them.

7.2. PRINCIPLES OF ISD PROCESS
Introducing a systematic approach to inherent safety into all projects and the operation of facilities is of
great importance for eliminating/reducing risks at source and benefitting ADNOC’s long-term operations.
The greatest value of the ISD Process will always be realized in the Conceptual, FEED and early in
Detailed Engineering phases of the project lifecycle, however, to ensure the plant or facility remains
‘Inherently Safer” there are ways to introduce the same principles into maintenance and operations
throughout the life-cycle of the plant or facility.
The vision of an Inherently Safer Designed Facility is one which has:
 Fewer inherent MAH potentials;
 Reduced probability of MAH events;
 Reduced inventory and damage potential from potential MAH’s, leading to reduced severity of
potential events;
 Reduced need for safety systems, and the maintenance activities associated with them, to the
greatest extent possible. Thus, a reduced exposure of the asset and personnel to risk during the
life cycle of the facility;
 Reduced scope for smaller MAH incidents to escalate and overwhelm the facilities;
 Reduced inherent/residual risks passed on to operations;
 Optimised the opportunity to reduce OPEX even with minor increase in CAPEX; and
 The assurance that an Inherently Safer Design is safer to build and operate.
7.3. ISD IN PROJECT LIFECYCLE
Throughout the life cycle of a project/facility (concept to operations), opportunities arise to apply the
concepts and practices of inherently safer technologies and strategies. These opportunities should be
evaluated to determine if the strategies can be applied, and whether the costs commensurate with the
possible risk reduction.
As the engineering progresses through project life cycle, conceptual plant design, FEED, engineering
and detailed plant design, plant construction, start-up, and ongoing operation and future modification,
different choices and decisions are made. The ISD philosophy applies at all stages, but the available
options change.
The best opportunities for ISD implementation are early in concept or pre-concept stage. At this point, no
commitment has been made to a technology or particular design or engineering method; no resources
have been spent on engineering that would have to be redone; project is not committed to develop their
processes/plant configuration and design; and no capital has been committed to build a plant to
implement a particular configuration and design.
The possibility for affecting the inherent safety of a process and plant/ facility design decreases as the
design proceeds and more and more engineering and financial decisions have been made. It is much
easier to affect the process, plant configuration and inherent safety in the conceptual design phase than
in the later phases of process design.

7.4. ISD PRINCIPLES
The basic approach to ISD can be described by four primary strategies:
 Minimize – use smaller quantities of hazardous substances (also referred to as Intensification or,
if removed entirely, Elimination).
 Substitute – replace a material or process with a less hazardous one.
 Moderate – use less hazardous process conditions, a less hazardous form of a material or facilities
chosen to minimize the impact of a release of hazardous materials or energy (also called
Attenuation).
 Simplify – design facilities that eliminate unnecessary complexity and make operating errors less
likely and that are forgiving of errors which may be made (error tolerance).
These principle strategies lead to following with regards to risk associated with plant/facility:
 fewer hazards;
 fewer causes;
 reduced consequence severity;
 reduced consequence likelihood; and
 fewer consequences.

b) Gate valves with rising spindles to clearly indicate open or close position; and
c) Spectacle (or figure 8) blinds instead of slip plates.
B. Design the equipment with sufficient strength to contain the maximum pressure generated,
even if “worst credible event” occurs?
C. Can passive leak-limiting technology be used to limit potential loss of containment?
Some examples include the following:
1) Blowout resistant gaskets;
2) Increasing wall strength;
3) Using fewer seams and joints;
4) Providing extra corrosion/erosion allowance;
5) Reducing vibration;
6) Minimizing the use of open-ended, quick-opening valves;
7) Eliminating open-ended, quick-opening valves in hazardous service;
8) Improving valve seating reliability;
9) Eliminating unnecessary expansion joints, hoses, and rupture disks; and
10) Eliminating unnecessary sight glasses/glass rotameters.

APPENDIX 3
APPENDIX 3 ISD REVIEW BRAINSTORMING CONSIDERATIONS
Production Targets: Throughput and Uptime
a. Production targets should be challenged if they are stretch limits of equipment such that
repeated hazardous intervention or equipment duplication is required.
b. Increase in production that may affect pressure, temperature or composition may have major
effect on risks through increased complexity, maintenance, activity, and staffing and therefore
may not be justified either in terms of risk or economics.
c. Simple design with low staffing and moderate throughput may be better business option.
Scheduling: Design and Construction
a. Compressed design schedule will reduce opportunities to optimise design in both safety and
other aspects.
b. Condensed construction schedule will invariably lead to overlap of activities and increase
Simultaneous Activities.
c. Project should consider options in which activities, such as design and construction or
construction and operation, are eliminated or minimized.
d. Simultaneous construction and operation will lead to increased risks.
Location and Separation of Hazards
a. Options should be considered that physically separate one hazard from another, such as
production from storage and personnel from effects of potential major accidents.
Staffing, Operating, and Maintenance
a. Facility that is normally staffed and has high occupancy of hazardous areas is by nature less
safe than unstaffed or minimally staffed one.
b. Options that do not require permanent personnel presence or minimize number of activities
and personnel should be considered.
Construction
a. Risks associated with transport of construction materials, large pieces of equipment, and
construction and assembly of equipment will vary considerably with different options.
b. Options that minimize number of heavy loads, heavy lifts, site assembly, and commissioning
should be actively considered.
Novel or Untried Technology
a. Novel technology should be considered if it might offer simpler, safer alternative.
b. It is possible to identify and evaluate hazards associated with novel technology and particular
techniques are available to do so.
c. New technology should be actively encouraged in search for inherently safer design.
