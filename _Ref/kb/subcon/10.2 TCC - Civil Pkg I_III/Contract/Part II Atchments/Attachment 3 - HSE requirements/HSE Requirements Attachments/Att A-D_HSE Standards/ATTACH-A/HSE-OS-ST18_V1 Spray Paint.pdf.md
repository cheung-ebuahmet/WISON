1. INTRODUCTION
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy,
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all personnel who
undertake Abrasive Blasting and Spray Painting activity, are fully aware of the hazards involved and have
the competence, training and awareness to undertake such activities.
Abrasive blasting means propelling a stream of an abrasive material at high speed against a surface
using compressed air, liquid, steam, centrifugal wheels or paddles to clean, abrade, etch or otherwise
change the original appearance or condition of the surface. Painting uses materials, often containing
volatile solvents, pumped at high pressures to apply coatings to surface prepared by abrasive blasting or
by other means such as washing or wire brushing.
Abrasive blasting and spray painting activity possess threat to people, environment and/or assets. Some
common hazards includes exposure to hazardous chemicals, fire and explosion, noise, exposure to dust,
exposure to electrical risks, etc.
2. PURPOSE
The purpose of this standard is to provide an overall understanding of abrasive blasting and spray painting
activity and its application within the ADNOC Group. This standard establishes requirements for the use
of abrasive blasting and spray painting activity within the ADNOC Group.
All works related to the abrasive blasting and spray painting activities carried out for ADNOC Group shall
be in accordance with the requirements of this Standard.
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates &
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood,
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7. REQUIREMENTS
7.1. OVERVIEW
Abrasive blasting means propelling a stream of an abrasive material at high speed against a surface
using compressed air, liquid, steam, centrifugal wheels or paddles to clean, abrade, etch or otherwise
change the original appearance or condition of the surface.
The most common method uses compressed air to propel abrasive material from a blast pot, through a
blasting hose to a nozzle that is manually controlled by the person. Automated abrasive blasting machines
such as centrifugal wheel systems and tumblers are also used. Abrasive blasting is generally performed
in enclosed environment like blasting chambers or cabinets, however there may be occasions where
open air blasting activities are performed, when there is no alternative. The process of abrasive blasting
is used but not limited to the following:
(i) Clean a surface by removing unwanted rust, scale, paint etc. to achieve desired profile, in
preparation for painting, anodizing, welding or other processes which require a clean surface;
(ii) Deburr, remove tool marks, or otherwise finish a crude product;
(iii) Change metallurgical properties or stress relieve a part by the peening action of multiple
impactions; and
(iv) Produce a desired matte or decorative finish.
The methods for applying abrasive materials can be segregated into two distinct types of process: dry
and wet.
(i) Dry Blasting: There are two methods used for dry blast cleaning; mechanical blasting and
air pressure blasting.
1. Mechanical Blasting: It most frequently employs the use of cabinet-type equipment
which is available in either batch, semi-automatic or automatic versions. Typically the
cabinet houses one or more blast wheels which directs the abrasive at the workpiece
by a centrifugal force.

2. Air Pressure Blasting: Air pressure blasting uses compressed air to apply abrasive to
a surface. It uses either a direct pressure or an induction method that may use either
the siphon or gravity method.
(ii) Wet Blasting: It involves high-velocity, compressed air propulsion if a slurry is directed onto
a workplace. The slurry normally consists of fine abrasive suspended in chemically treated
water. It is usually kept in continuous agitation to prevent settling of the abrasive.
A variety of abrasives are in common use which includes steel shot, steel grit, aluminium, flint/garnet,
glass beads, carbides, slag, and organic materials (such as ground walnut shells, ground corncobs and
crushed pecan shells).
Spray painting, including electrostatic spray painting, is a process by which liquid paint is applied under
pressure to an object. Spray painting may be carried out by hand or automatically. There are several
methods used to atomize the paint for spraying:
(i) Using a conventional air compressor - air is driven across the mouth of a small outlet under
pressure to draw liquid paint out of the container and produce an air-paint mist from the
nozzle of the spray-gun.
(ii) Airless spray painting - the paint container is pressurized pushing the paint to the nozzle
where it is atomized by the spray gun.
(iii) Electrostatic spray painting - an electric pump drives the electrostatically charged liquid paint
out of the nozzle which is then applied to the object which is earthed.
7.2. RISK MANAGEMENT PROCESS
7.2.1. HAZARD IDENTIFICATION
The first step in managing risks associated with abrasive blasting and spray painting activities is to identify
all hazards that could potentially cause harm to people, environment and/or assets. These shall be
identified by:
(i) Conducting a walk through assessment of the workplace;
(ii) Observing the work and talking to workers about how abrasive blasting and spray painting
activities are carried out;
(iii) Inspecting plant and equipment used during the activities (e.g. Live systems);
(iv) Reading product labels, Safety Data Sheets (SDS) and manufacturer’s instruction manual;
and
(v) Reviewing incident reports.
(a) Hazards Associated with Abrasive Blasting Activity
There are many hazardous situations and conditions created by abrasive blasting activity. The
prevalent hazards associated to the activity are as follows:
(i) Health Hazards: The three most significant health hazards are those which relate to dust,
noise and chemicals, with dust being the most serious threat to health (*).
1. Dust: Dust result from broken-down abrasives, pulverized surface coatings and
encrusted substances, and abraded material from the object being blasted. The solid
particles which comprise the dust range in size from less than one micron to more
than 1000 microns. Under normal conditions, dust particles of 10 microns or more
diameter settle relatively quickly. Smaller dust particles (less than 10 microns) remain
airborne for longer time and are easily inhaled which settles in the lungs and
sometimes dissolve into the blood stream.

(*): For more details refer to ADNOC Chemical Hazards Standard [Ref. 20]
2. Noise: Exposure to high noise levels can cause permanent hearing loss. Blasting
equipment may generate various noise levels that may cause workers to be exposed
to noise that exceeds the exposure standard. The major sources of noise during the
activity are from discharge of compressed air from the blast nozzle, blast cabinets and
air compressors. ADNOC Physical Hazards Standard [Ref. 19] shall be referred for
hazards associated to noise.
3. Chemical Exposure: Contact with hazardous chemicals is an everyday risk due to the
activity. Workers not adequately protected through procedures, position, or PPE are
likely to be exposed to substances that may cause significant acute injury or long-
term health effects. ADNOC Chemical Hazards Standard [Ref. 20] shall be referred
for exposure to hazardous chemicals due to the activity.
4. Heat: Heat is also a common hazard associated with carrying out blasting. Personnel
are at risk of heat strain due to working in hot, poorly ventilated or confined spaces
and the type of personal protective equipment that is worn, for example blast helmets,
protective suits or leather coveralls. Heat strain is a serious medical condition which
could lead to heat exhaustion and death. ADNOC Physical Hazards Standard [Ref.
19] shall be referred for exposure to heat.
5. Vibration: The force of the abrasive moving through the blast hose transmits vibration
to the hands and arms of operators holding the equipment. Prolonged use of abrasive
blasting equipment may lead to a condition known as occupational Raynaud’s disease
(also called white finger or dead finger). It results from persistent microscopic damage
to nerves and blood capillaries. It may also cause carpal tunnel syndrome. ADNOC
Physical Hazards Standard [Ref. 19] shall be referred for exposure to vibration.
6. Manual Tasks: Abrasive blasting may result in musculoskeletal disorders from
performing hazardous manual tasks, for example: back strain from lifting or pushing;
muscle strain from working in awkward positions; strain from hose whip; Occupational
Overuse Syndrome from controlling the blast hose. ADNOC Ergonomics Hazards
Standard [Ref. 21] and ADNOC Manual Handling Standard [Ref. 25] shall be referred
for activity associated with manual tasks.
(ii) Safety Hazards: This includes those associated with the abrasive blasting equipment itself,
flying abrasives, fire and explosion, and static electricity.
(b) Hazards Associated with Spray Painting Activity
(i) Hazardous Chemicals: The health effects that a worker may experience following exposure
to hazardous chemicals can become apparent after a short period of time and include
headaches, nausea or vomiting, dizziness, burns to the skin or eyes and irritation to the nose,
throat and lungs. Serious long term health effects where the symptoms may not be
immediately apparent can also occur. Long term health effects include asthma, dermatitis,
kidney or liver damage, cancer and damage to the reproductive system and central nervous
system. Many chemicals used in spray painting or powder coating also have
physicochemical hazards. For example, many organic solvents are flammable and some
chemicals used for cleaning or surface preparation may be corrosive. ADNOC Chemical
Hazards Standard [Ref. 20] shall be referred for exposure to hazardous chemicals due to
the activity.
(ii) Fire and Explosion: This may lead to serious burns and death, exposure to projectiles and
damage to property. For example, flammable paints and solvents may come into contact
with an ignition source.
(iii) Machinery and Equipment: Injection injuries, being caught by moving parts of machinery can
cause fractures, bruises, lacerations, dislocations, permanent injuries or death. Common
examples are spray booths, sanding, grinding equipment, airless spray equipment,
compressed air.

(iv) Electricity or Static Electricity: Exposure to electricity may cause shock, burns or death from

7.2.2. RISK ASSESSMENT
A risk assessment shall be performed prior to the commencement of the activity to determine the
measures to be taken to implement the control of risk. The risk assessment helps to:
(i) Identify the personnel at risk of exposure;
(ii) Determine the sources and processes causing the risk;
(iii) Identify that proper control measures shall be implemented; and
(iv) Assess the effectiveness of existing control measures.
The likelihood of each hazard actually causing harm in a specific situation shall be assessed. As minimum,
the following shall be used for the assessment:
(i) How often and for how long will exposure to the hazard occur?
(ii) If exposed to the hazard, how severe will the outcome be based on Risk Assessment Matrix?
(iii) What are the properties of the blasting medium being used?
(iv) What is the substrate being blasted?
(v) What is the likelihood of damaging the assets due to the pressure exerted and nozzle
selected?
(vi) What are the surface coatings/chemical content of the items being blasted (for example, are
they toxic materials or reactive chemicals)?
(vii) How do workers interact with the hazard (for example, being exposed to hazardous
chemicals; by breathing it in or skin contact)?
(viii) What are the conditions under which operations are being carried out (for example, are they
carried out in a confined space)?
(ix) What are the skills, competence and experience of the operators?

7.3. CONTROL MEASURES FOR ABRASIVE BLASTING ACTIVITY
7.3.1. CONTROL OF DUST
(a) Using Less Hazardous Abrasive Material
(i) Before using the abrasive blasting mediums, the concentration of impurities shall be checked
by referring to SDS;
(ii) Blasting medium with qualities that will generate minimum dust levels shall be selected;
(iii) Avoid using metallic abrasives since it is the major cause of dust produced during blasting;
(iv) Environmentally clean and recyclable abrasives, such as chilled iron grit or cast iron steel
grit shall be used where reasonably practicable.
(b) Using Less Hazardous Surface Area Preparation Method
The selection of methods of surface preparation also effects the amount of dust in the air which
includes the following:
(i) Wet abrasive blasting;
(ii) Centrifugal wheel blasting;
(iii) Vacuum blasting; and
(iv) Other removal methods like sodium bicarbonate blasting, blast cleaning with reusable
sponge abrasives, carbon dioxide (dry ice) blasting.
7.3.2. CONTROL OF NOISE
Exposure to high noise level can be controlled by the following:
(i) Isolating workers and other persons from the noise source by:

1. Using blast chambers;
2. Relocating or enclosing noisy equipment - blast cabinets, air compressors, and grit

(ii) Reducing the amount of pressure used to abrade the substrate;

7.3.3. CONTROL OF HEAT
The control of heat can be done but not limited to the following methods:
(i) Providing a cool, well-ventilated area where workers can take rest breaks or carry out other
tasks;
(ii) Scheduling work so that abrasive blasting is done at cooler times;
(iii) Ensuring cool drinking water is readily available.
ADNOC Physical Hazards Standard [Ref. 19] shall be referred for exposure to heat.
7.3.4. CONTROL OF VIBRATION
Exposure to high vibration can be controlled by the following:
(i) Using of vibration-reduced equipment such as vibration isolating handles incorporated into
blasting nozzles and/or supports to reduce the pressure of the hand to control the nozzle;
(ii) Job rotation; and

7.4. CONTROL MEASURES FOR SPRAY PAINTING ACTIVITY
7.4.1. SPRAY PAINTING IN SPRAY BOOTHS
Spray booths are enclosed or partially enclosed structures designed to prevent or reduce exposure to
hazardous chemicals or vapors. A spray booth shall be used when spray painting with a hazardous
chemical, except when:
(i) The shape, size or weight of an article cannot be easily moved or fit into a spray booth;
(ii) The painting involves minor work such as spotting or touch-ups.
Spray booths shall:
(i) Be fitted with an exhaust capture system and a ventilation system that includes a filter for
removing airborne contaminants;
(ii) Have ventilation systems capable of producing a minimum air movement;
(iii) Be inspected at regular intervals and maintained according to manufacturer’s
recommendation; and
(iv) A pre-purge cycle to remove any residue contaminants and also operate a minimum of a 5
minute post-purge period following spraying.
(b) Ventilation System
The purpose of ventilation is to:
(i) Draw overspray away from the personnel;
(ii) Control flammable and hazardous vapors;
(iii) Collect vapors, droplets and solid particles; and
(iv) Filter or wash the air before it is discharged.
The ventilation shall be sufficient to prevent the formation of dangerous concentrations of
flammable vapors. As a rule, the maximum flammable concentration in a spray booth or spray
space during normal operation shall not exceed 25% of the Lower Explosive Limit (LEL). [Ref. 3 &
8].
Airborne monitoring of vapors/solvents arising from spray painting process shall be performed such
that it does not exceed the allowable limits as per UAE regulations (refer to ADNOC OHRM
Standard [Ref. 18] for guidance on applicable Occupational Exposure Limit (OEL) values). The
airborne contaminants to be monitored based on SDS of the spray painting.
The requirements of Ventilation should be in line with requirements stated in American Conference
of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) Industrial Ventilation Manual [Ref. 10] and as
detailed below in Table 7.4.1.

7.4.3. SPRAYING IN CONFINED SPACES
Spraying is often used to apply coatings to the inside of storage tanks, vessels and other enclosed rooms
and spaces. In an unventilated enclosure, a harmful vapor concentration may be formed within a few
seconds of starting spraying, and a flammable atmosphere within a few minutes. ADNOC Confined Space
Standard [Ref. 23] shall be followed for working under these conditions. As a minimum the following shall
be considered:
(i) Mechanical exhaust ventilation shall be provided to ensure that the concentration in all parts
of the confined space is at a safe level. The maximum concentration of flammable vapor
shall be identified in the permit in line with the requirements of ADNOC Permit to Work
Standard [Ref. 22];
(ii) It is essential that any increase in the flammable vapor concentration above the specified
limit is detected immediately since personnel wearing breathing apparatus and protective
clothing may not be aware of increasing levels of vapor;
(iii) Vapors from spraying are likely to accumulate at floor level so extraction at this level is
essential;
(iv) The discharge point shall be situated in a safe place away from any building, work area or
source of ignition;
(v) Continuous gas monitoring is recommended although periodic check measurements may
be sufficient, once the effectiveness of the ventilation system has been confirmed; and
(vi) It is important to maintain the ventilation within the confined space until the coating is dry so
that there is no further risk of a flammable atmosphere.

7.4.5. FIRE AND EXPLOSION
The use of flammable materials in spray painting, for example, organic solvents, increases the risk of fire
and explosion because of the amount of solvent vapor in the air. Sources of ignition include:
(i) Open flames, including matches, lighters, cigarettes, cutting torches and welding;
(ii) Hot surfaces including engines, motors and light bulbs;
(iii) Chemical reactions, mixing hazardous chemicals can generate heat or static and create an
ignition source;
(iv) Sparks from electric equipment, portable electric tools, power points, radios, mobile phones
or from the discharge of static electricity from poorly-earthed equipment;
(v) Catalytic reactions, for example, a catalyst speeds the resin hardening process when two-
pack epoxy paints are mixed and this creates heat; and
(vi) Self-heating or spontaneous combustion.
The following controls shall be adapted to avoid fire and explosion:
(i) Areas in which spray painting is carried out, shall be separated from other parts of the same
building by compartment walls and floors having fire resistance of not less than 2 hours.
Spray painting booths shall have built in vapor extraction system [Ref. 1];
(ii) Where a spray painting room or booth is protected by an automatic fire suppression system,
the fire compartment to the room or booth can be reduced from 2 hours to 1 hour [Ref. 1];
(iii) All the fitting used in the vicinity of the activity shall be suitable for hazardous area and shall
be in line with ADNOC Control of Temporary Equipment in Classified Hazardous Areas
Standard [Ref. 24];
(iv) Over-speed shut down valves and Spark arrestor to be fitted with Diesel Operating
Equipment (applicable in Hazardous Areas Refer ADNOC Control of Temporary Equipment
in Classified Hazardous Areas Standard [Ref. 24]);
(v) Earthing and Bonding - i.e. before pouring flammable liquids from one container into another,
set both containers down on an earthed surface then bring the containers into contact before
pouring and keep them in contact while pouring;
(vi) Provide suitable fire extinguishers;

7.5. WORK ON LIVE SYSTEMS
Live systems are hydrocarbon production systems, high pressure water injection systems, both offshore
and onshore. These comprise of wells, pipework and vessels through which oil and/or gas or water is, or
may be flowing. Work on live systems is envisaged on a regular basis and is controlled through application
of the ADNOC Permit to Work Standard [Ref. 22]. In either case, work on live systems shall be carried
out only if:
(i) Abrasive blasting and painting can immediately be stopped for operator access to the facility
or when the wind direction is unfavorable;
(ii) The state of corrosion of the facility does not present a significant danger. Recent Non
Destructive Testing inspection reports may provide valuable information which can be used
to help decide whether blasting should proceed;
(iii) There are no spark producing operations in progress, e.g. grinding, welding, etc.;
(iv) There are no turbine, engine, compressor or ventilation intakes in the immediate work area; and
(v) Personnel can quickly and easily reach to a safe zone.
