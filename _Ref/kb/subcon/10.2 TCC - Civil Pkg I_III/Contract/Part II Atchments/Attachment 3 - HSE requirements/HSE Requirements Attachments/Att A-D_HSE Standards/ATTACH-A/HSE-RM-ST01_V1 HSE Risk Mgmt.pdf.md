1. INTRODUCTION
The ADNOC HSE Policy mandates that ADNOC and its Group Companies are committed to “Pursuing
no harm to people and the community” and “Protecting the environment” and makes commitments to
managing risks through effective controls and minimizing impacts on business and sets overall context
and leadership commitment in managing HSE risks.
This means that all activities must be conducted in a manner designed to minimise HSE risks, protect
health and safety of employees, contractors, customers, the community at large and the environment.
ADNOC, through active participation of all employees and contractors, will strive to manage HSE risks
with the goal of preventing accidents, injuries and occupational illnesses, progressively minimising
environmental impact by reducing discharges, using energy efficiently and producing safe, quality
products. ADNOC Group will manage all HSE risks to a level which is As Low as Reasonably Practicable
(ALARP).
Identifying hazards and analysing their potential effects, assessing the risks against the ADNOC criteria,
installing control measures to reduce risks to tolerable level are essential requirements for proactive HSE
management. In order to make a value judgement and to decide on what risks are tolerable, an easily
understood set of criteria should be set and followed rigorously. Risk criteria are required to promote
consistency in evaluating the results of relevant safety studies and to formulate a proactive approach to
accident and incident prevention.
2. PURPOSE
This Standard establishes the ADNOC criteria for evaluating and assessing HSE risks associated with
ADNOC, ADNOC Group Companies, Business Units and Affiliates operation.
Unless otherwise stated, all of the requirements set out in this Standard are Mandatory and it is the
responsibility of senior management of each GC, Business unit, affiliated operations to ensure that these
are implemented throughout the facilities that fall under their sphere of influence and, that subcontractors
working on their facilities are fully aware and compliant with these requirements.
The purpose of this Standard is to establish Risk Assessment and Management System framework to
ensure that Risks are identified, analysed, evaluated and treated in a consistent manner and assigned to
respective management tier for implementation, follow-up and closure. To achieve this objective, this
standard aims to:
Define Unified Risk Assessment and Management System Framework for ADNOC Group

Specify methods and tools for identifying hazards, risk analysis and evaluation using the Risk

Tolerability criteria defined in this standard.
Set a consistent risk tolerability criteria for qualitative, semi-quantitative and quantitative risk

assessments in line with ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix [Ref.2].
Unified ADNOC Group Corporate Risk Matrix [Ref.2] for all HSE risk assessment.

Aid robust and informed decisions making for management of HSE risk on a consistent basis

across ADNOC Group.

7.2.  HAZARD IDENTIFICATION AND RISK SCREENING
Hazards that may arise throughout the life cycle of ADNOC GC’s operation, the causes that could lead
to potential hazardous events resulting in undesirable consequences should be systematically identified.

Hazard Identification is the first step in risk assessment process. This can be done using structured
techniques such as HAZID, Inherently Safer Design (ISD) Review, HAZOP, SIMOPS, JSA, etc. or for
existing facilities can be through periodic review, audits, inspections, near miss reporting, incident
learning, etc.

Other processes at various stages of life cycle of the ADNOC GC operation such as P&ID Review, 3D
model review, HSE audit, HSE inspection, etc. may also provide input to hazard identification.

Once hazards are identified, for project phases, they are screened into Major Accident Hazards which
typically represents High and High-Medium Risks and Non-Major Accident Hazards which usually
represents Medium and Low Risk hazards (refer to section 7.3) .  For operations phase, based on the
risk mapping the hazards are screened into High, High-Medium, Medium and Low categories.

7.3. RISK ANALYSIS AND EVALULATION
The process of carrying out a risk assessment will result in an understanding of the level and significance
of HSE risks that leads to “Informed Decisions” related to the implementation of appropriate risk control
and risk reduction measures. The risk assessment consists of two parts; Risk Analysis and Risk
Evaluation.
Risk analysis involves for identified hazards, estimating potential consequences, should the hazard lead
to a hazardous event, estimating severity of these consequences, estimating likelihood of occurrence of
these consequences and calculating the risk level.
Risk evaluation refers to the process of making decisions on risk reduction requirement, by comparing
the estimated risks with an approved qualitative semi-quantitative or quantitative risk tolerability criteria
(e.g. Risk Classifications based on Risk Matrix, Quantitative Acceptance Criteria, etc.).
Risk assessment may be undertaken using qualitative, semi quantitative or quantitative methods. Each
of these methods are discussed briefly below.
7.3.1. QUALITATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT
In Qualitative Risk Analysis process, the risks are analysed based on expert judgment to judge the
likelihood and impact of the hazardous events. The estimated severity and likelihood are plotted on the
Risk Matrix to assign a risk level and category. Estimated risks are then evaluated for required action
based on the risk rank/risk level.
Where qualitative risk assessment identifies High and High Medium risks, further detailed risk
assessment using semi-quantitative or quantitative methods for specific hazard scenarios shall be carried
out to validate and detailed assessment before deciding on risk treatment options.
ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix [Ref. 2] provided in Appendix 2 of this Standard shall be used for carrying
out qualitative risk assessments. The ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix includes the definitions of
consequence severity levels and likelihood. It also indicates the HSE failure frequency and HSE likelihood
to estimate likelihood of scenarios. If the estimated consequence severity and or likelihood vary for
different categories e.g. people, asset etc. and then the highest severity shall be selected for determining
the overall risk level.
All risks must be assigned to one of the four categories as identified in ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix:
High

High-Medium

Medium




then Semi-Quantitative or Quantitative Risk Assessments shall be used to validate before deciding on

identified Major Accident Hazards which typically represents High and High-Medium Risks. The flowchart

7.3.2. SEMI-QUANTITATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT
Semi-quantitative risk assessments are structured risk assessment techniques which uses simple
consequence modelling techniques where applicable to derive estimates of the severity of the hazard
consequences and event trees and failure mode assessment to derive the likelihood of hazards resulting
in hazardous events.
Quantitative estimate of failure frequencies of specific equipment/systems (e.g. pump seal, vessel failure
due to overpressure, operator response etc.), reliability data of equipment/systems (e.g. SIS, DCS, PSVs,
etc.) are used to estimate the likelihood of events. These estimates can be combined with severity to
obtain estimates of the order of magnitude of the risk. Methods such as Scenario Based Risk Assessment
(SBRA) and Layers of Protection Analysis (LOPA) shall be used for carrying out semi-quantitative risk
assessments. These methods make use of techniques such as Fault Tree Analysis (FTA), Event Tree
Analysis (ETA) (refer to Appendix 10 for example), Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) (refer to
Appendix 9 for illustration), etc., to quantify the frequencies.
Scenario Based Risk Assessment is a systematic and structured method which uses combination of
qualitative assessment for initial screening followed by semi-quantitative technique to validate the
screened scenarios. SBRA technique is the recommended method for assessment of emerging risks
which are identified through existing process safety management systems (incident investigation,
management of change, audits, inspections, maintenance programs, HSE observations, design and
engineering reviews, safety reviews, other risk assessments, etc.) as part of routine operations of existing
facilities.
For each of the screened scenarios, specific event trees are developed detailing the chain of events
necessary to reach the various severity ranges and categories in the ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix. The
likelihood of each failure condition resulting in the scenarios is obtained based on the site failure rates
and supported by various reliable data sources such as IOGP, OREDA, etc. The severity level can be
assessed qualitatively or quantitatively based on nature of the identified hazard, information available and
timescale as per Risk Assessment team leader’s requirements. The validated High or High-Medium
Risks facilitates the discussion on the quality and health of key safeguards and associated risk reduction
measures to enable senior management to take informed risk based decisions to prioritize resources on
actions affecting the most significant risks. Structured Risk Assessment method using risk matrix which
uses SBRA principles is detailed in Appendix 5 and the template for the worksheet is shown in Appendix
6.
Layer of Protection Analysis (LOPA) typically uses order of magnitude categories for initiating event
frequencies, consequence severity and the likelihood of failure of Independent Protection Layers (IPF) to
approximate the risk of a scenario. LOPA is an analysis tool that usually builds on the information
developed during a qualitative hazard evaluation such as HAZOP. LOPA utilizes the inputs from HAZOP
to quantitatively assess the risk posed by specific hazardous scenarios after consideration of all valid
Independent Layers of Protection and applicable conditional modifiers. This residual risk can be then
used to demonstrate ALARP in line with tolerability criteria. For further details on LOPA method refer
ADNOC SIL Determination Standard, HSE-RM-ST05 [Ref. ; example of a LOPA worksheet is provided
in Appendix 7.
Usually LOPA method is used for process events, whereas SBRA takes a broader aspect which covers
process events, non-process events, routine emerging risks, unavailability of certain barriers, delays in
executions, etc. SBRA assigns the risk ownership based on current conditions and provides short term,
medium term and long term measures. Residual risk is identified against each of these measures and
risk remains till these measures are implemented. These risks are maintained and periodically reviewed
with management to give current risk level pictures for each assets.

7.3.3. QUANTIFIED RISK ASSESSMENT
Quantified risk assessment involves numerical estimate of the risk from a quantitative consideration of
event probabilities and severity of consequences. Quantified risk assessment yields a numerical indicator
of risk which is compared against ADNOC Risk Tolerability criteria. Detailed Quantified Assessment is
usually carried out in various studies such as QRA, FERA, BRA, etc. based on nature of receptors under
evaluations. These studies quantifies risk arising from all Major Accident Hazards from the plant/facilities
operations. These studies address both process and non-process hazards, however detailed key focus
is on MAH arising from the process facilities considering catastrophic nature of the consequences.
Quantified risk assessments must be conducted where it is mandated by legal/regulatory/ADNOC
requirements (e.g. as per ADNOC HSEIA Standard HSE-RM-ST02 [Ref. 6], and ADNOC COMAH
Standard HSE-RM-ST06 [Ref. 12]) or identified as tool to quantify the risk based on nature of operations
to establish that risks are managed to As Low As Reasonably Practicable (ALARP).
In carrying out quantitative risk assessments, specific quantitative tools and techniques are used for
estimating the severity of the consequences and the likelihood of the hazardous scenario occurring for
various identified scenarios within the study boundary.
Probability Determination:
Quantitative estimates of hazardous event likelihood are usually derived using a combination of the
following three approaches with full quantification of fault and event trees, and formal expert judgement:
Using relevant historical data;

Derivation through analytical modelling techniques; and

Using expert judgement.

It is possible to derive incident frequencies directly from historical records if the data are relevant and
applicable to the particular process/hazard under consideration. One advantage of using historical data
is that it already encompasses all common relevant contributory aspects including: reliability of equipment,
human factors, operational methods, quality of construction, inspection, maintenance and operation.
Modelling techniques to estimate incident frequencies from more basic data are used when suitable
historical data are not available or are inadequate. Various techniques are available, but the most
common and widely used techniques are Fault Tree Analysis (FTA) and Event Tree Analysis (ETA).
Physical Effects (Consequence) Modelling:
Quantitative estimates of the consequences of the hazard scenarios can be derived using appropriate
consequence modelling tools. Different mathematical models are usually used to estimate the physical
effect of the hazard. A range of models exists, from simple analytical empirical models, to complex and
more rigorous mathematical models using Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD). Care should be taken
to ensure that the methods and models used are appropriate to the problem being considered. For more
details on consequence modelling using CFD tools refer ADNOC CFD Dispersion and Explosion
Modeling Standard HSE-RM-ST014 [Ref. 10].
Risk Determination:
The risk is a function of the likelihood of occurrence of possible undesired events and the magnitude of
severity of the associated consequences. The risk estimation process is to combine the estimates of
likelihood and consequence for the identified hazardous events to produce a measure of the level of risk.

The ALARP principle which underlines the regulation of risk depicts three particular regions viz.:
the unacceptable region, the tolerable region and the broadly acceptable region.
At the top of the triangle is the unacceptable level, on or above which the risk is so great or the
outcome so unacceptable that it must be reduced immediately. At the other extreme is the broadly
acceptable region, where the risk is so low that there is no further requirement to undertake
additional risk reduction measures, i.e. the risk is, or has been made, so small that no further
precaution is warranted. In between these two extremes, lies a wide range of tolerable risk levels
to which the ALARP principle applies, i.e. the risk must be reduced to the lowest level practicable,
bearing in mind the benefits flowing from its acceptance and taking account of the costs of any
further reduction. Thus, for the risks which fall within the Tolerable region, some weighing of costs
and benefits is necessary to determine compliance with the ALARP principle.

7.5.2. ALARP PRINCIPLES
Application of the ALARP principle involves specifying two sets of risk tolerance criteria. The first set of
criteria, corresponding to the dividing line between the unacceptable and tolerable regions, usually called
the maximum tolerable risk for personnel or maximum Design Accidental Load (1E-4/Yr.) against each
MAH (Fire, explosion, etc.) for various receptors is a minimum requirement that must be met. The second
set of criteria, corresponding to the dividing line between the tolerable and broadly acceptable regions,
usually called the broadly acceptable risk, is a goal which may not be reached but towards which progress
must be made until risk reduction measures involve grossly disproportionate resources. The residual risk,
that is the risk remaining after controls have been implemented, should fall either in the broadly
acceptable region, or near the bottom of the tolerable region.
The key concept in using the ALARP principle is the trade-off between the benefits and costs of risk
reduction measures. Eventually a point of diminishing returns is reached wherein expenditure increases
markedly as risk reduction diminishes rapidly. Cost-benefit analysis (CBA) can be used with risk analysis
and entails a comparison of the sacrifice (costs) and the risk reduction (benefits) achieved for a risk
reduction measure.
CBA helps in making judgements on whether risk reduction measures are reasonably practicable. In CBA,
usually a measure is adopted only if benefits outweigh costs. However, in ALARP determinations, a
measure is adopted unless the sacrifice is grossly disproportionate to the risk. A Disproportion Factor is
used so that a risk reduction measure would not be considered worth the risk reduction achieved if:
Costs/Benefits > Disproportion Factor. The greater the risk, the greater should be the Disproportion Factor
to achieve a given benefit. The Cost-benefit analysis is useful to assist in deciding what risk reduction
measures are warranted for the costs involved. The use of a disproportion factor in the analysis formalizes
the concept of reasonably practicable and provides justification and transparency for decisions made.
7.5.3. THE COST BENEFIT ANALYSIS
The Cost Benefit Analysis (CBA) approach is an effective risk management tool as it aids consistency in
decisions for health, safety and environmental resource allocation. CBA aids the decision-making process
by giving monetary values to the costs and benefits and to enable a comparison of like quantities. The
analysis can help make an informed choice between risk reduction options and demonstrating ALARP
The CBA approach requires monetary evaluation of risks or the monetary evaluation of the loss. Some
typical examples are:
 Loss of plant, assets (e.g. rebuild cost);
 Loss of product and/or revenue;
 Loss of sales or customers (e.g. through loss of reputation);
 Loss of life;
 Loss of damage to a natural resource;
 Cost of clean-up (e.g. following accidental and/or chronic contamination); and
 Business interruptions.
A CBA shall not form the sole argument of risk management decision nor can it be used to undermine
existing standards and good practices.

7.5.4. IMPLIED COST TO AVERT FATALITY (ICAF)
QRA enables a better understanding of the relative risks associated with Risk Reduction options being
considered. Where risk is measured in terms of potential loss of life, perhaps in combination with
individual risk, then a measure of the relative safety of each option is derived. Each option will also have
an economic value attached to it. When the safest option is much more expensive than the less safe
option it is necessary to gauge the relative worth of each. While no amount of money can compensate
for the loss of life it would be unrealistic to assume that an investment aimed at the potential reduction of
loss of life must be made regardless of the size of that investment.
The evaluation of measures to avert loss of life requires ‘Valuation of life’ for which different figures have
been used by various industries and countries.
The ICAF is defined by the net cost of the risks reducing measures divided by the statistical number of
potential fatalities averted by the measures. It provides a useful indication of the effectiveness of risk
reduction measures.
Note:
ICAF provides a useful indication of the effectiveness of the Risk Reduction Measures but its use does
not however imply that money can compensate for the loss of life. The use of ICAF as a rigid and absolute
measure shall be avoided.
The net cost of the risk reducing measures is defined by the investments required minus the reduction in
statistical losses.

7.5.5. MAXIMUM JUSTIFIABLE SPEND (MJS)
Maximum Justifiable Spend (MJS) is a cost benefit analysis method that is similar to Implied Cost to Avert
Fatality (ICAF) approach. MJS addresses analysing cost of risk reduction measures verses benefits
attained in reducing asset losses and production losses. MJS is typically applied for the safety studies
such as FERA whereas ICAF is usually used in QRA.
MJS should be used as a guidance to evaluate the reasonable amount of money that could be justified
by CBA for specific risk reduction measure for a given receptor. Spending justification should be based
on the magnitude of the risk, priority in reducing the risk to lower level and demonstrating ALARP.
MJS provides criteria for identifying the additional risk reduction measures within the available options.
Risk reduction measures that requires spending more than the estimated MJS are deemed to be
disproportionate for the risk reduction achieved.

7.5.6. DEMONSTRATING ALARP
In all cases where risk lies in ‘Tolerable’ region, risk must be reduced to a level deemed tolerable with
ALARP demonstration reflecting among other factors local conditions and circumstances, the balance of
cost and benefits and the current state of scientific and technical knowledge. To reduce a risk to lowest
tolerable level practicable involves balancing reduction in risk to a level, objectively assessed, where the
trouble, difficulty and cost of further reduction measures becomes unreasonably disproportionate to the
additional risk reduction obtained.
Demonstrating ALARP requires consideration of all the hard and soft issues related to a range of options.
It requires a judgmental decision at the right level in the organisation with the full knowledge of all options,
associated risks and costs.
The ALARP demonstration shall include detailed technical rationale and supporting calculations that it
was not reasonably practicable to do more than that had been done to reduce risks.
ALARP demonstration by comparing costs of the various risk reduction options with the respective risks
should be provided.
Care should be taken in transferring risk to others/public as risk reduction option (e.g. exhaust, ventilation,
flaring, stack emissions etc.) as the added risk to these receptors might offset the benefits the measure
otherwise bring.
For emerging risks, full ALARP demonstration shall be carried out assessing the risk reduction potential
for each hazard, with a full range of risk reduction approaches considered, signed off as per sign-off
authority mentioned in ADNOC Corporate Risk Matrix (also refer to 7.6 of this Standard for reporting
requirements) and records maintained; this requirement is obligatory, not optional.
For activity-based risk assessments such as risk assessments carried out as part of PTW (e.g. Job Safety
Analysis, etc.), sign-off in the risk assessment could be considered as ALARP demonstration record.

PRIOR TO THE RISK ASSESSMENT
1. Identify Need for Risk Assessment: The need for a risk assessment could be based on a
periodic requirement, or special situations where a concern has been raised about the level of
risk. For special situations, ensure a documented risk assessment is required versus other
alternatives such as applying recognized and generally accepted good engineering practices as
a solution through pre-screening by the line Management. A risk assessment may be useful for
prioritization of potential issues that might be identified during a HAZOP or from a site-wide risk
assessment activity.
2. Set Risk Assessment Scope: The Line Management (through a person responsible for
managing the risk to be assessed) should set the scope by specifically identifying the facilities
and/or activities to be assessed, and the types of risks to be addressed (HSE or Business Loss).
The scope should also indicate whether the intent is to only assess current risk or to also identify
options to reduce risk prior to review with the sponsor.
3. Assemble Information for the Risk Assessment: For process designs, information needed
includes P & IDs and properties of any hazardous materials used in the process. For activities
such as crane lifts, copies of procedures may be needed. Incident event frequency data should
be available to help test the "reasonableness" of scenarios. Information should be developed that
defines how often, and how many, people could be located close to an incident were one to occur.
This information, along with information on ecological systems that could be affected by an
incident, is used to help assess consequences.
4. Identify Initial List of Hazards to be Assessed: An initial list of hazards to be assessed can be
identified using techniques such as HAZOP, brainstorming or site-wide risk assessment, or other
similar forms of risk assessment. Hazards are typically associated with a given operation or
design. They may also be associated with maintenance and inspection activities, plant upsets,
near misses or incidents.
5. Designate and Train the Risk Assessment Team: The Risk Assessment Coordinator/Team
Leader should designate a multi-skilled team similar to that used for a HAZOP. The team leader
must be trained and experienced in the use of the Risk Matrix. For team members not familiar
with the Risk Matrix, familiarization session can be accomplished prior to assembling the team,
or can be the first activity when conducting the risk assessment. It may be advantageous for GC
to select a group of individuals to intensively train in the assessment process. These become the
core risk assessment team leaders.
THE RISK ASSESSMENT
6. Develop Scenario(s) for each Hazard: For each hazard, there may be more than one scenario
of concern. Different initiating events are developed as different scenarios. Different
consequences as an outcome of an initiating event (different "event tree" path) also result in
different scenarios.
7. Describe Hazard and Initiating Event: For example, when handling or processing hazardous
materials, the hazard is the potential for a release and the initiating event leads to "loss of
containment". It is important that the initiating event represents a "basic cause" or reason for the
loss of containment. This helps in the assessment of the frequency of the initiating event, and in
determining what controls, safeguards or barriers are, or should be, in place to prevent the
initiating event.
8. Identify Existing Controls, Safeguards and Barriers to Prevent, Alert and/or Mitigate the
Scenario: Controls, safeguards and barriers are terms used interchangeably by different people
and organizations to describe engineering and administrative controls.
These barriers should only be considered if they will help "prevent, alert and/or mitigate" the
actual scenario. For example, just stating "the operation has emergency block valves/emergency
shutdown valves (EBV/ESDVs)" is not sufficient. The EBV/ESDVs must be designed, located,
tested, and maintained such that they would be available and used in the actual event.
9. Assess Scenario Consequences: The Health, Safety, Environmental, Reputation and Financial
consequences associated with the scenario should all be assessed by the team. The
consequences are the end point of one path of the "event tree" for the initiating event and
"success or failure" of the barriers.

used when making this comparison because some of the frequencies noted in this Guide are
event frequencies, not scenario frequencies. There may be many plausible scenarios that lead
to the "event"; individual scenarios leading to the event will typically have a lower frequency.
15. Identify Risk Reduction Options: If the risk is determined to be H CAT 1, HM CAT 2 or M CAT
3 the team should attempt to identify options that would reduce risk. Options could include
"inherent" risk reduction, like reducing the inventory of a hazardous material, options to reduce
initiating event frequency, improving the reliability of existing barriers or implementation of
additional barriers. If a L CAT 4 risk is being effectively managed with current HSEMS practices,
the team can recommend accepting the risk with no further action. Once risk is determined, risk
owner is assigned based on category of the risk. (Refer risk matrix for risk owner)
16. Reassess Scenario with Risk Reduction Options: improving or increasing the number of
barriers typically has the effect of decreasing the frequency of the scenario. There are some
"passive" (require no action/ always available) barriers, such as adding a dike around a tank, that
can reduce consequences. However, lowering the consequence generally requires reducing the
hazard through "inherent" measures such as using a less hazardous material, increased spacing,
or reduced inventory. A dike could still fail to contain a catastrophic tank failure.
17. Re-plot the Scenario on the Risk Matrix: After considering risk reduction options, the team
should re-evaluate the scenario and document the assumptions and rationale for any change in
probability range or consequence level.

assumptions and reference sources for probability or consequence assessment, should be

Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA)
APPENDIX 9
FAILURE MODES AND EFFECTS (FMEA) ANLAYSIS
While establishing failure frequencies for use in semi-quantitative risk assessment or event trees, generally failure
data from various databases such as IOGP are used. However, on many occasions more detailed analysis of
failure mode associated with specific equipment or scenario is required. In such as case, FMEA method can
used to define scenario specific failure frequencies.
Failure Modes and Effects Analysis (FMEA) and Failure Modes, are methodologies designed to identify potential
failure modes for a product or process before the problems occur, to assess the risk associated with those failure
modes and to identify and carry out measures to address the most serious concerns.
A FMEA identifies all potential failure modes for a system or subsystem along with their causes and effect on the
operation of the system. The effects are often further characterized by an assessment of the criticality of the
effect. In simple terms, criticality can be reported as no effect, safe or dangerous. The use of a structured method
is very important to being able to achieve reliable and consistent results from the analysis.
FMEA is one of the key method used in the Reliability Availability and Maintainability (RAM) studies and various
standards are available on how to carry FMEA. In the context of semi-quantitative risk assessment role of failure
mode assessment is limited to determine the failure rate for specific operations or task or activity being carried
out. Key steps involved are shown in figure below.
