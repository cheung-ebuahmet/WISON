1. INTRODUCTION
This document illustrates ADNOC requirements for the management and execution of Safety Integrity
Level (SIL) Determination studies. This standard utilizes guidance provided on IEC 61508 [Ref. 3] and
IEC 61511 [Ref.4], to provide a clear procedural steps against which all ADNOC SIL Determination
studies shall be completed.
SIL Determination studies forms a vital step in the broader ADNOC Process Safety Lifecycle for its
facilities and projects and the effective design of reliable Safety Instrumented Systems (SIS).
2. PURPOSE
The purpose of this document is to define the basis, scope and methodology for the completion of Safety
Integrity Level (SIL) Determination studies across ADNOC Group. The purpose of a SIL Determination
study is to establish the level of risk reduction required for SIS to reduce risks from identified process
hazards (including explosion, toxic exposure, loss of containment, etc.) to as low as reasonably
practicable (ALARP). The functional output of a SIL Determination study is to assign each Safety
Instrumented Function (SIF) a performance target or Safety Integrity Level (SIL) which can then dictate
the design of the SIF to be installed with regards to its reliability.
All existing or prospective SIF installed on ADNOC facilities must comply with the requirements outlined
in IEC 61508 [Ref. 3] and IEC 61511 [Ref. 4], necessitating that every SIF be assessed to determine its
corresponding SIL. SIL will determine through the analysis the potential hazardous scenarios the level of
reliability the SIF must achieve to meet the required residual risk level. Following this procedure will
ensure compliance of the SIF assessed with IEC standards.
This SIL determination study will serve as a basis of the design and specification of all SIS and associated
SIF. Design of the SIS and associated SIF for the assigned SIL levels will be confirmed through SIL
verification study as required by IEC 61511/61508.
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to the ADNOC Group and its
Contractors.
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood,
implemented, complied with and monitored at all times.

7. REQUIREMENTS
7.1. SIL DETERMINATION THROUGH PROJECT/FACILITY LIFECYCLE
The SIL Determination is an integral part of the risk management process to ensure that the risk is
managed and demonstrated to ALARP for both project activities and operations.
SIL Determination Study is performed during the FEED Stage of the project and is later updated during
the EPC (Detailed Engineering) Stage based on updated safety studies, documents and newly-
established information/data. In addition, SIL Determination Study shall be performed for newly added
SIF functions if any during the EPC stage of the project. For the package facilities, SIL study shall be
carried out during the EPC stage of the project. SIL determination study report later forms part of the
HSEIA dossier.
For existing facilities, where SIL Determination Study is not available, it shall be carried out at the first
available opportunity (earliest) and update the HSEIA Dossier accordingly as per ADNOC Standard for
HSEIA [Ref. 8].
As part of 5-year COMAH/HSEIA update, SIL determination study shall be reviewed to determine if
significant changes to facilities, SIFs, etc. are observed or carried out. SIL determination study shall be
updated based on these changes to ensure assessment to reflect the updated changes to SIFs.
Where no significant changes are identified over five years and outcome of previous SIL determination
Study is still applicable and technically robust, no update of SIL determination is required. In such case,
GC CHSE shall develop technical note as part of HSEIA process justifying the same with relevant
supporting documents (such as HAZID, risk register review, risk assessments, MOC, etc.) and shall
submit to ADNOC as part of HSEIA process for Regulatory Approval.

7.2.2. PREPARATION PHASE
(i) The Preparation Phase typically includes the following activities:
1. Identifying and locating supporting data and information;
2. Project management preparations;
3. Consensus on SIL Determination Study Scope; and
4. Development of Terms of Reference.
(ii) Facilities
The meeting room should be appropriate for the SIL Determination Study techniques that are to
be adopted, e.g. large enough for the entire team, curtains/blinds may be required if an overhead
projector is to be used, and there should be adequate wall space to display the drawings. The
appropriate hardware (computer, overhead projector and printer) should be made available during
the workshop. It is also important that adequate refreshments are provided as this helps with
maintaining concentration and in building a team spirit.
(iii) Terms of Reference (TOR)
TOR shall be developed for each SIL Determination study. Though this Standard ensures
consistency across all studies performed in ADNOC Group, each study shall have separate terms
of reference defining the key elements of specific study including, but not limited to:
1. The scope and exclusions including existing SIF with defined rationale (if any that are
impacted by the specific project);
2. The team members;
3. Reference documentation; and
4. Tentative schedule.
The TOR shall be issued and approved by SME from ADNOC Group prior to the SIL Determination
workshop.
7.2.3. EXAMINATION PHASE
The Examination Phase begins with identification of all SIFs within the facility to be considered. The study
will proceed until performance gaps that may exist are identified for all assessed hazardous scenarios
and reliability requirements (in the form of a Target SIL) are provided for all SIF within the scope.
7.2.4. SIL STUDY DURATION
Predicting the time required for a SIL Determination study is always a matter of judgement. It is however
important that a realistic schedule be developed for the whole study. The overall timing must be
established in order that team members can be released from other tasks. The timing is dependent upon
the experience of the SIL Determination Study Chairman, the size of the team, method of SIL
Determination Study and the number of SIFs to be assessed. The size of the team is determined by the
complexity of the study but may also include the involvement of engineering contractors, subcontractors
and vendors. The Chairman should estimate the overall time for the study based on the predicted length
of the individual SIF, the session length and the number of schedule sessions per day.

7.3.1. RECOMMENDATIONS
In addition to the SIL for each identified SIF, SIL Determination process should provide recommendations
to further minimise the risk associated with scenarios assessed.
High demand mode SIF (Initiating Event Likelihood more than 1 a year) must be avoided. When the SIL
Determination Study identifies a high demand on the SIF, recommendation should be provided to lower
the demand rate on the SIF to less than once a year.
It is recommended that where risk analysis shows a high risk reduction that SIL 4 to be necessary, the
difficulty of achieving a SIL 4 safety instrumented function in the process sector should be noted. Is such
case, consideration should be given to using multiple independent SISs, of lower integrity or non-process
control IPLs.
Closed (simple) Actions
It is preferable for the team to agree and recommend simple actions, which can be defined as 'closed',
because they do not ask the responding engineer to consider options. The team should be of enough
experience and seniority in order to make design decisions for the project. The team should therefore be
able to take decisions whilst not spending undue time developing solutions to complex problems.
Open (complex) Actions
If the team is divided in its view, or if there is not enough time available to complete the discussion, an
open action (recommendation for further work) may be appropriate. It is most important that the team's
concern is clearly established in the worksheet so that the responding engineer knows the scope of study
required and the objective they are trying to achieve. It is often helpful to offer a preferred solution whilst
giving the responding engineer scope to suggest alternatives.

7.3.3. SIL DETERMINATION STUDY TEAM
A SIL Determination Study team usually consists of five to eight people though this number may be
reduced for small facilities or studies performed in response to change orders. If a system is particularly
complex or a particularly large number of stakeholders are involved in its design/operation (including
licensors, vendors, EPCs, etc.) a larger team may be required to ensure sufficient expertise is available
during the study. If the team is too large, the group approach central to the SIL Determination Study will
begin to break down and will naturally gravitate towards imbalance between the inputs from attendees.
As such, all effort should be made to restrict the team size and, where practical, specialist input can be
sought prior to the meeting or only when required.
The SIL Determination Study team is multi-disciplinary and the effective completion of the study relies
upon the shared experience of the team to answer questions raised and accurately assess the system
under consideration. The SIL Determination Study team should consist of participants knowledgeable in
process, risk, safety and environmental protection technology, including the attendance of technical
specialists if the design/facility under assessment demands:
(a) SIL Determination Study Chairman
(b) SIL Determination Study Secretary
(c) SIL Determination Study Team Members

Layer of protection analysis (LOPA) is a semi-quantitative tool for analyzing and assessing risk. LOPA typically
uses order of magnitude categories for initiating event frequency, consequence severity, and the likelihood of
failure of independent protection layers (IPLs) to approximate the risk of a scenario. LOPA is an analysis tool that
typically builds on the information developed during a qualitative hazard evaluation, such as a HAZOP.

LOPA is carried out with a set of specific criteria and restrictions for the definition and evaluation of Independent
Protection Layers (IPLs), serving to eliminate subjectivity inherent in more qualitative methods (including HAZOP)
while avoiding the increased cost and effort that would be required for a full quantitative assessment.

A LOPA utilises the inputs from HAZOP to quantitatively assess the risk posed by specific hazardous event
scenarios and subsequently assess the adequacy of safeguards currently in place to control these hazards. The
output of LOPA is the residual risk posed by a hazardous scenario after consideration of all valid Independent
Layers of Protection (IPL) and applicable Conditional Modifiers.

LOPA Overview
LOPA is an analytical tool used in the assessment of the adequacy of protection layers used to mitigate process
risk; LOPA is also used in the determination of target SIL for identified SIF.

Selection of Event Scenarios
The event scenarios to be included within a LOPA should be based upon the results of the associated
HAZOP.
The format in which scenarios should be detailed in a HAZOP worksheet is provided in ADNOC Hazard
and Operability Study (HAZOP) Standard [Ref. 2] and the information included can be transferred to a
LOPA Worksheet as follows:
 HAZOP Consequence = LOPA Consequence
 HAZOP Cause = LOPA Initiating Event
The consequence included in the LOPA worksheet, taken from the associated HAZOP, should be
sufficiently detailed to fully explain the unmitigated impact (i.e. if all barriers, IPLs and safeguards were
to fail simultaneously) the event scenario would have with regards to all risk categories considered by
ADNOC, specifically:
 Health & Safety – Injuries and fatalities resulting from fires, explosions and toxic releases.
Typically estimated in terms of a number of onsite and offsite fatalities or injuries.
 Environment – Impact upon the environment surrounding a facility or more general considerations
for release limits and pollution. As environmental harm can be multi-faceted and hard to quantify,
generally estimated in a qualitative sense for the purposes of HAZOP and LOPA.
 Financial – Impact upon ADNOC’s financial position including direct loss of capital (due to damage
to facilities and equipment) and consequential business loss resulting from stoppage in production.
Financial impacts are estimated quantitatively based upon the team’s understanding of facility
being assessed.
Though the ADNOC Group Risk Matrix includes potential consideration for impacts to ADNOC Reputation,
it is not considered applicable controlling risk categories for the purposes of LOPA.
Determination of Initiating Events
Following the selection of event scenarios to be included within a LOPA, the Initiating Events (IEs) for
each scenario can then be identified, along with their associated Initiating Event Frequency.
Initiating causes of hazardous process scenarios will generally fall into one of three categories:
 Equipment Failure:
 Equipment related initiating events can either be due to failure of control systems or
mechanical failure;
 Control system events may be caused due to component failures (e.g. transmitters, switches,
valves), software faults or utility failures (loss of electricity, instrument air, etc.); and
 Mechanical failures can be due to any number of reasons, including corrosion, fatigue,
improper design, vibration and hydraulic hammer.
 Human Error:
 Failure to properly execute steps of a task in a proper sequence or omission of steps; and
 Failure to observe or respond appropriately to conditions or other prompts by the system or
process.

risk associated with each IE should be calculated prior to their summation.
Identification of IPLs
For consideration with a LOPA, an IPL is an active or passive system, capable of preventing a scenario
from progressing to an undesired consequence, which meets the following requirements:
 Effectiveness – The IPL is capable of performing its intended function when demanded and to the
standard intended, and it is capable on its own of preventing a threat from developing into a
hazardous occurrence. The IPL must provide a quantifiable level of protection against the
consequence it is intended to mitigate and, in doing so, reduce the identified risk by a quantifiable
minimum amount.
 Specificity – The IPL is designed solely to prevent or mitigate the consequence of single
hazardous event. Multiple initiating causes may necessitate the action of a single IPL if they would
lead to the same hazardous event.
 Independence – The IPL is independent of all other protection layers associated with the identified
hazardous event. Independence requires that the performance of the IPL must not be in any way
affected by the failure of any other protection layer or share any common cause failures with other
protection layers.
 Auditability – The IPL must be designed to enable a periodic evaluation of its protection
functioning. All IPLs must be proof tested and maintained as per ADNOC HSECES Management
Standard [Ref. 7].

be assigned a Probability of Failure on Demand (PFD). A list of typical IPLs for consideration in a LOPA
assessment, along with generic PFD data, can be found in Table 1.2 of Appendix 2 [Ref. 5 Section 5]
Mitigation Barriers
Protective layers for hazardous scenarios may be considered preventative or mitigation. Preventative
barriers will serve to prevent the hazardous occurrence entirely while mitigation barriers will only seek to
minimise or entirely prevent the ultimate consequence of the occurrence. Examples of mitigation barriers
include active fire protection systems, dikes/bunds and blast walls. Those these barriers will not prevent
a loss of containment or other hazardous top event, they can still qualify as IPLs if they meet the
aforementioned criteria and can fully prevent the ultimate hazardous consequence (be it harm to
personnel, the environment or ADNOC assets).
Mitigation barriers are, by their nature, not specific to initiating events but instead specific to
consequences and specific areas of a facility in which they are installed. It is important to consider that
many mitigation barriers, especially those relating to emergency response activities and evacuation, are
not credibly applicable to off shore facilities.
