1. INTRODUCTION
Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC), as part of its Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy,
commits to ensure that a safe working environment is maintained in all areas and that all personnel who
undertake activities/operations where there is threat due to the exposure of hazardous Hydrogen Sulfide
(H S), are fully aware of the hazards involved and have the competence, training and awareness to
2
undertake such activities/operations.
The criticality of activities involving the exposure of H S is underlined by the fact that it is part of the Life-
2
Saving Rules, which call for a commitment by all ADNOC employees and contractors to always follow
the defined rules, at all times, without exception.
This complements the embracing of ADNOC’s 100% HSE, which means that we will always find the time
to do it safely or not do it at all.
H S is a colourless gas commonly associated with ADNOC Group oil and gas operations. It is highly toxic
2
causing fatality after as little as ten seconds’ exposure to high concentrations. It has been responsible for
the death of many people worldwide, including many cases where people have been overcome
attempting to rescue colleagues.
2. PURPOSE
The purpose of this standard is to provide an overall understanding of hazards associated with H S and
2
its application within the ADNOC Group. This standard establishes a consistent safety guideline to the
personnel involved in the activities where hazardous H S may be present within the ADNOC Group.
2
This Standard applies to all ADNOC Group operations and activities where there is a H S hazard to
2
people.
The requirements of this Standard shall be considered for all stages of the facility life cycle whenever a
H S hazard exists. They shall also apply to ADNOC Group operations where potential personnel
2
exposure to H S is of an intermittent nature such as when a marine vessel where there is normally no
2
H S hazard is working alongside an offshore facility platform handling hydrocarbon containing H S.
2 2
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates &
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood,
implemented and thoroughly monitored.
4. LAWS AND REGULATIONS
The relevant UAE legislations applicable to this Standard includes but not limited to:
(i) UAE Federal Law No. 8 (1980), Chapter V: Safety, Protection, and the Health and Social
Care of the Employees; Article (91).
(ii) Federal Law No.: 24 of 1999 for the Protection and Development of the Environment.
(iii) UAE Ministerial Order No. (32), 1982. Specifying Preventive Methods and Measures for
Protecting Workers against Work Hazards.
ADNOC Group shall ensure that their activities comply with all relevant UAE Federal Local laws
and regulations at all times, including any that may be introduced after the publication of this
Standard.

7. REQUIREMENTS
7.1. DESIGN
7.1.1. PROCESS AND MECHANICAL CONSIDERATIONS
The facility design shall consider H S concentration in the fluids as key factor since it causes severe
2
corrosion and sulphide stresses resulting in Loss of Primary Containment.
Piping and vessels shall be designed and installed in such a way that areas of insufficient flow (including
dead-end piping) are as far as possible eliminated. Where this cannot be accomplished, provisions shall
be made for draining of accumulated fluids. Drain systems for H S laden fluids shall be designed to
2
prevent migration of H S from one point in the facility to another.
2
H S containing facilities shall be designed to ensure minimum interference during normal operation and
2
maintenance. Some of the measures which are typically used to achieve minimum intervention are as
below:
(i) Provision of dual control valves, so that control can be diverted to a back-up valve if failure
occurs;
(ii) Design to avoid the use of pumps, using pressure driven liquid transfer, where possible;
(iii) Provision of transmitters for remote reading of equipment, rather than local instruments and
level gauges;
(iv) Using high reliability instruments or redundant and diverse instruments where a single
traditional instrument would be provided along with a level gauge or sight glass in earlier
design practice;
(v) Using primarily welded construction rather than flanges wherever possible;
(vi) Screwed fittings shall not be allowed on H S containing piping;
2
(vii) Planning integrity inspections so that these can be carried out during major turn-rounds,
when the plant is depressurised and preferably emptied of gas; and
(viii) Design which allows major maintenance when the plant is shut down for major turn-rounds
and emptied of H S gas.
2
All these measures shall be considered and implemented unless demonstrated as not reasonably
practicable.
7.1.2. MATERIALS CONSIDERATIONS
When exposed to an environment containing H S, many materials may suddenly fail in a brittle manner.
2
Failure occurs by a form of embrittlement known as sulfide stress cracking. Susceptibility of a given
material to sulfide stress cracking increases as strength and tensile stress (residual or applied) increase.
Material hardness is frequently used as an indirect measure of strength and sometimes is referenced as
a limiting parameter.

7.1.3. MATERIALS SELECTION
Metallic materials satisfactory for use in H S environments and the conditions under which they shall be
2
used are described in NACE Standard such as MROI75 [Ref. 12]. The latest revision of this standard
shall be consulted when selecting materials for use in H S environments. The provisions of NACE
2
Standard MROI75 shall be considered minimum standards, with the equipment user free to apply more
stringent specifications.
The material requirements of NACE Standard MROI75 [Ref. 12] offer resistance to sulfide stress cracking;
however, other forms of corrosion and modes of failure (such as pitting, hydrogen-induced cracking, and
chloride cracking) shall be considered in the design 17and operation of equipment. Control of failures by
mechanisms other than sulfide stress cracking shall be mitigated by chemical inhibition, material selection,
and environmental controls (refer to NACE Standard MROI75, Par. 1 .i) [Ref. 12].
Manufacturer certification of compliance with NACE Standards such as MROI75 [Ref. 12] for equipment
intended for H S environment shall be provided.
2
H S is corrosive to steel, especially when present with free water. The sulfur combines with iron, freeing
2
hydrogen. Hydrogen Induced Cracking (HIC) occurs when hydrogen diffuses into the steel and collects
at inclusions or defects. Pressure can become very high and cracks and blistering can occur. Harder
steels and areas such as un-heat treated welds are more susceptible to such cracking.
Stress Corrosion Cracking (SCC) occurs on passivated materials such as stainless steels subjected to
tensile stress. Micro cracks open up slightly, and because there is little oxygen in the cracks the
passivating oxide layer is not formed, corrosion can occur. Classic SCC is then caused by chlorides (or
other halides), but H S can accelerate the corrosion. The combination of sour salt water is particularly
2
corrosive.
Stress Oriented Hydrogen Induced Cracking (SOHIC) occurs where there is a high stress concentration
in sour service equipment. High stress fields allow hydrogen generated by H S dissociation to accumulate
2

7.1.7. SELECTION AND PROTECTION OF PIPELINES AND FLOW LINES
Increasing pipe wall thickness by selection of a higher schedule or class than that required for service is
a standard risk reduction approach. Increasing thickness reduces stress, provides an increased corrosion
allowance, and makes pipelines more robust against third party interference. The technique is generally
used where there is specific cases where very high severity or sensitive receptors are located, such as
in pipeline sections near villages or through towns, ultra-sour gases or process fluids within the pipeline
and flow lines, etc.
Third party interference is one of the major causes of pipeline failure. Risk to pipelines can be reduced
by various techniques. Techniques which shall be considered in design are deep burial, use of designated
and fenced rights of way or easements, use of mounding to signal pipe location and to discourage
excavation and driving over pipes, and use of plastic marker strips at 30 cm above pipeline depth.
Many of these techniques can be selected as standard, for example use of dedicated rights of way, and
mounding of the pipelines. Some introduce additional costs, and many have large effect or limited effect,
depending on circumstances. An example is that of selection of burial depth, which may have large impact
in an industrial area, but little effect in the desert.
Sectioning the pipeline can be an important measure to limit the consequences of pipeline failure and is
of particular relevance for sour gas pipelines. The use of Sectionalisation can aid in controlling the
duration of event and also aid in design of blowdown provision for the sectionalized section. Together
these two factors not only will reduce the duration of event but also provide benefits in reducing impacted
areas considerably due to depressurization. The advantages of sectioning though shall be weighed
against the additional possibilities of leaks from block valves and associated flanges considering potential
cost benefits and number of personnel at risk.
An important issue in onshore operations is avoidance of pipeline bridging due to sand drift. Flow line and
some gathering lines are susceptible to this.
Design measures can include avoidance of sand drift areas, organization of flow lines in engineered
corridors and good support design. There is no absolute preventive measure for bridging due to sand
movement however and periodic inspection is required. Maximum bridging length criteria for pipes shall
be calculated, for design where bridging is likely (sand dune areas) and remediation applied where
bridging occurs.
The most common context of third-party interference is during construction of or maintenance on
neighbouring pipelines. For these activities, a thorough risk assessment shall be performed where all
concerned parties are represented.
Similarly, for subsea pipelines, factors which shall be addressed to prevent accidents as far as reasonably
practicable include:

7.1.8. MINIMIZATION OF PIPEWORK FAILURE
The following are common pipework failure mechanisms:
(i) External dripping water from condensation at road overpasses and conduits causing heavy
external corrosion;
(ii) External corrosion by pipes touching soil or sand;
(iii) Missing or ineffective pipe supports leading to overstress or vibration;
(iv) Pipes “crawling” due to alternating (diurnal) heating and cooling, resulting in pipes resting
on, or even curling around each other, creating possible points for galvanic corrosion;
(v) Cavitation downstream of control valves;
(vi) Vibration and slugging at vertical two-phase flow lines;
(vii) Condensate pooling in gas lines, followed by condensate pick up, resulting in vibration or
hammer; and
(viii) Pitting corrosion especially in dead legs and at the internal 6’o clock position in pipes such
as those on infrequently used (spare) pump piping, pigging piping and some manifold
sections.
All such mechanisms shall be taken account when designing facilities, including modifications, and also
in carrying out inspection and corrosion management activities.
Failure frequencies used in Quantitative Risk Analysis (QRA) and ALARP analyses can be adjusted to
take into account such factors using structural reliability methods used in Risk Based Inspection (RBI) as,
for example, in API RP 581 [Ref. 22 & 23] as sensitivity cases for existing facilities / operations. Note that
existing standard RBI techniques may need to be extended by reference to research reports to cover
issues such as those listed above.
Acoustic Induced Vibration and Flow Induced Vibration Analysis should be conducted to prevent pipework
failure due to vibration.
Pipe supports shall be designed to prevent external corrosion. Best practice is to provide pipe shoes. On
smaller pipes, and when back fitting is needed, use of neoprene rubber support pads provides good
corrosion reduction, though they still allow (possibly salt) water collection at the contact point. In all cases,
steel pipe on steel support contact shall be avoided.
Pipe leaks into low lying parts of plants, such as bunded areas, sumps and lagoons shall be considered
for potential for release of sour gas, or of oil with a potential to release H S.

(i) All equipment where opening is expected once per six months or more frequently;
(ii) All spared equipment; and

7.1.11. FLARE SYSTEM AND COLD VENTING
Cold venting occurs when vents or safety valves release directly to atmosphere or where gas is sent to
flare (from vents and from pressure safety valves) and there is a possibility of the flare flame-out. (for
various reasons, including but not limited to, nitrogen release into the flare, CO relieving from CO rich
2 2
sour gas, low gas flow, high wind speeds, loss of ignition and flammable liquid in the flare, etc.).
Electronic spark ignition system shall be provided to maintain pilot flame and High Energy Spark Ignition
System shall be also be provided to prevent prolonged cold venting (sometimes called cold flaring). The
reliability of these igniters shall be studied, and adequate redundancy shall be provided to bring risks to
tolerable region and ALARP demonstrated.
Relief valves venting sour gas to atmosphere shall be avoided by design. Reliefs shall be routed to a
suitable flare or an acid gas recovery system. For remote and unmanned facilities such as well head
towers, well pad areas, etc. infrequent venting such as maintenance vents, cold drum vents, etc where
provision of providing acid gas recovery system or flare is impractical, alternate provisions / means shall
be considered in line with ALARP principle. This shall take into account amount of gases to be vented,
H2S concentration within the process fluid, dispersion modelling / pattern and location of various
receptors such as adjacent facilities, manned areas, etc. with respect to vent locations;
It shall be noted that standard dispersion calculations used for design of vents , flare flame-out, relief
releases, etc. are based on simple empirical models, which ignore secondary aspects of gas dispersion
such as vent downwash, building downwash, obstruction, wind in opposite directions, congestion,
stripping of the vent jet by the wind, etc. . Therefore, for such cases dispersion modelling using more
sophisticated modelling approaches such as CFD dispersion may be adopted.

7.1.12. SUMPS AND DRAINS
Sumps and drains are a frequent area for incidents. These potential incidents can be prevented by
implementing safe operating practices such as confined space entry precautions. There are cases
however where H S can be emitted from sumps and drains. The most important ones are admission of
2
rich amine solution to drains, admission of hot water (steam condensate) to drains, and gas release into
closed drains due to level control failure.
Amine solutions which are drained down from process equipment shall be collected, not released to
drains. This may be done by providing a closed sump, from which solution can be returned to storage, or
sent to disposal, or by draining to a portable vessel.
Drainage of hot water to drains needs to be controlled procedurally (e.g. under PTW). It is particularly
likely when draining steam condensate during steaming out for maintenance. For this reason, it is good
practice to ensure that drains and sewers which could contain amine (or caustic solutions used for
sweetening) are flushed prior to major maintenance.
Where the possibility of toxic gas release to drains exists as a result of level control failure, appropriately
SIL rated shutdowns shall be provided. Also, the area around sumps and drains shall be H S classified
2
accordingly.

The prime safety measures during drilling and workover are good control of mud weight and blow out

standardized drilling and workover arrangements are used.

7.1.18. MINIMIZATION OF H S EMISSIONS
2
The principle of emission reduction for H S is to keep the gas inside the equipment. This means sealing
2
all openings. The European Sealing Association has given statistics concerning the sources of Volatile
Organic Compound (VOC) releases as well as best practice. Although H S has some other potential
2
sources, the majority of releases resemble those for VOCs.
For new plants, it is highly desirable that chronic H S emissions are calculated prior to operation.
2
Emissions can be calculated on the basis of Emission Factors (EF) which give the average rate of release
from specific equipment types. The total fugitive emission rate depends not just on the leak rate, but also
on the fraction of equipment which are leaking. The US Environmental Protection Agency has given tables
both of leaking fraction (LF – the fraction of a particular type of equipment found to be leaking at any one
time) and EFs.
For actual exposure protection close to a source for employee protection, the expected release rates (ER)
shall be calculated as:
ER = EF/LF
Appropriate dispersion calculations can then be used to determine at what distance TLV-TWA and STEL
concentrations will occur. Then:
(i) Exposure time in these zones shall be at most a fraction of the time to exceed the TLV-TWA
and STEL;
(ii) Release reduction measures shall be implemented; or
(iii) Operating procedures shall contain warnings and require PPE.
(a) Valve Seals
Valve seals are intended to prevent leakage along the movable stem of the valve. Use of low
emission seals can give release rates 1,000 times smaller than simple traditional seals (mineral
fibre rings). Best practice in packing selection shall be used. In hazardous cases, the use of
bellow-sealed valves shall be considered.
Pump Seals
Pumps handling fluids with significant H S (defined as greater than 500ppm by volume in the
2
vapour phase when flashed to atmospheric pressure) shall be a minimum of double-seal design
with a seal fluid or shall be of seal-less (fully closed or “canned”) construction. Seal fluid system
shall be designed to minimize H S build up in the seal fluid and seal fluid reservoirs, shall take
2
operator and maintenance personnel safety into account. Seal fluid systems shall be designed to
alarm following failure of one seal to allow intervention before a second seal fails.

Alternative designs may be acceptable provided that the manufacturer/vendor can demonstrate a
performance close to zero release.
Reciprocating compressor seals shall have a high integrity primary seal, and flushing gas. For
lethal service compressors, best practice is to have a spacer chamber with primary and secondary
seals and inert gas flushing of the spacer chamber.
Sulphur Pits
Sulphur pits are designed for degassing the sulphur and are agitated, therefore resulting in free
H S gas emission. Good practice is to ventilate (extract) with air and pass the air to an incinerator
2
or thermal oxidizer.
Liquid sulphur degassing down to 5ppm H S is regarded as good practice. To achieve this, several
2
proprietary processes are available, including sulphur spraying and the use of stripping and
columns.
Sulphur Flakers
In a flaker, liquid sulphur is passed between cooled rollers. The sulphur cools and freezes to form
bright yellow flakes. In the process part of the residual H S is released. This is particularly the case
2
if the sulphur is “off spec” due to failures in the degassing process.
To keep H S emissions low, sulphur flakers are ventilated to a safe location, usually to a high vent
2
because of the low H S emission rate. New flakers shall be completely enclosed to minimize
2
emissions.
Drilling
Mud rooms on platforms offshore drill rigs and mud shakers on onshore rigs are a typical source
of H S exposure during the period where the drill is approaching or is in sour oil or gas producing
2
locations.
Adequate ventilation shall be provided, and drilling crew operators approaching the area shall be
provided with exposure monitors as well as emergency escape devices. Closed areas such as
mud rooms shall be provided with fixed gas alarms.

7.2. H S CLASSIFICATION ZONES
2
H2S classification zones shall be defined for all facilities in production, handling, processing,
transportation and storage of process fluid containing H S more than 100 ppm in stream. The key
2
objectives of defining H S classified areas are:
2
 To ensure that safety measures taken, provide a level of safety based on the toxic dose arising
from H S exposure.
2
 To provide “Inherent Risk Reduction Measures” based on level of risk during the design and for
operations.
 To define minimum requirements against H 2 S hazards during design and operation of facilities.
Based on nature of H S hazard and associated risk, facilities shall be delineated in three zones which are
2
Red Zone, Amber Zone and Yellow Zone. Initially these zones are identified based on the toxic dispersion
profile and toxic risk as theoretical zones. These are later converted to more meaningful practical zones.
Where, empirical models are used for the dispersion modelling, facility spatial features and terrain factor
such high hills, obstructions, walls, sea, etc. are not taken into consideration. Additionally, theoretical
zones may not match with the natural boundaries, unit boundaries, access provisions, etc. Therefore,
these theoretical zones shall be updated as practical zone taking into account these site/facility specific
factors.

absolute minimum;
(ii) Elimination/Reduction of personnel entry to Red Zones as far as reasonably practicable;
(iii) Reduction in the size of Amber and Yellow zones;

7.2.2. PLANNING OF H S ZONING STUDIES
2
The size of the Red, Amber and Yellow zones and the area impacted is probably best evaluated in
conjunction with the project/facility QRA, which is also part of the COMAH / HSEIA.
The scope of QRAs shall include evaluation of Red, Amber and Yellow Zones as defined in ADNOC
Quantitative Risk Assessment (QRA) Standard [Ref. 51]. Red, Amber and Yellow zones shall also be
evaluated for simultaneous activities such as Unit Maintenance while an adjacent unit is in production,
well interventions beside a producing well, etc. The scope shall include development of theoretical zone,
initial practical zone and H2S zoning workshop for finalization of the practical zones.
It is important that zoning study is initiated at an early stage in the project (FEED), so that any practical
restrictions, requirement and engineering risk reduction measures for H2S zones, are taken into account
in equipment design and selection and detailed engineering. As the Emergency Planning & Awareness
Zones (EPZ and EAZ) need to be communicated with Stakeholders, this shall also be evaluated at early
stages on the facility.
Zoning studies shall be updated during EPC stages to reflect latest project documentations / information
such that all calculations, dispersion & risk modelling and theoretical & practical zoning reflect “as built
information” as far as practicable. Zoning studies shall be approved, and risk reduction measures shall
be implemented prior to introduction of Hydrocarbon containing H S.
2
It is also important that Zone restrictions are taken into account when designing Health, Safety and
Environment Critical Equipment and Systems (HSECES), especially when considering the practicality of
inspection and testing, which are required to ensure that the element meets its designated performance
standard. In general, all the efforts shall be made to locate HSECES, which require regular inspections
and testing, outside Red Zones where practical. Similarly, firefighting systems (e.g. fire monitors) located
in a H S Zone should be considered for remote operation.

7.4.6. MONITORING EQUIPMENT-MAINTENANCE, TESTING AND CALIBRATION
The equipment used for monitoring the exposure levels of H S shall be serviced and tested at intervals
2
recommended by the manufacturer, and possibly more frequently under extreme humidity, temperature,
dust or other adverse environmental operating conditions.
Status calibration shall be checked before every use of portable gas monitors in accordance with the
manufacturer's recommendation. Records of device testing and maintenance shall be kept for verification
and audit purposes.
Bump Testing
Bump testing is the only way to ensure proper sensor and alarm functionality. A bump test is
defined as the process of briefly exposing sensors in a gas detector to an expected concentration
of gas that is greater than the alarm set points. The purpose of the bump test is to check for sensor
and alarm functionality. However, it does not check for accuracy. It is important to note that
accuracy is ensured through calibration, which is a completely different process than bump testing.
The bump test for personal gas detectors shall be done once in a month or according to
manufacturer’s recommendation whichever comes earlier. However, for portable gas monitors
used by Authorized Gas Tester shall be bump tested before every use. Records for the bump
testing shall be maintained.
Any gas monitor that fails to respond properly during a functional bump test shall be sent for
troubleshooting or further maintenance as required.

Self-Testing
The personal detectors shall be self-tested each day before the use of the detector. The device will

Calibration
Calibration is a quantitative test utilizing a known traceable concentration of test gas to demonstrate
that the sensor(s) and alarms respond to the gas within manufacturer’s acceptable limits. It is the
adjustment of the baseline and sensitivity of the sensor/electronics circuit, that is, it is based on an

over-exposure to the targeted gas, or after exposure to extreme environmental conditions, after a
severe physical jolt, or if a Function Test produces a less than satisfactory result. It is important to
observe the response times of the sensors, the stability of the sensor signal, and the recovery
times of the sensors during this period.

be different. The frequency of calibration shall be governed as per the manufacturer’s
recommendation as indicated in the instruction manual for the routine test and re-calibration
interval.

7.4.11. PYROPHORIC IRON SULFIDE
It can be formed on the internal surfaces of carbon steel equipment containing H S. It is the chemical
2
reaction of iron oxide present on the internal surfaces which reacts with H S to form pyrophoric iron sulfide
2
which, on exposure to oxygen can autoignite leading to fire/explosion. The following controls shall be
adopted for safe handling of pyrophoric iron sulfide:
Opening up to Atmosphere
It is important to take utmost care when opening up equipment to the atmosphere since oxygen
will react with the pyrophoric debris in an environment which is yet to be free if hydrocarbons
thereby leading to cause fire/explosion. The removal of hydrocarbons by e.g. nitrogen purging with
a mixture of 5% oxygen in nitrogen which allows controlled oxidation.
Transportation, Storage and Disposal
The pyrophoric shall remain wet, preferably submerged in water throughout the period of storage
and transportation to the designated safe area for its disposal.
Transportation shall be in watertight metal container. The container used for transporting the
pyrophoric debris shall be clearly indicated and shall never be transported together with flammable
materials.
The safe disposal of pyrophoric material shall be in line will ADNOC Waste Management Standard
[Ref. 64].

7.9.6. ESCAPE & EVACUATION
Self-evacuation is one of the most effective protective measures in response to H S release. However,
2
evacuation itself can be dangerous if not done properly:
(i) Smell is not a reliable basis for evacuation. Escape & evacuation shall commence when
there is a H S alarm
2
(ii) All alarms shall be treated as real until proven false, and the appropriate response shall be
initiated (alarm sirens and beacons based on fixed alarms will generally only be activated
on confirmed gas release);
(iii) All alarms shall be investigated by competent person and conclusions reported. If the release
is a nuisance release the alarm may be reset, but the person investigating shall test the area
with a hand held indicating instrument to attempt to determine the source;
(iv) Failed personal detectors shall be replaced promptly;
(v) It is a great help in securing safe escape, if the alarms are “staged”, so that a local alarm
leads to local evacuation, a plant unit alarm leads to evacuation of a specific plant unit etc.
This allows most large scale evacuations to be avoided. Dispersion patterns shall be taken
into account while designing staged alarm to ensure safe evacuation. The assembly points
shall be either outside the H2S impacted areas or within the safe area such as TR. A single
instrument shall never be the basis for a full site or plant evacuation unless it can be
demonstrated to be highly reliable in the field;
(vi) Running away from a gas plume is appropriate provided that you can see the plume taking
in consideration that gas is colourless and plume will be invisible in many cases [Ref. 28].

7.9.7. MINUTE VENTILATION RATE
Air supply and replacement air cylinders will be needed for search and rescue long duration emergencies.
The amount of air required needs to be considered during emergency planning.
The amount of air breathed by a person is measured as the minute ventilation rate. The standard minute
ventilation rate for an adult is most commonly quoted as 5 to 8 l/minute when resting, but this value can
rise up to 19 when moving and up to 30 when exercising (bicycling).
Values of >69, >95, and >100 have been reported for fire fighters (presumably fit) on a tread mill in full
fire gear, [Ref. 42, 43 and 44]. The minute ventilation rate (MVR) used for determining the capacity of
emergency escape mask and self-contained BA and for the design of air-line breathing air supplies and
for TR is by standard 50 l/ minute [Ref. 48].
Companies shall consider extreme cases when determining the volume needs and cylinder refilling and
reserve capacity needs, particularly for rescue.
7.10. COMMON/GENERAL PRECAUTIONS OF REDUCING RISK
There are a few simple rules which can be taught, and which significantly reduce risk, particularly for
persons working in a Red, Amber or Yellow zone, or going to an area to investigate a leak which shall be
adhered to:
(i) Before going to the H S hazard zone, check the personal H S detector (press the test button
2 2
- as mentioned in Section 7.4.6), and check the EEBA or BA set;
(ii) Before entering a Red Zone, or approaching a possible leak, pause for 30 seconds and
check the personal detector;
(iii) Before climbing a ladder or ascending stairs to a working platform, check the personal
detector;
(iv) When going to the roof of a sour oil or sour water tank, check the wind direction. Also, when
reaching the top of the ladder or stair, pause for 30 seconds and check the personal detector;
(v) If there is a smell of H S, or a gas release, check the personal detector immediately. The
2
release may be worse, or the wind may change;
(vi) When entering an Yellow Zone, it is essential to know where EEBA are located and to be
familiar with the routes from the work location to the nearest EEBA locations; and
(vii) The location of EEBAs and routes thereto shall be included in any JSA for work in an Yellow
Zone.

APPENDIX 2 EXPOSURE CALCULATIONS
All calculations of impact of accidental releases on people shall use the toxic load (dose) defined as
TL = ∫Cn.dt
Where:
TL = toxic load
C = concentration of hydrogen sulfide in air
t = time
n = toxicological exponent
The integral is over the period of exposure.
The probability of fatality as a function of toxic load in the form of a probit defined by:
Probit = A + B ln TL
Where A and B are specific parameters derived from toxicological experiment.
Values for n, A and B shall be taken as per UK HSE Probit.
A probit is a normally distributed function with mean of 5.0 and standard deviation of 1.0, so a probit of 5.0
corresponds to a 50% chance of fatality and a probit of 2.67 corresponds to a 1% chance of fatality. It is related
to, but not quite the same as the probit function used in statistics. The following table can be used to translate
between probits and probability of fatality:
