1. INTRODUCTION
This Standard provides information to ADNOC Group and Contractors to enable informed decisions to
be made regarding assessment of the nature and extent of potentially hazardous agents in the indoor
working environment.
The Standard outlines how Indoor Air Quality (IAQ) and indoor environments can be assessed; what
information shall be collected; the interpretation of field data, and the planning of control measures to
ensure that people are not exposed to unnecessary risks to health.
2. PURPOSE
This Standard provides best practice to ADNOC Group and their Contractors to enable them to conduct
reliable qualitative and quantitative assessments of physical, chemical and biological agents that may be
present in the indoor environment. In this Standard, the indoor environments encompass offices, control
rooms, living accommodation, rest areas and other indoor facilities associated with an organisation.
The qualities of indoor air and environment have become very important occupational health and safety
issues in modern working and living. Rapid and extensive increases in office space, meeting, leisure and
accommodation areas coupled with demand for energy efficiency and conservation have led to the
construction of air-tight buildings and the proliferation of open-plan indoor architecture.
Often, these buildings are equipped with large air handling units that supply ‘conditioned, or ‘comfort
controlled air’ by limiting the proportion of fresh (i.e. outdoor) air intake but ensure that the existing indoor
air is filtered and recirculated within the building. The impact of such configurations is that there is a
potential for build-up of airborne contaminants within the building. Without adequate exhaust or removal
of the contaminants, this can lead to complaints and increases cases of ill health.
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates &
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors. It covers
workplaces including but not limited to offices, Operator Shelter, Control Rooms, Accommodation building
(including camps), Mess Halls, Gymnasiums, Printing Centre, and excludes Laboratories, Maintenance
Workshops, Welding Workshops, Fabrication Workshops, Painting Booths that are covered under
Chemical Hazards and Physical Hazards Standards.
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood,
implemented and thoroughly monitored.
This Standard, is not intended to cover assessment of external (i.e. environmental) air quality caused by
traffic smog, sandstorms, or industrial pollution.

7. REQUIREMENTS
A broad spectrum of symptoms and complaints may be experienced by occupants of buildings that have
poor indoor air quality or are poorly designed and laid out. Expectedly, the symptoms tend to increase in
severity with time spent in the building and improve over time or else disappear when away from the
building [Ref. 3].
In such involving it is common for occupants of a building, office or an accommodation block to report
one or more of the following symptoms [Ref. 4]:
(i) Dryness and irritation of the eyes, nose, throat, and skin;
(ii) Headache, and sinus congestion;
(iii) Dizziness, fatigue and shortness of breath;
(iv) Hypersensitivity and allergies; and
(v) Coughing and sneezing; and nausea.
However, it is important to recognise that many of the symptoms can also be caused by other health
conditions such as common colds, influenza, poor posture, disturbed sleep patterns and stress. Thus,
identification and resolution of symptoms associated with indoor air and indoor environments tend to be
lingering, difficult and may often be inconclusive.

A term used when occupants of any building experience adverse health effects that are apparently linked
to the time they spend in the building, yet no specific illnesses or cause can be identified. The term does
not include discomfort from physical conditions in the workplace such as excessive noise, heat, cold, or
even work-related stress.
Sick Building Syndrome (SBS) is not a recognised “diagnosable” illness or medical condition. It is purely
a term which conveniently describes a phenomenon which cannot be diagnosed precisely and hence
must not be confused with specific occupational illnesses that are directly associated with workplaces,
such as humidifier fevers and Legionnaires disease. This term is used to refer to less frequent, but often
more serious cases of occupants becoming ill after being in a specific building at a certain time.

7.2. RECOGNITION OF AGENTS IN INDOOR ENVIRONMENT & HEALTH EFFECTS
Symptoms associated with poor indoor conditions are similar to those from any other occupational illness.
However, not all people at work are equally at risk and not all will exhibit the same symptoms or to the
same extent. In addition, some persons may be more sensitive or susceptible than others to the causative
factors and levels of agents in the indoor environment.
People who most commonly report symptoms tend to be those with little or no control over the workplace
conditions and tend to be employed in routine activities such as general clerical work or work involving
several hours using computer screens. In some cases, people may be exposed to more causative factors
than others or may experience symptoms earlier than other people in the same area. However, it is certain
that as indoor conditions deteriorate and/or the extent of exposure increases, more people tend to be
affected and the symptoms are usually more serious [Ref. 4].
Research has shown that sensitivity to poor indoor conditions may increase as time passes, hence it may
be possible that recent employees might not initially be sensitive to problems, but they become sensitised
as exposure to the causative factors progresses over time. As indicated in Section 7.1.1, there are several
potential stressors on indoor environments. The Table 7.2.1 presented below, provides examples of some

7.3.1. INDOOR AIR QUALITY INVESTIGATION STAGES
(a) Stage 1 - Preliminary Assessment
(i) Step 1: Investigating the Complaint (RESPONSIBILITY: OH SME)
A sudden increase in complaints after long occupation of a building may well point to an
obvious explanation; hence it is important to check whether there are any known conditions
or situations that may be relevant. Obvious examples are a local epidemic of flu or colds, or
else a breakdown in the air handling systems. Also, it is important to establish what are the
magnitudes and distribution of the complaints, and whether complainants are receiving
medical support and treatment.
Once these have been identified, prompt remedial action shall be taken and all affected
personnel should be kept informed of any developments and a review date set to confirm
that the remedial actions have been successful.
(ii) Step 2: Checking Reported Symptoms & Interview Staff (RESPONSIBILITY: OH SME)
If no obvious conclusions relating specifically to the indoor environment are reached, the
reported symptoms shall be investigated in more depth. Symptoms relating to indoor
environments often do not result in absence from work, hence reviewing staff absence or
sickness records might reveal very little.
Consequently, it is more effective to arrange confidential and discrete, face-to-face
interviews with complainants and asking the following questions:
1. What are the symptoms?
2. How frequently, and at what time of day do the symptoms appear?
3. How long have the symptoms been noticed?
4. Do symptoms subside after leaving the building?
(iii) Step 3: Correlating Symptoms with Causative Factors (RESPONSIBILITY: OH SME)
Against the background of the interview notes, it may be necessary to visually inspect the
workplace environment and ask the staff directly what they consider to be the causes of the
problem. Some caution will need to be exercised when interpreting the results of this step,
since opening the subject may invite personal complaints rather than point at the actual
stressors affecting the indoor environment.
(iv) Step 4: Take action through Introduction of Preliminary Controls (RESPONSIBILITY: OH
SME)
Depending on findings of the initial assessments, it may be advisable to introduce a few
preliminary and practical control measures that specifically target the main causative factors
[or stressors]. A few typical examples of these scenarios are presented below.

(b) Stage 2 - Walkthrough Assessment
(i) Step 1: Investigating Procedures & Working Practices (RESPONSIBILITY: OH SME &
Facilities Management)
The step is aimed at carrying out more detailed reviews of the building services,
maintenance routines, and cleaning procedures. As previously stated [Ref. 4] inadequate
ventilation has been shown to be the main stressor in 52% of indoor environments
investigated. Thus, as a minimum, an initial assessment of the HVAC system shall be
conducted whenever building-related complaints are received. In virtually all cases, this step
is considered to be well within the capabilities of the in-house HSE personnel and/or
engineers responsible for management of the Buildings and Facilities.
The walkthrough assessment shall cover all areas where building-related symptoms have
been reported. Factors that shall be evaluated include the following [Ref. 5]:
1. Are there sources of indoor contaminants that could lead to employee complaints?
Examples include sources of noise, photocopiers, blueprint copiers, paints, cleaning
compounds and disinfectants, tobacco smoke, adhesives and glues, contaminants
generated by construction or renovation, air fresheners, or pesticides?
2. Are there sources of outdoor contaminants that ingress into the building and could
lead to complaints? Examples include vehicle exhaust from nearby car parks or main
roadways, hydrocarbons from nearby processing plant, cooling towers, dust, or other
contaminants from construction and refurbishing activities, gasoline vapours, pollen,
biological contaminants, and atmospheric pollutants.
3. Are the HVAC systems being operated and maintained properly? This topic shall
address the location of air intakes and exhausts, pressure differentials between rooms
that may account for influx of airborne contaminants, design for supplied outdoor air,
flow and distribution of air, position of dampers, local exhaust ventilation, air cleaning

The following equipment can be used for determining indication of mould growth:

ceiling plenums, crawl spaces, and other tight areas. It consists of a video camera on the end of a
flexible "snake." No major drilling or cutting of dry wall is required. There are various types of
equipment useful in mould remediation.

7.5. CONTROLLING THE INDOOR ENVIRONMENT
Once indoor air contaminants have been assessed quantitatively, various controls can be employed to
mitigate employee exposure. Control measures can aim in attaining employee thermal comfort, or can
ensure indoor air quality contaminants do not exceed their corresponding guideline values. Subsequent
sections will describe different control measures applicable to indoor air contaminants.
7.5.1. OPTIMIZING INDOOR AIR QUALITY
This element addresses the expectations and deliverables of the HVAC systems commonly present at all
onshore and offshore installations. In that respect, the HVAC system shall be capable of delivering air of
suitable quality and in sufficient quantities to:
(i) Provide fresh air which creates and maintains a healthy and comfortable environment;
(ii) Dilute and/or remove airborne impurities and pollutants;
(iii) Create and maintain comfortable temperature and humidity levels; and
(iv) Prevent stagnation and undesirable draughts.
Indoor environments where natural ventilation is not an option, an HVAC system shall be considered
satisfactory if it conforms to the following parameters [Ref. 7]:

7.5.2. TEMPERATURE
As per Table 7.4.1, the recommended temperature range is between 20-24C (summer/winter), but actual
need will vary according to the occupants’ needs and demands.
When evaluating the effectiveness of an HVAC system to maintain comfort standards, it is also necessary

(i) The amount and period of sunlight entering to the building;
(ii) The seasonal ‘external’ temperature;
(iii) The type of window coverings [e.g. curtains, blinds, reflecting film, or solar reflective glass];

(iv) The amount of radiant heat emitted from office machinery such as computers, printers,

7.5.3. HUMIDITY
Unreasonable humidity levels in combination with other factors can exacerbate problems. The most
prominent example is that high humidity encourages growth of harmful bacteria and fungi while low
humidity contributes to a dry/dusty atmosphere and to dry eyes, nose, throat and skin.
As per Table 7.4.1, it is generally considered in the interests of work efficiency that indoor humidity levels
be maintained in the range of 30% - 60%. Offices situated in warm areas, the relative humidity shall be
at the lower end of this range. Humidifier controls, cleanliness and operation shall also be frequently
checked.
7.5.4. CHEMICAL CONTAMINANTS
Equipment, new furnishings [e.g. carpets and bedding]; decorations [e.g. paints, varnishes, and
adhesives] and several cleaning products [e.g. aerosol, polishes, and air fresheners] release several
odours and chemical agents that impact on the indoor environments.
Most of the effects associated with these substances shall be resolved during renovation, commissioning
or early occupation stages but it should be recognised that symptoms and complaints can be prolonged
for several weeks or months.

APPENDIX 1
APPENDIX 1 GUIDELINES FOR PREVENTION AND CONTROL OF MOLDS IN INDOOR ENVIRONMENT
This appendix provides guidelines for the prevention and remediation/clean-up of mould and moisture problems
in indoor workplaces; these guidelines include measures designed to protect the health of building occupants.
Brief definition for mould is included in the definition part above.
Health Effects due to Moulds:
The following defined adverse health effects are fully associated with moulds in the buildings:
(i) Allergic Reaction:
Moulds produce substances that can cause allergic reactions known as allergens. Other mould products
include irritants, and in some cases, potentially toxic substances (mycotoxins). Inhaling or touching mould
or mould spores may cause allergic reactions in sensitive individuals. Typical symptoms include sneezing;
irritation of the nose, mouth, or throat; nasal stuffiness and runny nose; and red, itchy or watery eyes.
Inhaling or touching mould or mould spores can cause a person who was not previously allergic to mould
to become allergic to mould. For people with known allergies, moulds can trigger asthma symptoms such
as shortness of breath, wheezing, or cough. Irritation can also occur in non-allergenic (non-sensitized)
people.
(ii) Airway Hypersensitivity
Airway Hypersensitivity is a type of lung inflammation that occurs in individuals who develop
hypersensitivity (similar to an allergy) to inhaled mould particles. Dozens of different fungi, bacteria, animal
proteins, plants, and chemicals are known causes of Airway Hypersensitivity.
Symptoms of Airway Hypersensitivity can vary. Some individuals experience shortness of breath and flu-
like symptoms, including cough, muscle aches, chills, fever, sweating, and fatigue (acute disease). These
symptoms start within hours of exposure and last for 1 to 3 days if there is no further exposure. Other
individuals develop gradual and progressive shortness of breath and cough, often accompanied by weight
loss. Airway Hypersensitivity in an individual may only become apparent over long absence from mould
damaged buildings if symptoms resolve and then recur on return to work inside. With continued exposure,
the persistent lung inflammation can lead to scarring and permanent damage. The slow progression of
symptoms and the persistence of symptoms away from work may result in delayed recognition of Airway
Hypersensitivity both occupants and physicians.
The main treatment for Airway Hypersensitivity is removal from exposure to the causative agent or
environment. This may be accomplished by relocation. Acute disease may resolve completely with
exposure termination; corticosteroid (a type of steroid) medications may shorten disease duration. With
long-term exposure, the disease may not improve or may continue to worsen and progress to permanent
lung scarring even after exposure ends.
Prevention of Mould in Indoor Environment
(i) Moisture Control
The key is to prevent the mould is moisture control and the following are the important tips for the controlling

1. HVAC systems should be checked regularly, particularly for damp filters and overall cleanliness.

2. A preventive maintenance plan should be put into place for each major component of the building’s

6. HVAC system filters shall be seated properly and replaced on a routine schedule.
Mould Remediation
The purpose of mould remediation is to identify and fix the water or moisture problem, remove or clean all mould-
damaged materials, prevent the spread of contamination to other areas, and protect the building occupants from
exposures to mould. During any remediation, the health and safety of remediation workers must also be a priority.
In every case of microbial contamination, including mould contamination, the underlying cause of the
contamination must be rectified or the growth will recur.
(i) Remediation Plan
Remediation includes both the identification and correction of the conditions that permit mould growth, as
well as the steps to safely and effectively remove mould-damaged materials. The size of the mould and/or
moisture problem and the type of damaged materials should be assessed before planning the remediation
work. The remediation plan should include:
1. Steps to permanently fix the water or moisture problem;
2. The use of appropriate Personal Protective Equipment (PPE);
3. Steps to carefully contain and remove mouldy building materials to avoid spreading the mould; and

when building occupants are less likely to be affected.

(ii) Remediation/Clean-up Methods
Remediation’s main purpose is to fix the moisture problem and to dispose mouldy materials in order to
prevent human exposure and further damage to building materials and furniture. Non-porous materials,
such as metals, glass, and hard plastics can almost always be cleaned. Semi-porous materials, such as
wood and concrete can be cleaned if they were well-built. Porous materials, such as ceiling tiles, insulation,
and wallboards (stained with more than one spot of mould growth) shall be removed and discarded
because moulds can infiltrate porous substances and grow on or fill in empty spaces or crevices. This
mould can be difficult or impossible to remove completely.
Because the chemicals and proteins that can cause a reaction in humans, are present even in dead mould,
the mould must be removed and not just killing it with biocide.
A variety of clean-up methods are available for remediating damage to building materials and furnishings
caused by moisture control problems and mould growth. The specific method or group of methods used
will depend on the type of material affected. Some methods that may be used include the following:
1. Wet Vacuum
Wet vacuums are vacuum cleaners designed to collect water. They can be used to remove water
from floors, carpets, and hard surfaces where water has accumulated. They shall not be used to
vacuum porous materials, such as gypsum board. Wet vacuums should be used only on wet
materials, as spores may be exhausted into the indoor environment if insufficient liquid is present.
The tanks, hoses, and attachments of these vacuums should be thoroughly cleaned and dried after
use since mould and mould spores may adhere to equipment surfaces.
2. Damp Wipe
Wiping or scrubbing with water and detergent can generally remove mould from non-porous
surfaces. It is important to dry these surfaces quickly and thoroughly to prevent further mould

APPENDIX 4  MEASUREMENT OF RADON [REF. 14]
A summary of radon sampling methods is summarized in Table 4.1.  Initial radon tests are often made with
passive samplers containing activated carbon.  Radon measurements are made in frequently occupied rooms
with substantial ground contact.  The duration of short term measurements can range from 2 to 90 days.  Prior
to placement of the radon measurement devices, general observations are performed to verify test conditions,
identify device placement conditions, and determine structural and mechanical building components.

Radon test devices are placed in such a way to limit unintentional interference from building occupants.  Ideally,
the measurement devices should be placed at least 3 feet from doors, windows to the outside, at least one foot
from exterior walls, at least 4 feet from heat sources, out of the direct flow of ventilation ducts and sunlight, and
suspended in the general breathing zone.  After sampling, passive devices are sent to a laboratory for analysis.
The laboratory calculates the radon concentration after measuring the gamma activity by the radon decay
products produced from the random decay of the collected radon.

Indoor radon concentration varies with the construction of buildings and ventilation.  These concentrations not
only vary substantially with the season, but also from day-to-day and even from hour-to-hour.  Because of these
fluctuations, estimating the annual average concentration of radon in indoor air requires reliable measurements
of mean radon concentrations for at least 3 months and preferably longer.  Short term measurements provide
only a crude indication of the actual radon concentration.  Quality assurance for radon measurements is highly
recommended in order to ensure the quality of measurements.

The results of the radon measurements are interpreted to determine the need for additional testing and assess
the quality and confidence of the measurement data.  Typically, follow up measurements will be recommended
in every room with results greater than 4.0 pCi/L.  Mitigation is not usually recommended based solely on the
initial measurement results, and follow up testing using long-term measurements is recommended.
