1. INTRODUCTION
It is the ADNOC Group HSE policy to ensure that its employees and contractors are protected from
occupational risks that they could be exposed to. ADNOC is achieving this through the implementation
and enforcement of HSE Governance and Administrative Framework comprising of full suite of Standards,
including this Physical Hazards Standard.
The Physical Hazards Standard involves hazard identification, risk assessment and risk control practices
of physical hazards present in ADNOC workplaces. It is also required that risk management process is
based on solid and tested methodologies.
2. PURPOSE
Activities that are carried out by the ADNOC Group and several of their contractors involve complex
interfaces and reactions between processes, plant & machinery, and people. In many cases, such
interactions can directly or indirectly present scenarios or conditions that pose risks to the health and
wellbeing of personnel at work.
This Standard provides a framework and detailed guidance on:
(i) Various physical agents present in the work environment;
(ii) Health effects of physical hazards when a worker is exposed;
(iii) Evaluation of health risks associated with physical agents present in the working
environment;
(iv) Controlling the risks to As Low as Reasonably Practicable (ALARP); and
(v) Meeting Legislative and Regulatory requirements (e.g., FANR).
3. SCOPE
This document stipulates the mandatory requirements applicable to ADNOC Group (Directorates &
Functions in ADNOC Head Quarter, Group Companies and Affiliates) and its Contractors.
This Standard outlines the requirements for management of the following Physical Hazards:
(i) Noise;
(ii) Vibration;
(iii) Heat Stress;
(iv) Radiation (Ionization and Non-Ionization);
(v) Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material (NORM);
(vi) Hyperbaric Pressure.
ADNOC Group and Contractors shall ensure that all expectations listed herein are fully understood,
implemented and thoroughly monitored.

7.2. NOISE
7.2.1. RECOGNITION OF NOISE HAZARD
(a) Non-Industrial Noise
“Sound” is a natural phenomenon which reflects the movement or pressure changes that occur in
the surrounding environment. Humans experience man-made sounds that are produced by traffic
[e.g. that emitted by air, sea, and road transportation]; construction activities; machinery; air
conditioning plants; fans; or from leisure activities [e.g. music, radio, TV, or public address systems].
However, the sound that is universally experienced by everyone from an early age is speech, which
is used for daily communication. A healthy human ear can distinguish sounds in the frequency
bandwidth of 20 Hz–20 kHz.
“Noise” on the other hand, is the combination of unwanted, interfering and often irritating sounds,
regardless of their source.
(b) Workplace Noise
Employees’ exposure to excessive noise is a recognized potential health hazard. Such exposure
may occur during outdoor activities such as working with air compressors, pumps, turbine, boilers,
diesel/petrol engines, diesel generator, air coolers, fin fan coolers, heat exchangers, vent and flares,
or from indoor activities such as grit/sand blasting or maintenance workshops.
Before measuring the sound levels in workplace the following shall be considered:
(i) Observation of specific working practices (e.g. Workers not wearing assigned HPE);
(ii) Relevant information pertaining to equipment noise as per manufacturers’ specifications ;
(iii) Worker exposed to ototoxic substances or vibration;
(iv) Identification on high risk SEGs prone to excessive noise;
(v) The noise risk assessment shall consider the following factors;
(vi) Frequency and duration of exposure including any exposure to peak sound level;

7.2.2. HUMAN EAR & HEALTH EFFECTS OF NOISE
The ear is a complex organ which is sensitive to very soft sounds [e.g. whispers] but can also receive
loud sounds [e.g. bangs, cracks, and shouting]. However, as the sound pressure becomes louder, the
ear experiences pain and in high/medium cases the eardrum may burst.
Prolonged exposure to loud noise, whether it happens socially or through work-related (i.e. occupational)
activities can lead to Noise-Induced Hearing Loss (NIHL). This condition is the result of sound pressure
impacting on the microscopic hair cells known as Cilia (receptor) located in the middle and inner regions
of the ear, causing permanent damage and death to these cells leading to disability to transmit sound
signal to the brain.
NIHL starts with loss of hearing at 4000 Hz (audiometric notch) then spreads across the different
frequency bandwidths. The loss is manifested by the person not understanding certain letters during
speech or listening to TV, radio or other forms of communication. For long-term exposures to high levels
of Noise, the full range of audible frequencies is lost, thus leading to total deafness. The exposed person
may also develop tinnitus (i.e. ringing, whistling, buzzing or humming in the ear) leading to disturbed
sleep and general social life.
Generally, hearing deteriorates with age and as a result of repeated illness [e.g. ear infections, or wax
build-up]. However, NIHL afflicts people regardless of age. The following are the different types of hearing
losses, as well as threshold shifts experienced by individuals:
(a) Conductive Hearing Loss:
It may be temporary or permanent when an individual experiences a ringing sound or muted
hearing in one or both ears. Though these effects typically only last for a short period of time, it can
damage hearing in the long run [Ref. 16].
(b) Sensorineural Hearing Loss:
It is a permanent hearing loss that occurs when there is damage to either the tiny hair-like cells
(cilia) of the inner ear or the auditory nerve itself, which prevents or weakens the transfer of nerve
signals to the brain.
(c) Socioacusis:
Hearing loss incurred from non-occupational noise sources e.g. recreational that develops over
time after repeated exposure.
(d) Presbycusis:
It is the loss of hearing that gradually occurs in most individuals as they grow older. Hearing loss
is a common disorder associated with aging. The loss associated with presbycusis is usually
greater for high-pitched sounds.
(e) Temporary Threshold Shift (TTS):
Threshold shifts that recover to baseline levels in the hours, days or weeks following exposure are
termed TTS.

7.2.3. THE NOISE/SOUND SCALE
Sound is generated from a broad spectrum of pressure changes that happen near and at the ear. As a
result, sound pressure has to be measured on a base 10 logarithmic scale, which is known as the Decibel
(dB). The pressure measurement is also suffixed by one of the letters A or C (e.g. dBA or dBC) to indicate
that an electronic frequency filter [i.e. an electronic weighting] has been used during the measurement.
The weightings, enable the Sound Level Meter (SLM) to respond to the same frequencies as those
recognized by the human ear. The three weightings correspond to the following settings:
(i) A-weighting, is noise that the human ear can recognise.
(ii) C-weighting, is impact [i.e. pulse or peak].
(iii) Z-weighting, is the flat frequency response of 8Hz to 20 kHz (± 1.5dB). It is the actual noise
that is made with no weighting at all for the human ear (Z for zero). Often used in octave
band analysis and for determining environmental noise such as peak level in mining and
blasting due to extreme low frequency of the shock wave.
7.2.4. CHARACTERISTICS OF SOUND
The main features of sound are outlined below:
(a) Intensity
Sound intensity is defined as the sound power per unit area. The usual context is the measurement
of sound intensity in the air at a listener’s location.
(b) Frequency
Human hearing is sensitive to sound that occurs in the frequency band of 20 Hz to 20 kHz, but
workplace sounds are composed of a broad spectrum of frequencies, some of which occur in the
hearing range, while other do not [e.g. ultrasound or very low frequency sound]. For that reason,
when conducting risk assessments of noise at work, the audible frequency range is sometimes
divided into smaller bandwidths in order for the measurements to be used for profiling noise
[especially from plant and machinery] or for evaluating noise control and the efficacy of hearing
protection equipment. The most common bandwidths are the Octave [1/1] and Third [⅓] Octave.
The frequencies usually included in the Octave are 63, 125, 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000 & 8000
Hz. On the other hand, the ⅓ Octave is divided into 36 frequencies between 6.3 Hz and 20 kHz.
(c) Time
There are three time-related noise sources commonly encountered in the workplace. These are
described below.
(i) Sources with noise levels that fluctuate by more than ±3 dB are generally defined as variable
or intermittent. These sources are characterized by long periods of silence which is
followed by a loud noise. A typical example is the intermittent use of angle grinders or metal

(ii) Continuous or steady state sources produce uninterrupted sound, varying by less than ±

osmosis plants.
(iii) Impulse or impact is characterized by a sharp rise and rapid decay in sound levels and is

solid objects or by a rapid pressure release. Typical examples are hammering on metal
surfaces, dropped heavy objects, doors slamming shut, and opening of pressure release

7.3. VIBRATION
Vibration is the result of mechanical oscillation of an object about an equilibrium point. The oscillations
may be regular [e.g. a pendulum movement] or random [e.g. the movement of a machine, or tyre on a
gravel road]. Any vibration has three measurable quantities that determine its vibrational characteristics.
These are described below and demonstrated graphically in Figure 7.3.1.
Amplitude
This is the movement of an object to a certain maximum distance on either side of its stationary
[central] position. Amplitude is the distance from the stationary position to the extreme position on
either side and is measured in metres (m). The intensity of vibration depends on amplitude.
Frequency
A vibrating object moves back and forth from its normal stationary position. A complete cycle of
vibration occurs when the object moves from one extreme position to the other extreme and back
again. The number of cycles that a vibrating object completes in one second is called frequency.
The unit of frequency is hertz (Hz). One hertz equals one cycle per second.
Acceleration (Vibration Intensity)
The speed of a vibrating object varies from zero to a maximum during each cycle of vibration. It
moves fastest as it passes through its natural stationary position to an extreme position. The
vibrating object slows down as it approaches the extreme, where it stops and then moves in the
opposite direction through the stationary position toward the other extreme. Speed of vibration
(acceleration) is expressed in metres per second (m/s) and acceleration is expressed in meters
per second squared (m/s2).

7.3.1. HEALTH EFFECTS OF VIBRATION
Vibration impacts the body part that is in contact with the vibrating equipment. When a worker operates
a hand-held power tool, such as a chain saw, a grinder or jackhammer, vibration affects the hands and
arms. This type of exposure is called hand-arm vibration. On the other hand, when a worker sits or
stands on a vibrating floor or the seat of moving vehicle or crane, the vibration exposure affects almost
the entire body and is called whole body vibration exposure.
The risk of vibration-induced injury depends on the average daily exposure. Hand-arm vibration causes
damage to hands and fingers and especially to its blood vessels and nerves. The resulting condition is
known as white finger disease, Raynaud's phenomenon or Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome (HAVS).

One of the symptoms is that affected fingers may turn white especially when exposed to cold, a loss of
grip force and sensitivity to touch.
Vibration-induced white finger (VWF) is the most common occupational health condition among operators
of hand-held vibrating power tools. The symptoms of VWF are aggravated when the hands are exposed
to cold temperatures.
Vibration can also cause changes in tendons, muscles, bones and joints, and can affect the nervous
system. Collectively, these effects are known as Hand-Arm Vibration Syndrome (HAVS). HAVS develop
gradually and increases in severity over time; hence it may take a few months to several years for the
symptoms to become clinically noticeable.
Nonetheless, workers affected by HAVS commonly report the following symptoms:
(i) Attacks of whitening (blanching) of one or more fingers when exposed to cold;
(ii) Tingling and loss of sensation in the fingers;
(iii) Loss of light touch;
(iv) Pain and cold sensations between periodic white finger attacks;
(v) Loss of grip strength; and
(vi) Bone cyst in fingers and wrists [Ref. 17];
(vii) Health Effects such as: Lower back pain (damage to vertebrae and discs, ligaments
loosened from shaking), Motion sickness, Bone damage, varicose veins/heart conditions
(variation in blood pressure from vibration).
Whole body vibration can cause fatigue, insomnia, stomach problems, headache and "shakiness" shortly
after or during exposure. After daily exposure over a number of years, whole body vibration can affect
the entire body and result in a number of health disorders. Sea, air or land vehicles cause motion sickness
when the vibration exposure occurs in the 0.1 to 0.6 Hz frequency range.
The combined effects of body posture, postural fatigue, dietary habits and whole body vibration are the
possible causes for these disorders.

(a) Quantitative Assessment
The qualitative risk assessment may conclude the need for detailed quantitative surveys of
vibration exposures in the workplace. In such circumstances, exposure to HAV shall be evaluated
using the method defined in European Standard EN ISO 5349-1:2001 on Mechanical Vibration. In
summary, vibration is measured with a triaxial sensor (transducer) which can be attached to a tool;
or else it is placed on the seat of the vehicle and is known as a seat accelerometer.
A triaxial sensor is used because damage caused by vibration transmitted through the hand or the
body, is related to the averaged magnitude of acceleration in the three axes x, y, and z. For hand
arm vibration, the acceleration levels in three orthogonal axes [the x axis, y axis and z axis at right
angles to each other] are squared and summed to give the Vibration Total Value, ahv. The formula
for ahv is:
ahv = √ (ahwx2+ahwy2+ahwz2); where hw = hand-arm weighted
The measurements of WBV are presented as separate averages for each of the x-, y- and z-axes.
The values for the x- and y-axes (Wd) are then multiplied by a factor (k) of 1.4, while the values for
the z-axis (Wk) are multiplied by a factor (k) of 1.0 (i.e. they stay the same).
The average whole-body vibration level is then taken as the highest of the values in the three axes

7.4. HEAT STRESS
Working in hot environments, whether these are man-made (e.g. working near hot surfaces) or natural
(i.e. summer climate, sunshine, hot winds, or dry desert conditions) puts people under extreme thermal
stresses that can have serious consequences on their health and safety.
The human body is at optimum comfort when its core temperature is maintained between 36°C-37.5°C.
If the core temperature increases by more than 1°C, the body is either suffering from an infection that is
causing a fever; or else it is unable to deal with the heat in the surrounding environment.
A normal body exchanges heat in several ways as shown below in Figure 7.4.1. If all the elements balance
out, the body does not store heat; but if they do not the body either loses (i.e. feels cold) or gains heat
(i.e. feels hot). Either situation can potentially have serious health effects on the human body.
Tiredness, dehydration, overweight, physical fitness, and the calorific content of diet can all have an effect
on the body’s ability to exchange heat. However, the physical demands of activities being carried out,
whether these are work-related or leisure, rest periods, and general acclimatisation to the conditions also
play important roles in the body’s ability to exchange heat.

Acclimatization
This is the body’s mechanism of coping with a new thermal environment. Complete heat
acclimatization generally takes about 7 days, but some people may require a slightly longer period
[Ref. 28]. Loss of acclimatization occurs gradually when a person is moved permanently away from
a hot environment, but a decrease in heat tolerance occurs even after a long weekend. As a result
of reduced heat tolerance, it is not advisable for anyone to work under very hot conditions on the
first day back to a working week.
New employees shall acclimatize before assuming a full workload. For experienced employees, it
is advisable to assign about half of the normal workload on the first day of work and gradually
increasing the work period on subsequent days (Refer to Table 7.4.3). Well-trained, physically fit
workers can tolerate heat better than people in poor physical conditions. However, fitness and
training do not substitute the acclimatization regime.
Some medications can interfere with acclimatization. For example, blood pressure tablets, diuretics,
antispasmodics, sedatives, and tranquilizers decrease the body's ability to cope with heat. Workers
shall always seek advice from the medical practitioner on the suitability of a medication for them if
they work in hot environments.

7.5. COLD STRESS [REF. 55]
The most significant effect of exposure to cold climate is manual dexterity, especially with prolonged
contact with surfaces having temperatures below 15°C. Cold exposure also affects visual acuity as a
result of ocular discomfort, including tearing and sensations of dryness accompanies by frequent blinking.
The general psychological responses to prolonged work in the cold can be significant. Elements of
isolation and confinement due to cold work locations, as well as discomfort from the cold, have been
noted to result in behavioural responses. These include changes in arousal, perception, mood,
personality and apathy.
One factor that can control exposure from cold stress is cold acclimatization. This lowers the deep body
temperature and an increase blood flow through the exposed extremities. These changes help conserve
heat by reducing the heat loss gradient. Other controls from exposure to cold stress can include:
(i) Provide proper clothing. Choose fabrics that inherently trap air (e.g., wool) and stop air
penetration. (e.g., windbreakers);
(ii) Allow for self-limiting of work exposure to cold conditions;
(iii) Reduce air velocity and minimize drafts (e.g., windbreakers);
(iv) Balance work so that periods of intense work are not followed by low work rates;
(v) Increase the clothing insulation;
(vi) Use windproof clothing as insulation;
(vii) Provide hand warming for fine work below 16°C;
(viii) Increase radiant heat with micro or spot heaters;
(ix) Provide for the use of buddy system;
(x) Allow for productivity reductions due to the use of personal protective equipment;
(xi) Schedule work/recovery cycles;
(xii) Use active warming systems (microenvironmental heating);
(xiii) Provide hydration with warm, sweet, non-caffeinated drinks; and
(xiv) Encourage a balanced diet.

protection as ALARA (As Low as Reasonably Achievable). The types of radiation are:
(i) Non-Ionizing Radiation;
(ii) Ionizing Radiation;
(iii) Naturally Occurring Radioactive Material (NORM).

7.6.1. RADIATION MANAGEMENT
Radiation management activities shall be performed in a standardized and coordinated manner and shall
be prioritized based on the level of risk to a person’s health. Management of radiation shall be achieved
through the following, at a minimum:
(i) Compliance with International and Federal Legislation, including compliance with this
Standard and FANR requirements;
(i) Identification and preparation of inventory of radioactive sources at each facility i.e.
Radioactive Sources/Equipment Register as provided in Appendix 7;
(ii) Characterization of radiation exposures that are likely to contribute to exceedances of
occupational exposure limits as per this Standard;
(iii) Establishment of baseline levels of radiation at each facility including NORM;
(iv) If presence of NORM has been established, NORM management guidelines for handling,
storage, transportation, disposal including NORM-contaminated equipment or waste shall
be established;
(v) Assessment, demarcation and monitoring of site-specific Controlled Areas and Supervised
Areas; and
(vi) Establishing and implementing radiation control strategies to reduce exposure to radiation
sources in the work environment.
(vii) Time, Distance, and Shielding - Minimizing exposure time, maximizing distance from the
radiation source, shielding employee from the radiation source.

Non-Ionizing radiation (NIR) is a generic term used to describe part of the electromagnetic spectrum

  Effects on Human Health
Optical radiation is absorbed in the outer layer of the body and its biological effects are mostly
confined to the skin and eyes, although systemic effects may also occur. Different wavelengths
cause different biological effects depending on which part of the skin or eye absorbs the radiation
and the type of interaction involved. For example:

(i)  Photochemical effects dominate in the ultraviolet region;
(ii)  Thermal effects dominate in the infrared region; and
(iii)  Laser radiation can produce additional effects characterized by very rapid absorption of
energy by tissue and is a particular hazard for the eyes where the lens can focus the beam.
Biological effects of exposure to optical radiation can broadly be divided into acute (i.e. rapidly
occurring) and chronic (i.e. occurring after prolonged and repeated exposures over a long period).
Generally, acute effects will only occur if exposure exceeds certain thresholds [i.e. exposure limits]
and the risk of an adverse health effect will increase as exposure levels increase above the
threshold limit. The majority of effects tabulated below (Refer Table 7.6.1) will occur in the healthy
adult working population at levels substantially above the exposure limits; however, persons who
are abnormally photosensitive may suffer adverse effects below the exposure levels.

Chronic effects often do not have a threshold below which they will not occur. Consequently, the
risk of these effects occurring cannot be reduced to zero; but the risk can be reduced by limiting
exposure to acceptable levels.

(b) Electromagnetic Fields
An electromagnetic field is a force field generated around an electric current, and is equivalent to
an electric field and a magnetic field at right angles to each other. Common sources of
electromagnetic fields include power lines, household electrical wiring, motor driven instruments,
computer screens, telecommunications and broadcasting facilities, and mobile telephones.
In contrast to electric fields, a magnetic field is only produced when a device is switched on and
current flows. Thus, the higher the current, the greater the strength of the magnetic field.
Electric fields are strongest close to a charge or charged conductor, but their strength rapidly
diminishes with distance. Metal conductors provide an effective shield; and other materials such
as bricks, walls, and trees, all provide shielding capability. When power lines are buried
underground, electric fields at the surface are hardly detectable.
The strength of a magnetic field is measured in amperes per meter (A/m); or as a flux density (in
microtesla, μT).
Low-frequency electric fields influence the human body just as they influence any other material
made up of charged particles. When electric fields act on conductive materials, they influence the
distribution of electric charges at their surface. They cause current to flow through the body to the
ground.
Low-frequency magnetic fields induce circulating currents within the human body and the strength
of these currents depends on the intensity of the outside magnetic field. If sufficiently large, these
currents could cause stimulation of nerves and muscles or affect other biological processes.
Both electric and magnetic fields induce voltages and currents in the body but even directly beneath
a high voltage transmission line, the induced currents are very small compared to thresholds for
producing shock and other electrical effects.
Heating is the main biological effect of the electromagnetic fields of radiofrequency fields. The
levels of radiofrequency fields to which people are normally exposed are very much lower than
those needed to produce significant heating.

7.6.3. IONIZING RADIATION
Ionizing radiation activities are regulated by the Federal Authority for Nuclear Regulation (FANR) in UAE
and it is the responsibility of ADNOC Group and Contractors to comply with its applicable regulations.
However, the following shall provide a framework for Ionizing Radiation Management.
Ionizing radiation occurs either as electromagnetic rays (e.g. X-rays and gamma γ-rays) or as particles
(e.g. α and β particles), but it also occurs naturally from the radioactive decay of natural substances such
as radon gas.
Industrial Radiography
Industrial radiography is a method of inspecting materials for seeing hidden flaws by using the ability of
X-rays, gamma rays and neutrons to penetrate various materials. It is a major element of non-destructive
testing.
Industrial radiography for non-destructive testing is used to inspect, among others, concrete and a wide
variety of welds, such as those in gas and water pipelines, storage tanks and structural elements. It can
identify cracks or flaws that may not be otherwise visible. These characteristics have made non-
destructive testing a key tool for quality control, safety and reliability. Industrial radiography uses two
types of radiation; namely x-rays and gamma (γ) rays.
X-Rays and γ-Rays
X-rays used in Non-Destructive Inspections (NDI) are produced by a large generator which is used in a
fixed location and similar to medical x-ray machines, radiation is present only when these machines are
turned on. Portable industrial radiography units are smaller in size as compared to X-ray machines and
use negligible amount of radioactive gamma-emitting material in a sealed source, which is typically less
than 1 cm in diameter and a few centimetres long. Portable industrial radiography units are heavy in
weight due to the shielding which is added to protect operators from ionizing radiation.
X-ray machines are also used for medical imaging during examination for orthopaedic damage, lung
disease, tumours or foreign objects in the human body, or for assessing tooth decay or damage.

7.6.5. NATURALLY OCCURRING RADIOACTIVE MATERIALS - NORM
NORM includes radioactive elements found in the environment. Long-lived radioactive elements of
interest include uranium, thorium and potassium, and any of their radioactive decay products, such as
radium and radon. These elements have always been present in the earth’s crust.
These are also known as Low Specific Activity (LSA) materials and are associated with oil and gas
exploration, development and production activities because these species are mixed in with the water,
oil and gas that are present in reservoir formations. Due to the acidity of reservoir water, elements such
as uranium, thorium and their daughter isotopes Radium-226 and Radium-228, present in the rock
minerals are leached into the water.
During processing of oil & gas within a well, NORM are brought to the surface by the formation pressure
or as a result of injection of seawater. Generally, levels of NORM in water, oil and gas are very low.
However, as changes in pressure and temperature occur, and when mixed with injected seawater,
Radium 226 & 228 may co-precipitate with barium sulphate and natural carbonates to form scale. The
scale deposits in pipelines and surface equipment and sludge containing Radium 226 & 228 may also
accumulate in pits and tanks (Refer to Figure ).
Radium-226, which is the most common isotope found in NORM, is an alpha emitter and accompanied
by gamma radiation. The isotope has a half-life of ca. 1600 years. The second isotope Radium-228, is a
beta emitter and has a half-life of 5.76 years.

(b) Presence of NORM
(i) NORM in Oil and Gas Production
Analysis of oil and gas from many different wells has shown that the long-lived uranium and
thorium isotopes are not mobilized from the rock formations that contain them. However Ra-
226, Ra-224, Ra-228 and Pb-210 are mobilized, and appear mainly in the water co-produced
during oil and gas extraction. These isotopes and their radioactive progeny can then
precipitate out of solution, along with sulphate and carbonate deposits as scale or sludge in
pipes and related equipment. Radon-222 is the immediate decay product of Radium-226
and preferentially follows gas lines. It decays (through several rapid steps) to Pb-210 which
can therefore build up as a thin film in gas extraction equipment.
The level of reported radioactivity varies significantly, depending on the radioactivity of the
reservoir rock and the salinity of the water co-produced from the well. The higher the salinity,
the more NORM is likely to be mobilized. Since salinity often increases with the age of a well,
old wells tend to exhibit higher NORM levels than younger ones.

(ii) Mineral Sand
Mineral sands used generally as abrasive blasting material (e.g., sand and garnet) may
contain zircon, ilmenite, and rutile, with xenotime and monazite. These minerals are mined
in many countries and production amounts to millions of tonnes per year of zirconium and
titanium (from rutile and ilmenite), though thorium, tin and the rare earth elements are
associated. The NORM aspect is due to monazite – a rare earth phosphate containing a
variety of rare earth minerals (particularly cerium and lanthanum) and 5-12% (typically about
7%) thorium, and xenotime – yttrium phosphate with traces of uranium and thorium.
The minerals in the sands are subject to gravity concentration, and some concentrates are
significantly radioactive, up to 4000 Bq/kg. Most of this NORM ends up in the waste streams
from mineral processing (often including monazite) and so, apart from zircon, the final
product is itself devoid of NORM. However, sometimes niobium and tantalum are recovered
from the waste stream, and residues may be used as either landfill or in construction sites
where there is a possibility of public exposure.

(j)  NORM Sampling Techniques - Contamination Monitoring - Wipe Tests
Monitoring for the presence of alpha/beta contamination on a surface is usually carried out by direct
measurement  with  contamination  probe.  This  direct  measurement  reading  simply  indicates
whether Alpha/Beta contamination on a surface is present. However, additional information by
carrying out a ‘wipe test’ shall be obtained. A wipe test allows a distinction to be made between
fixed and loose contamination.

(i)  Wipe a tissue over the surface to be surveyed with an area of 300 cm2; this will remove a
proportion of the contamination.
(ii)  Take the wipe to a low background area and if the tissue is wet allow drying.
(iii)  Hold the wipe as close as possible to the contamination probe (Alpha and Beta Probe)
without touching it and note any response from the meter. Record the results.
(iv)  The Correction factor for wipe test is 2 for an area about 300 cm2.
(k)  Radiation Survey of Personnel for Contamination
(i)  Hold the alpha and beta probe with the window as close as possible to the surface of the
body (within 1 centimetre) without touching it. Slowly pass the probe over the area, noting
any response from the rate meter at a rate of 5 to 7 cm per second.
(ii)  Certain areas of the body require special attention, including:
  The head and hair.
  The face, especially around the mouth and nostrils, paying special attention to any
facial hair.
  Hands and feet, including cuffs and ankles of clothing.

(ii) Workers Protection and Training
During normal operating conditions at NORM-impacted sites, external radiation exposure is
usually low enough that no changes to normal work procedures are required. However,
during non-routine operations such as well workovers, equipment maintenance, equipment
handling and repair, and vessel entry, employees may have direct physical contact with
NORM-impacted sand, scale, and sludge. Site RPO shall evaluate the levels of NORM and
determine if employee exposures have the potential to require monitoring as specified in
Section 7.6.5 (e) of this Standard.
Personnel working with NORM shall be given awareness and training on the associated
health hazards by trained RPO.
(iii) Workers Protection from External Radiation
External radiation exposure involves external source with gamma radiation of which controls
are straight forward and simple as follows:
Minimize the time spent close to a NORM installation (vessels, pipes, valves, pumps),
or the exposure time when the workers are handling NORM wastes during desludging.
The total dose a person will receive while near a NORM installation or waste is directly
proportional to the length of time for which worker is exposed. Therefore, unclassified
workers managing NORM wastes must limit the exposure time at which effective dose
rate is below the annual dose rate of 1 mSv/year.
Maximize the distance between personnel and any NORM installations. Radiation
exposure decreases rapidly as one moves further from NORM installations. Therefore,
unclassified workers managing NORM wastes must be at a minimum distance at
which effective dose rate is below the annual dose rate of 1 mSv/year.

7.6.6. RISK ASSESSMENT
(a) Radiation Identification And Measurement
Ionizing and non-ionizing radiation hazards that have the potential to adversely harm workers shall
be identified so that exposure risks can be assessed and control strategies can be determined and
implemented. Hazard identification activities can be performed by physically walking though the
workplace and identifying radiation sources such as density meters, lasers, laboratory X-Ray
equipment, magnetic and electrical fields and NORM sources such as pipes, tanks and sludge
stockpiles. Qualitative assessment information shall be obtained through interviews with
employees and Contractors and through review of applicable records where available, such as
equipment registers. At minimum, sources emitting the following non-ionising radiations shall be
identified and included in the radiation risk assessment:
(i) Ultraviolet Radiation (UV-A, UV-B and UV-C);
(ii) Visible Radiation (Illumination);
(iii) Lasers; and
(iv) Non-Ionising Electromagnetic Radiation.
Where any person is likely to be exposed to radiation, exposure monitoring shall be performed in
order to determine site-specific Controlled Areas (7.5 µSv/hr) and Supervised Areas (<2.5 µSv/hr)
and to establish occupational groups of workers that are to be designated as Classified Workers.
Radiation risk assessment activities shall be conducted in accordance with HSE-OH-03 ADNOC
Standard on Occupational Health Risk Management with the objective of:
(i) Identifying radiation sources, both ionizing and non-ionizing which can cause personal
exposure to radiation (acute and chronic);
(ii) Identifying person(s) who work with radioactive sources or material and determine the nature
and magnitude of the risks arising from each of the radiation hazards;
(iii) Establishing site-specific Controlled Areas and Supervised Areas; and
(iv) Establishing and defining site-specific Classified Workers.
Radiation assessment activities are broken down into two components, the first being an
assessment of the working environment and the second being an assessment of personal radiation
exposure. The difference between these assessments is that radiation assessment of the working
environment is conducted to determine the radiation ‘level’ within an environment or in proximity to
identified radiation sources, whereas a personal assessment is performed in order to determine a
person’s radiation exposure or ‘dose’ across the working year for comparison against FANR
defined Dose Limits.
Both assessment activities form part of the Occupational Health Risk Assessment and are further
elaborated in ADNOC OHRM Standard [Ref. 52].
(b) Assessment of Radiation Levels In the Occupational Environment
The baseline level of radiation in the occupational environment shall be established and then
periodically monitored thereafter (where applicable including whenever there are changes in
engineering controls, production methods or materials used).
A NORM survey that includes sampling and analysis shall be conducted to establish the baseline
level of NORM at each facility.

7.6.8. CONTROL OF RISK
Once radiation risks have been identified and assessed, resources shall be directed to establishing and
implementing radiation control strategies. At a minimum, site-specific Radiation Control Plans shall be
established based upon FANR requirements and prioritised based upon the class of risk (i.e. High, High-
medium, Medium or Low) in combination with the number of persons exposed and the frequency and
magnitude of the exposure. The site-specific Radiation Control Plans shall aim to control exposures to
radiation using one or a combination of the following:
(i) Reducing radiation exposure time;
(ii) Increasing distance between works and radiation sources; and/or
(iii) Intercepting radiation pathways.
(a) Time
There is a direct relationship between radiation dose and time of exposure. Therefore, Radiation
Control Plans shall include documented actions aimed to reduce an individual’s radiation dose by
controlling the amount of time exposed to radioactive sources and substances.
When exposed to high risk radiation levels, individual’s dose levels shall be routinely monitored
during work using personal dosimeter alarms.

7.6.11. CLEANING METHODS
The choice of the removal procedure shall depend on its removal efficiency as well as the resources, cost,
and safety considerations outlined earlier.
(a) Manual cleaning
Manual cleaning does not involve any machinery and may be as simple as hand washing
equipment with detergent and water. The removal of NORM-impacted tank sludge could be done
by manual cleaning (for example, shovelling the sludge from the tank and placing it into storage
containers), or washing and pumping could do it.
(b) Mechanical cleaning
Mechanical cleaning methods vary widely and may include high-pressure water washing, drilling
or reaming, and vacuuming.
(i) High-Pressure Water Blasting
Water blasting is a commonly used method of NORM removal. It is used to clean scale from
production equipment, such as separators and tanks, as well as from tubing and pipes. High
pressure water blasting is usually defined as water jetting operations using pressure above
750 psi. NORM scale cleaning operations typically range in pressure from 10,000 psi to
30,000 psi. High pressure washing systems shall be closed systems. Wash water shall not
be allowed to escape to the environment but shall be contained, recirculated, or filtered to
remove scale. The ultimate disposal of wash water after settlement of solids must be injected
into field disposal wells along with produced water or transported to drilling mud disposal
wells at appropriate locations.
(ii) Drilling or Reaming
Drilling or reaming is typically used in the cleaning of production tubing and piping. A tube
will be positioned on a cleaning rack and a reamer enters one end of the tube to begin
cleaning. This process has high removal efficiency but may generate NORM impacted dust
if used as a dry process. Wet drilling processes may be used to reduce the amount of dust
generated. As with high pressure water blasting, wet and dry drilling processes shall be
closed systems to prevent the release of NORM materials.
(iii) Vacuuming
Vacuum systems may be wet or dry processes and are generally effective in removing loose
material. Vacuuming is often used before manually cleaning equipment. Vacuum systems
shall be properly filtered to prevent the spread of material to surrounding areas and reduce
the potential for airborne activity. Care shall be taken to ensure that large amounts of NORM
material do not accumulate in the vacuum system.
(iv) Equipment in Gas Service
NORM material may occur in natural gas processing equipment as thin films, which are often
invisible to the naked eye. These films may consist of radioactive materials that embed
themselves into the walls of the processing equipment. Chemical film removal or grinding
may be necessary to remove NORM film from gas plant equipment. Additionally, NORM
material may accumulate as a dusty black powder on the internal surface of gas
processing/storage equipment. The powder, often iron sulfide, has been shown to potentially
contain elevated levels of Pb-210. It shall be noted that, due to the radioactive species

7.7. HYPERBARIC PRESSURE
Atmospheric (or barometric) pressure is the pressure exerted by the mass of atmosphere above sea level.
This pressure decreases as we move upward through the atmosphere and increases as we descend
down into a mineshaft or deep into the sea.
As a rough indicator, the following pressure levels can be experienced:
(i) 0.5 atmosphere Aircraft at 18,000 feet or 5,500 meters;
(ii) 1 atmosphere = 1 bar Boat at Sea level;
(iii) 2 Atmosphere = 2 bar Diver working at 33 feet or 10 meters of sea water;
(iv) 3 atmosphere = 3 bar Diver working at 66 feet or 20 meters of sea water.
7.7.1. HEALTH EFFECTS
The main hazards associated with working in hyperbaric pressures, particularly during diving and
conducting underwater welding and maintenance mainly relate to safety issues. However, hyperbaric
exposure caused by diving is associated with several Occupational Health Hazards such as
decompression illness (DCI); gas embolism and barotrauma. Some also relate to exposure to airborne
contaminants in the breathing air and noise.
Recovery from hyperbaric conditions depends on the severity and nature of the diving incident, but in
many cases it can be complete with the person making a full recovery. However, there is less certainty
about the possible long-term health effects of diving, particularly in the absence of an obvious safety
related injury.
Dysbaric osteonecrosis [i.e. death or breakdown of bones caused by reduced blood flow in the bones
and joints] has been associated with diving and is now a notifiable industrial disease among divers in
UAE. Other aspects of health however, have raised concerns among divers which includes neurological
abnormalities, lung function changes and inner ear damage.
7.7.2. NOISE AND FATIGUE
Under hyperbaric conditions a diver’s hearing is more sensitive, and the noise of gas being injected into
a diving bell or chamber, particularly when from a high pressure source, may cause permanent damage
to their hearing.
The following noise prevention/reduction measures shall be considered:
(i) Fitting gas inlets and outlets with a silencer; and
(ii) The use of suitable ear protection to prevent hearing damage for divers under pressure if
the noise exceeds acceptable levels. The use of noise protection equipment shall reduce
the quality of oral communications.
During saturation diving, fatigue arising from irregular work and rest patterns can affect efficiency and
safety. Noise and traffic flow through and around the saturation chamber complex shall be kept to an
absolute minimum so that divers get the best chance to rest and sleep.
