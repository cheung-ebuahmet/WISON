1 INTRODUCTION
It is the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) Group; Health, Safety and Environment (HSE) policy
to ensure that its Employees and Contractors are protected from occupational risks that they could be
exposed to. This shall be achieved through an Occupational Health Risk Management (OHRM) process,
which involves hazard identification, risk assessment and risk control practices. In order to carry out an
effective risk management process, it is necessary to have a clear understanding of the legal context,
concepts, risk analysis, risk management, control processes and the role played by all involved in the
process. It is also required that risk management process is based on solid and tested methodologies.
This document outlines the requirements of OHRM process to conduct Occupational Health Risk
Assessment (OHRA) study (first part is Qualitative assessment, and second part is Quantitative
assessment) to ensure that a comprehensive and structured approach is adopted by all ADNOC Group
Companies and Contractors. The Quantitative OHRA makes use of air sampling techniques, analytical
methodologies and statistical tools to ensure that the assessment is accurate, valid and meaningful.
Occupational Health described in this Standard, is a field combining the methodologies and knowledge
from two main fields [Refer to Figure 1]. These include (Occupational) Industrial Hygiene and
Occupational Medicine.

2 PURPOSE
This Standard specifies ‘mandatory requirements of Occupational Health Risk Assessment to be
conducted by ADNOC Group Companies and their contractors to ensure that the occupational health
risks are adequately managed at a level which is As Low as Reasonably Practicable (ALARP)’.
This Occupational Health Standard provides information to ADNOC Group and Contractors, to enable
informed decisions to be made regarding quantitative assessment, as well as the extent of occupational
exposure to hazardous agents.

7 REQUIREMENTS
(a) ADNOC Group shall be responsible to ensure that OHRA study is conducted for all the phases of
the project life cycle. OHRA study shall be conducted at four phases of a project, regardless of a
stand-alone study or part of HSEIA:
(i) Conceptual design and Front End Engineering & Design [FEED] (Qualitative assessments);
(ii) Engineering Procurement and Construction (EPC) (Qualitative and Quantitative
assessments);
(iii) Operations. (Qualitative assessment as per HSEIA followed by Quantitative assessment);
(iv) Decommissioning and Disposal (Qualitative and Quantitative assessment).
(b) OHRA study shall be conducted as per the methodology provided in Appendix 1 report, prepared
either as a stand-alone document or part of HSEIA report.
(c) The process involved in the survey is described in Appendix 2 of this Standard.
(d) OHRA study shall be carried out by qualified, experienced and demonstrably competent personnel,
who is either appointed [e.g. external consultant] or directly employed by the Group Company or
Contractor organization.
(e) OHRA shall encompass all facilities, workplaces and activities to be performed by employees and
contractors, where potentially hazardous agents are present and, as a minimum, shall cover the
following:
(i) Routine operational activities and tasks including those carried out in office environments;
(ii) Planned maintenance activities;
(iii) Emergency response activities in controlling accidental release of chemical, hazardous
material or energy;
(iv) Cleaning activities;
(v) Non-routine activities such as shutdowns or unplanned maintenance;
(vi) Waste handling and disposal tasks;
(vii) Laboratory, quality assurance and quality control activities; and
(viii) Other planned and unplanned activities.
(f) Contractor shall be responsible for preparing OHRA study for all the phases. Quantitative OHRA
study shall be done by the contractor for health hazards identified to have ‘Medium’ health risk

(l) The OHRA shall be presented as a formal report, written in clear language and free from
ambiguous and theoretical discussions and conclusions. The report shall contain adequate
appendices which provides information to the reader and reviewer to all of the findings and

(ii) Meet legal requirements set out in Federal and local legislation and in the ADNOC Standards

(iii) Contain sufficient and traceable information to ensure clarity on how the conclusions were
reached;
(iv) Act as a benchmark against which future reviews of the OHRA can be compared; and
(v) Be kept for a minimum of 40 years from the date of issue or for the lifespan of the facility,
whichever is the longest.

(a) OCCUPATIONAL HEALTH RISK ASSESSMENT PROCESS
Occupational Health Risk Assessment is a method for estimating health risks from exposure to various
levels of a workplace hazard. Workers shall be protected from occupational risks that they could be
exposed to. This could be achieved through a risk management process, which involves risk analysis,
risk assessment and risk control practices. In order to carry out an effective risk management process, it
is necessary to have a clear understanding of the legal context, concepts, risk analysis, assessment and
control processes and the role-played individuals.
Consequently, it is a process of information gathering, face-to-face interviewing, data acquisition,
establishing what measures are [or need to be] in place to control risk; then reviewing or verifying
effectiveness of the control measures.
(i) EXPOSURE ASSESSMENT
Quantitative assessment of exposure is a process where the nature and extent of contact to certain
hazardous agents is determined by a well-defined procedure and specific techniques. The process
also requires using appropriate laboratories for analysing samples and generate reports for taking
corrective actions for controlling the risk.
A comprehensive Quantitative Occupational Health Exposure Assessment should consist of
several steps as indicated below. However, the Industrial Hygienist may decide to skip a particular
step, if it is considered redundant or unnecessary.
The following are the stages of exposure assessment process:
1. REVIEW OF QUALITATIVE HEALTH RISK ASSESSMENT
Review of Qualitative Health Risk Assessment results e.g. health hazard Risk Register, task-
based risk assessment; is required prior to the conduct of Quantitative Exposure
Assessment.
Qualitative OHRA shall define Quantitative Exposure Assessment recommendation and
plan. Current result of Qualitative Occupational Health Risk Assessment (OHRA) shall serve
as the basis input for Quantitative Exposure Assessment; however, during the process
further health hazard identification and job task appraisal may be required to capture any
significant change to the process hazard, risk control and unidentified health hazard and risk
during qualitative stage.
2. DETERMINATION OF SAMPLING AND ANALYSIS METHOD
Current HSE-MDHS (UK), NIOSH-NMAM (US), OSHA (US) or ISO sampling methodology
shall be employed for airborne sampling and analysis of workplace health hazards.

As guidance, a general outline of the various categories of equipment available for
Occupational Health Monitoring is given below.
Category 1: Semi-Quantitative Monitors
This category represents equipment used for direct, rapid, and “semi-quantitative” indication
of the presence and level [concentration or intensity] of a hazard in the workplace. The
equipment is suitable for personal and ambient measurements.
Typical examples are single use colour detector tubes, laser particulate monitors, single or
multiple gas detectors, low cost sound level meters, single axis vibration meters, non-
integrating thermometers, hygrometers, film badge radiation dosimeters, etc.
Category 2: Personal Dosimeters
This category covers portable, battery powered equipment with detection technologies that
are specific to the agents of interest. Dosimeters are clipped on the lapels or helmets of
personnel entering or working in environments where there is a potential risk to health from
a specific agent.
In certain cases, a dosimeter may be set to alarm at predetermined levels and to act as an
early warning and thus enabling the person to react [e.g. leave the area, raise the alarms,
or escape] in a manner that minimizes risks to his life and the lives of others working in the
vicinity. Typical examples of such instrumentation are oxygen, flammable gas and hydrogen
sulphide monitors, ionising and non-ionising radiation dosimeters, etc.
Most dosimeters used for measurement of physical agents [e.g. noise, heat, vibration, or
radiation] and some used in measurement of chemical agents [e.g. dusts, H S, oxygen,

computer interface for data downloading and processing.

calibration reference or at set frequencies [usually annually] by an accredited organization
using primary standards and which issues a formal calibration certificate.

6. GRAB SAMPLING
Grab samples are taken over number of short periods of time (generally only minutes or

7. WORKPLACE EXPOSURE LIMITS AND THRESHOLD LIMIT VALUES
International occupational health standards [also known as Threshold Limit
Values/Workplace Exposure Limits/Permissible Exposure Limits/Recommended Exposure
Limits] are based on two time-dependent exposure thresholds, namely 8-hours and 15-
minutes. In some standards, reference is also made to Ceiling Values [or a maximum
exposure limit] that must not be exceeded at any time.
Consequently, when planning a quantitative survey, selection of the optimum sampling
period should ensure that:
It is consistent with the compliance limit against which the data will be compared;

It takes into account the variations in working exposure patterns; and

It provides an accurate reflection of the degree of harm posed by the hazard(s) being

monitored.
Important Note: Where a hazard is governed by a Ceiling Value, it may be necessary to
quantify exposure using continuous monitoring equipment that can capture the exposure
profiles against time.
8. GROUPING PERSONNEL AND SAMPLE NUMBERS
Grouping personnel within a workplace population is based on the concept of “stratified
random sampling” [Refs. 6 & 7], in which the population is divided into groups that have or
are believed to have equivalent exposures. These are known as “Homogeneous Exposure
Groups” or “Similar Exposure Groups” [SEGs] referred to in various Occupational Health
standards.

10. DATA INTERPRETATION
Once the 95% upper confidence level has been determined using the IHSTAT software, the
value can be compared against the OEL to determine the acceptability of the exposure
profile. Interpretation of quantitative survey data and correlation of the findings shall be
carried out by an experienced and competent Industrial Hygienist.
The Threshold Limit Values found in Annex (7b): Maximum Allowable Limits for Air Pollutants
in Working Areas (Chemical Substances) prescribed in the Cabinet Decree (12) of 2006
Regulation Concerning Protection of Air Pollution is the mandatory first point of reference
for use in interpreting occupational exposure data. Where there are no limits mentioned in
this UAE Federal Regulation, the Workplace Exposure Limits (WELs) of UK-HSE shall be
referenced; followed by OSHA PELs, NIOSH RELs and ACGIH TLVs.
Important Note: Results obtained from fixed area sampling are suitable only for
determining background levels or concentrations of hazardous agents and cannot be used
to demonstrate compliance with OEL. This is because OELs are targeted only at workers
and human conditions where nearly all workers may be repeatedly exposed, daily over a
working lifetime without adverse health effects [Ref 24].

12. IMPLEMENTATION AND VERIFICATION OF CONTROLS
The application of controls for the elimination or minimisation of exposure to hazardous
agents must be always carried out in accordance with the hierarchy of controls. This includes
elimination/substitution, isolation, application of engineering controls, implementation of
system, procedural & behavioural changes; and lastly the use of protective equipment.
ADNOC Occupational Health Standard places specific duties on each Group Company and
contractor to ensure that the controls chosen to reduce risks to health are effective and they
are regularly maintained. The objective shall always be to verify that each element of the
control system and strategy is performing at an optimum level and as intended. Effectiveness
of controls can also be verified through airborne exposure reassessment.
