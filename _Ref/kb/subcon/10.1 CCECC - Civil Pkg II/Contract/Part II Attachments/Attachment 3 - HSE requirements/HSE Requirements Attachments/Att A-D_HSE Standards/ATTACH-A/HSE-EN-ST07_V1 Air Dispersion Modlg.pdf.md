1. INTRODUCTION
1.1. BACKGROUND
This Standard provides guidance on suitable methods and approaches that should be used when
undertaking Air Dispersion Modelling (ADM) studies, in order to assess potential air quality impacts
arising from Abu Dhabi National Oil Company’s (ADNOC) assets and operations. The intention of this
Standard is not to provide a technical description of the theory behind dispersion modelling, however,
applicable information sources and references are provided within the text and should be consulted where
further additional information is required.
1.2. OBJECTIVES
The objectives are to standardize model applications for regulatory purposes, thus ensuring consistency
in the applications of models, and creating confidence and transparency of appropriate model applications.
This document covers the development of a modelling protocol, establishment of the preferred modelling
software, guidance on emission inventories, meteorology, and baseline air quality requirements. The
Standard draws largely from published international modelling guidelines and sets out to provide the
following:
 An overview of the ADM approaches.
 Guidance on appropriate technical methods; and
 Information required to demonstrate that a source meets the Ambient Air Quality Standards (AAQSs).
2. PURPOSE
This document provides technical guidance to undertake ADM studies which form part of the
Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA) (HSE-EN-ST01) or Pollution Prevention and Control (PPC)
(HSE-EN-ST02) reporting process for various projects, facilities, sites and activities under ADNOC’s
control. ADNOC has produced these guidelines in line with the following principles:
 To ensure that the ADM study undertaken is tailored to the individual needs of each project;
 To ensure that the necessary steps of the protocol are applied correctly and consistently using
accepted scientific techniques;
 To assist ADNOC in air quality management decisions to protect public health and the environment;
and,
 To ascertain/determine suitable discharge conditions so that AAQSs are met at the ground level.
In subjective aspects, the standard serves as a guideline to the modelling practitioner for the satisfaction
of minimum requirements proposed by the ADNOC Group.
3. SCOPE
This standard provide guidelines to the ADNOC Group and its Contractors when developing an ADM
study. The ADM process is applicable across all relevant ADNOC projects and facilities / sites that entail
the emissions of potentially harmful pollutants into the atmosphere for both standalone ADM studies as
well as those intended to supplement the Health, Safety and Environmental Impact Assessment (HSEIA)
Process (HSE-RM-ST02). This includes the assessment of potential air quality impacts both prior to
construction and operation as well as for the continuous assessment, during the operations phase, of
regulatory compliance.
ADNOC Group and its Contractors shall ensure that all requirements listed herein are fully understood,
implemented, complied with and monitored at all times.

7. REQUIREMENTS
7.1. AIR DISPERSION MODELLING PROCESS
This section provides information for modelling practitioners to follow when conducting ADM studies. The
custodians of the project are expected to arrange a meeting with their GCs Corporate HSE prior to any
modelling effort to avoid any misconceptions and consequent delays. A plan of study in the form of an Air
Quality Modelling protocol is also required for submittal to ADNOC for approval in order to decide on
aspects of assessment detail level. A schematic of the modelling process is shown in Figure 7.1.1 along
with a description of each step [1].
The simple screening procedure (Tier 1) is applied to determine if the source poses a potential threat to
air quality. The purpose of first applying a simple screening procedure is to eliminate analysis of those
sources that clearly will not contribute to ambient concentrations in excess of air quality standards or
allowable concentration increments. A relatively large degree of "conservatism" is incorporated in that
screening procedure to provide reasonable assurance that maximum concentrations will not be
underestimated. If the results of the simple screening procedure indicate a potential to exceed air quality
standards or allowable concentrations, then a detailed analysis is conducted by recommended models
(Tier 2).

7.1.1. STEP 1 SET THE CONTEXT
The context of the study shall be set through definition of the scope, aims and objectives of the ADM
study under consideration. Defining these aspects allows the modelling practitioner to identify key
standard and non-standard modelling requirements before resources are allocated to the task. Once the
context for the study has been set, the schedules and assignment of tasks to project stakeholders can
be determined and reviewed by ANDOC if required, in a timeframe satisfactory to all project proponents.
7.1.2. STEP 2 CHARACTERISE SOURCES / CONTAMINANTS
Based on the context of the study defined in the first step, the modelling practitioner must gather
information regarding the emission sources and contaminants to be modelled. All dispersion models
require input data describing the rate of pollutants emitted, the physical mechanics of how they are
emitted and the environment into which the emission is propagated [Ref. 1]. Detailed guidance regarding
the characterization of sources and contaminants can be found in Section 7.3.
7.1.3. STEP 3 CHARACTERIZE PHYSICAL AND METEOROLOGICAL SETTING
The modelling domain describes the extents of the study area. The size of the modelling domain is
dependent on multiple factors including the source of emissions, overlapping effects from other sources
and in some cases the topographical and geopolitical setting of the region to be modelled. Geophysical
aspects largely influence the extent of pollutant deposition. For example, when screening the potential

7.1.4. STEP 4 DETERMINE ASSESSMENT LEVEL
The information gathering procedures described in Steps 1 – 3 of the ADM procedures together with a
screening model allow the modelling practitioner to formulate an understanding on the assessment level
required to satisfy the scope, aims and objectives of the modelling study. Screening models are usually
applied before the refined air quality model to determine if refined modelling is needed. In case the results
of the simple screening model indicate a potential to exceed allowable concentrations, then a detailed
analysis is conducted. Where further assessment is required (post screening), the air quality assessment
should utilise the modelling software most conducive to informing the decision-maker, and meet the level
of detail required for the particular analysis under consideration without dedicating too many resources
by way of funds and project man-hours, but without compromising on the particular air quality analysis
requirements of the project.
7.1.5. STEP 5 SELECT MODEL(S)
After satisfying the requirements in Steps 1 – 4 of the ADM procedures, the modelling practitioner may
select the model(s) to be used to satisfy the requirements of the project by reviewing their technical
capabilities and recommended uses. The following are considerations regarding the selection of an
appropriate air dispersion model [Ref. 1].
 If more than one model is appropriate, the modelling practitioner should select the model that may
be applied most easily; and,
 If none of the models recommended are appropriate, ADNOC must review the merit of using any
other proposed model based on motivation provided by the modelling practitioners.
Section 7.2 provides information on model selection based on technical capabilities and recommended
uses.
7.1.6. STEP 6 PREPARE A MODELLING PROTOCOL
Before an ADM study is conducted, the modelling practitioner shall prepare and submit a Modelling
Protocol detailing the modelling scope and objectives, scenarios, assumptions and data sources (ambient,
emissions, site, etc.) for ADNOC Group Company approval. Should consultation with ADNOC GHSE
Function be required by the Group Company, such shall be undertaken prior to the Group Company
approval of the air dispersion modelling protocol.
7.1.7. STEP 7 DETERMINE INPUTS – SOURCE, METEOROLOGICAL AND GEOPHYSICAL INFORMATION
Source parameter definition, meteorological data requirements and geophysical representativeness shall
be adequately described by the modelling practitioner to meet the requirements of the ADM study.
Emissions inventory requirements necessary to determine the input parameters required for the model
are presented in Section 7.3. In general, the emissions inventory may consist of the project sources, as
well as other applicable onsite and offsite sources. The modelling practitioner is required to calculate the
emissions for proposed projects either through general calculation using physical parameters or vendor-
guaranteed emission limits, which should always be empirically verified. Typically, these emissions will
be explicitly stated by source, and further summarised collectively in the units of tonnes per annum for

7.1.8. STEP 8 DETERMINE BACKGROUND AIR QUALITY
All assessments must consider the ambient background concentrations of atmospheric contaminants
regardless of the level of detail assumed, with the intention to compare the ambient air quality to the
cumulative impact of new emission sources on the region defined in the proposed modelling domain. The
modelling protocol must explicitly outline the baseline data used for impact assessment purposes. Further
guidance on baseline data requirements are presented in Section 7.6.
7.1.9. STEP 9 PREPARE INPUT FILES AND RUN MODELS
Once the model inputs have been determined, all input files should be prepared in a standardised manner
and imported to the model for calculation.
7.1.10. STEP 10 PREPARE OUTPUT AND SUBMIT DOCUMENTATION
The model results should be discussed and interpreted in a way that satisfies the scope, aims and
objectives of the study under consideration. The submission of a draft report should be accompanied with
the relevant input and output files, including the project emissions inventory. Section 7.7 provides a
reporting framework intended to guide the modelling practitioner in the presentation of the results.
7.2. RECOMMENDED MODELS
7.2.1. APPLICABLE MODELS
The level of sophistication of the modelling analysis will be dictated by the size and complexity of the
proposed project, the nature of the surrounding terrain, and the available meteorological data. For simple
projects with relatively small emissions, a simple “screening” analysis may be appropriate. For more
complex facilities, facilities located close to “complex terrain” (defined as terrain higher than the final
plume height of a particular stack), facilities with significant building downwash, and facilities where
coastal fumigation and recirculation exists, more sophisticated or “refined” models may be required [Ref.
3].
7.2.2. SCREENING MODELS
(a) SCREEN3
SCREEN3 is a single source Gaussian plume model which provides maximum ground-level
concentrations for point, area, flare, and volume sources, as well as concentrations in the cavity
zone, and concentrations due to inversion break-up and shoreline fumigation.

(b) AERSCREEN
AERSCREEN is the screening model based on AERMOD. The model will produce estimates of
"worst-case" 1-hour concentrations for a single source, without the need for hourly meteorological
data, and includes conversion factors to estimate "worst-case" 3-hour, 8-hour, 24-hour, and annual
concentrations. AERSCREEN is intended to produce concentration estimates that are equal to or
greater than the estimates produced by AERMOD with a fully developed set of meteorological and
terrain data, but the degree of conservatism will vary depending on the application.
AERSCREEN is recommended for use on:
 Single point, area, volume sources;
 Single building effects on point source;
 Building wake cavity concentration;
 Flare releases; and,
 Transport distances of less than 50 km in simple terrain.
7.2.3. REFINED MODELS
(a) AERMOD
AERMOD is the recommended model for more sophisticated near-source applications in all terrain
types (where near-source is defined as less than 50km from source).
AERMOD is a steady-state plume dispersion model for simulating transport and dispersion from
point, area, or volume sources based on an up-to-date characterisation of the atmospheric
boundary layer. The model can be applied to rural and urban areas, flat and complex terrain,
surface and elevated releases, and multiple sources, including, point, area and volume sources.
AERMOD has been designed to handle light wind conditions (wind speeds less than 1m/s), and
also incorporates an approach for treatment of horizontal meander that can be significant under
such conditions. The model can also accept multiple levels of site-specific wind measurements
and will determine the transport direction for each source based on the wind direction from the
vertical profile appropriate for the individual plume.
AERMOD is recommended for use on:
 Transport distances over which steady-state assumptions are appropriate, less than 50 km
(depends on terrain);
 Sources in an industrial complex (single or multiple point, area, line, volume sources) with no
buildings or single or multiple buildings with building downwash;
 Gas and particle depositions;

 Rural or urban areas; and,

(b) CALPUFF
CALPUFF is the recommended model for dispersion applications requiring detailed description of
physical and chemical atmospheric processes, typically associated with assessments for distances
greater than 50 km.
CALPUFF is a multi-layer, multi-species non-steady-state puff dispersion modelling system that
simulates the effects of time- and space-varying meteorological conditions on pollutant transport,
transformation, and removal. The model can simulate emissions at downward distances ranging
from tens of metres up to 300 km for multiple point, volume, area and/ or line sources with constant
or variable emission rates. CALPUFF includes algorithms for near-field effects such as stack tip
downwash, building downwash, transitional buoyant and momentum plume rise, rain cap effects
and partial plume penetration into elevated temperature inversions.
While the United States Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) has historically recommended
CALPUFF Version 5.8, later versions, which correct errors and shortcomings of the earlier versions
may be used.
CALPUFF can use different forms of meteorological input data (surface, profile, or gridded);
however, this Standard recommends 3D prognostic model outputs (e.g. from models such as Fifth-
Generation Penn State / NCAR Mesoscale Model (MM5) or Weather Research and Forecasting
(WRF)) processed in CALMET or Mesoscale Model Interface Program (MMIF). CALPUFF is
recommended for most long-range (i.e. > 50 km) modelling applications.
CALPUFF is recommended for use for:
 Long-range transport distances between 50 and 300 km;
 Complex, non-steady-state meteorological conditions where transport distances are less than
50 km, on a case-by-case basis including:
 inhomogeneous winds
 inversion breakup fumigation
 shoreline fumigation
 stagnation conditions
 No buildings, single or multiple buildings;
 Availability of detailed meteorological and geophysical inputs;
 Deposition and light extinction where long-range transport distances are greater than 50 km;
 Secondary formation of particulate matter (PM) in long-range transport distances greater than
50 km;
 Multiple source (point, area, volume) and buildings; and,
 All new Projects in Ruwais.
(c) SCIPUFF
SCIPUFF is appropriate for modelling both short and long range (greater than 50 km) transport,
steady or non-steady state emissions of primary pollutants (gases or particles), buoyant or neutral

(d) OFFSHORE AND SHORELINE MODELS
With regards to coastal sites and offshore operations (particularly platforms), CALPUFF, Offshore

(e) OTHER MODELS
Some types of sources require specialised model formulations. These types are typically part of
accident release scenarios, which are emissions of short duration or either situations where a
specific model is required. Examples of accidental releases include complex chemical
transformations, fires, jet (failure of a valve on a high-pressure pipe), tank rupture, denser than air
gas, buoyant (i.e., emitted gas is lighter than air and rises to an altitude of neutral buoyancy) and
evaporative (i.e., spill of volatile liquid, where emission rate changes as the liquid evaporates over
time). Models that may be used for emergency purposes include, but are not limited to, SLAB,
CAMEO/ALOHA, HEGADIS, DEGADIS, etc. It must be noted that this standard does not provide
guidance for such cases.
In addition, situations where more complex atmospheric processes are to be considered in
modelling studies, for example the treatment of ozone (O3) and PM formation, models
recommended in this document might not suffice. In certain instances, the modellers may consider
the use photochemical grid models, such as Models-3 / Community Multi-scale Air Quality (CMAQ)
modelling system, NAME (Numerical Atmospheric-dispersion Modelling Environment),
WRF model coupled with chemistry (WRF-CHEM), or CAMx (Comprehensive Air quality Model
with Extensions). In all instances, specialised models should be selected in consultation with
ADNOC and the competent authority.
(f) AIR QUALITY PRIORITY AREAS
Given the density of industrial facilities within Ruwais, the city is considered an air quality priority
area. Ruwais is also subject to specific meteorological conditions (land/sea breeze recirculation),
and has number of large industrial facilities in very close proximity to the coastline. Coastal
fumigation is of concern in cases involving tall stacks located close to the shoreline, and hence
when undertaking ADM studies in Ruwais, CALPUFF or SCIPUFF shall be used.

7.4. METEOROLOGY
Consult with the ADNOC GHSE Function to determine if representative meteorological data are available
for the area under consideration. If the only available meteorological data are of questionable
representativeness, ADNOC may require applicants to perform modelling analyses using alternative
meteorological data. A summary of the various options regarding meteorological data is presented in the
following sections.
7.4.1. ON-SITE METEOROLOGICAL DATA
In the event that ADNOC-prepared meteorological data are not available for the Project site, onsite
meteorological data may be used. The meteorological data used in the modelling analysis should be
representative of the meteorological conditions at the Project site. The representativeness of measured
meteorological data is dependent on the following:
 The distance from the meteorological monitoring site to the area under consideration;
 The complexity of the terrain surrounding both the meteorological monitoring site and the area under
consideration;
 The exposure of the meteorological monitoring site; and
 The period of time during which data are collected [Ref. 6].

on-site data to be approved, the meteorological station must be suitably quality assured and

7.4.4. PROGNOSTIC DATA
The requirement for meteorological data can be accomplished by either onsite monitoring or
meteorological modelling, however the number of onsite meteorological measurement locations are
limited within ADNOC. Therefore, a meteorological model can be used to generate onsite meteorological
data for use in air quality models. In accordance with international best practice, this guideline
recommends the use of meteorological datasets obtained from the WRF model.
The advantages of the mesoscale model approach are that [Ref. 1]:
 They provide surface and upper air data for any location, thus avoiding the cost of setting up a new
meteorological station and providing information at locations where data is not available;
 It takes less time to generate a data set than collecting data in an observational program;
 The approach avoids subjective decisions regarding the use of quasi-representative meteorological
data (e.g. judgment required to adjust or rotate winds from a remote site to account for different
terrain orientations at the modelling site);
 The approach avoids subjective decisions regarding the interpolation for missing data; and,
 They provide details of the space and time variability of the meteorology in three dimensions within
the modelling domain, which is especially important in complex terrain.

(b) FUGITIVE EMISSION SOURCES
Fugitive emission sources are not released to the atmosphere by stacks; examples include the
following: process leaks from piping components; evaporation from wastewater treatment facilities;
or wind transport from storage piles [Ref. 12]. Fugitive emissions should only be modelled if
emission rates can be reliably quantified. Fugitive emissions should be modelled as area, or area-
polygon emission sources. Documentation of source parameter assumptions should be provided
as part of the modelling study.
(c) RAIN CAPS AND HORIZONTAL RELEASES
Stacks that vent emissions horizontally and/or have rain caps should be modelled as point sources
with stack parameters that will simulate the manner in which emissions are released to the
atmosphere. AERMOD will set the temperature to ambient temperature if the stack exit
temperature is set to 0 °K. If the model being used does not do this, then set the temperature to
ambient temperature or to a close approximation thereof.
If modelling only horizontal stacks that are not capped, turn stack tip downwash off, whether there
are buildings or not. Stack tip downwash calculations are considered inappropriate for horizontal
stacks. If only some stacks have rain caps or are horizontal and others release upward without
caps, use stack tip downwash [Ref .13]

7.6. BASELINE AIR QUALITY REQUIREMENTS
7.6.1. OVERVIEW
In accordance with the EAD Technical Guidance Document [Ref.4] background air quality concentrations
are considered an essential part of the assessment process in order to effectively consider cumulative
impacts associated with the Project.
Background air quality includes two key components:
 Pollutant concentrations from nearby sources other than the one currently being modelled; and
 Regional background, consisting of natural sources, distant anthropogenic sources and other
unidentified sources.
The background concentration must be added to the modelled concentration at selected receptor
locations to determine the expected total concentration of that pollutant at that location [Ref.4]. This total
concentration may then be compared to the applicable air quality standard to determine if the locations
of the receptors may be expected to be within air quality guidelines when the source being modelled is
emitting the pollutant.
In the absence of a background measured value for a particular pollutant of concern, the modelled
concentrations must be presented as a percentage of the ambient standard or guideline, in order to
suitably demonstrate that emissions do not contribute a significant portion to the attainment of the
relevant guidelines or standards.

additional emissions due to the expansion must also be modelled. In these instances, it is acknowledged
that emission data from nearby facilities is not always available, however these sources and their
parameters should be identified and modelled in consultation with ADNOC and the competent authority
subject to data availability.

Priority shall be given to the use of ADNOC or EAD ambient AQMS data for the establishment of baseline
concentrations. Similarly, to the requirements for meteorological data, the station should be
representative of the site considering prevailing wind directions, proximity to the site and data
completeness.
7.6.3. ESTABLISHING BACKGROUND CONCENTRATIONS FOR CUMULATIVE ASSESSMENT
PURPOSES
Background concentrations may already include contributions from the Project site. For situations
involving a source modification where the existing facility is determined to impact the ambient monitor,
the background concentration at each monitor can be determined by excluding values when the source
in question is impacting the monitor. In such cases, monitoring sites inside a 90° sector downwind of the
source may be used to determine the area of impact [Ref. 15]. Alternatively, use a background
concentration from a monitor that isn’t located predominantly downwind of the project site [Ref. 16].
One important aspect of cumulative impact assessment entails combining modelled concentrations with
monitored background concentrations to determine the potential cumulative ambient air quality impacts
[3]. Background air quality data must be sourced from either an existing ADNOC or EAD monitoring
network, or be collected as part of the Project, subject to approval by ADNOC.
The use of a single uniform monitored background contribution is the simplest approach to implement
since it can be applied outside of the modelling system. However, in determining a suitable background
value for short term periods it is acknowledged that use of the overall highest hourly background
concentration will be overly conservative in many cases, as the maximum process contribution and
maximum background concentration may be separated both temporally and spatially, so that the addition
of the two “worst case” concentrations together may not represent a likely event [Ref. 17].
In order to be able to provide a comparison of the short-term average AAQS in conjunction with short-
term average model outputs, the approach advocated by the United Kingdom (UK) Environmental Agency
(EA) should be adopted. When assessing short-term effects, it is reasonable to consider the maximum
short-term modelled output in an additive context with a background concentration equal to two times
(double) the long-term background value [Ref. 16].

7.7. REPORTING FRAMEWORK
This section provides guidance regarding the general requirements to be included in the modelling
protocol and final ADM report submitted to ADNOC. The modelling protocol serves as a framework
describing the minimum requirements to be included to enable sufficient review and facilitate competent
decision-making regarding ambient air quality impacts. Additional information that is relevant and that
which adds value may be included.
7.7.1. MODELLING PROTOCOL
An important aspect of the regulatory modelling process involves the establishment of a modelling
protocol upon which all parties can agree prior to the commencement of the modelling study. While the
pre-application meeting is the initial engagement for the applicant and appropriate reviewing authority to
discuss the various underlying pieces of a modelling protocol, a written modelling protocol should be
agreed upon by the reviewing authority prior to the commencement of any considerable air quality
modelling [Ref. 21].
ADNOC recognizes that many air quality specialists have their own preferred formats for modelling
protocols and does not wish to require applicants to use a specific modelling protocol format mandated
by ADNOC. Instead a listing of typical protocol elements as an aid in developing a modelling protocol has
been provided. This listing does not address all possible components of a protocol. Case-by-case
judgments should be used to decide if additional aspects of the analysis should be included in the protocol
or if certain elements are not necessary in a given situation. An example modelling protocol outline for a
major stationary source is provided below [Ref. 2].
