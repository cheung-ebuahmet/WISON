1.

INTRODUCTION

Health Safety and Environment (HSE) Design Philosophy Standard is applicable to all new facilities and
modifications to existing facilities of the facility life cycle across ADNOC Group.

This document also  illustrates basic Occupational Health, Safety  and Environmental philosophies and
interaction with them. It also provides guidance on HSE Standards, Regulations and design criteria in
order to achieve a risk-based design in accordance to ADNOC HSE Risk Management Standard [Ref. 3].

2.

PURPOSE

The principal objectives of this standard on HSE Design Philosophy are:







To  ensure  that  a  project  is  conceptualized,  designed  and  constructed  in  compliance  with  UAE
Federal and Local Laws & Regulations, and ADNOC Standards & HSE Regulations;

To ensure that the design of facility includes the principles of inherent safe design;

To define the HSE objectives for the project to ensure a safe, risk-based approach to the design
is achieved, thus minimizing the possibility of harm or injury to all personnel and the public from
the consequences of an accidental event;

To ensure the design achieves a demonstrated ‘ALARP’ condition with regard to Health & Safety,
Environment, Financial and Reputation;

To control the environmental (including social) impacts of the project on wildlife, marine life habitat,
air and groundwater quality based on the prevention of pollution or, where this is not possible or
practical, the minimization of pollution using Best Available Technology (BAT);

To assess and control the climatic change impacts due to projects and operations;

To reduce the probability of an accidental event and loss of primary containment occurring. To
minimize the damage caused to plant, equipment and structures, or pollution to the environment,
should an accidental event occur;

To provide guidance in the selection of equipment and design of the projects to achieve its HSE
objectives;

To preserve and optimize the usage of natural resources on a sustainable basis; and

Identify interfaces and ensure consistency in HSE design between engineering disciplines.

7.1.  DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

7.1.1.  PRINCIPLES OF INHERENT SAFETY

Inherent safety describes a broad, philosophical approach to process safety in which the focus lies upon
the designing of processes and products in such a way as to eliminate hazards completely or sufficiently
minimize their magnitude and negate the need for elaborate safety systems incorporated in the design.
This hazard elimination or reduction is achieved via means which are inherent to the process and, as
such, are permanent and inseparable from it.

Besides  elimination  or  reduction  of  a  hazard,  Inherently  Safer  Design  (ISD)  applies  to  the  layers  of
protection in place  within a system such that the strength of a layer of protection can be improved by
features that are permanent and inseparable from that layer.

ISO  17776  “Petroleum  and  natural  gas  industries  -  Offshore  production  installations  -  Major  accident
hazard  management  during  the  design  of  new  installations”  [Ref.  1]  outlines  requirement  for  the
application of ISD for all facilities. The ISO requires that ISD be implemented to eliminate any credible
Major Accident Hazard (MAH) or reduce their potential consequences through the application of design
measures inherent to the design, permanent and inseparable from the installation.

ISD  forms  a  vital  part  of  a  broader  approach  to  risk  control  and  hazard  management  in  the  process
industry and represents the theoretical ideal approach to the control of hazards. Hazard management
can be broadly divided into three primary principles:







Prevention – Measures taken to eliminate or reduce hazards at source or to reduce the likelihood
of a hazard being realised.

Control – Measures taken to keep hazards within the design envelope, be it through containment
or control systems or reactions to events that could result in an incident.

Mitigation – Measures taken to respond to a hazard once an incident has occurred either to bring
it back under control or limit its effects.

ISD  represents  the  primary  method  for  the  implementation  of  the  prevention  category  of  hazard
management and is preferable to Control and Mitigation measures due to its status as an inherent safe
component of the design and does not rely on other measures. In the Hierarchy of Controls, as shown in
Figure 7.1.1, the most effective forms of protection are Elimination, Substitution and Isolation, all of which
come under the remit of ISD.

7.1.2.  PROCESS SAFETY

Figure 7.1.1: Hierarchy of Controls

Process Safety is a disciplined framework for managing the integrity of operating systems and processes
handling hazardous substances by applying good design principles, engineering, and operating practices.
It deals with the prevention and control of incidents that have the potential to release hazardous materials
or energy. Such incidents can cause toxic effects, fire, or explosion and could ultimately result in serious
injuries, property damage, lost production, environmental impact and reputation.

Hazard Identification and  Risk Analysis is a collective term that encompasses all activities  involved  in
identifying hazards and evaluating risk at facilities, throughout their life cycle, to make certain that risks
to  employees,  the  public,  environment  and/or  the  reputation  are  consistently  controlled  within  the
organization’s risk tolerance. These are addressed through HSE studies and reviews.

7.5.  PROCESS SAFETY DESIGN

The  analysis  and  design  of  safety  system  shall  be  as  per  API  RP  14C  or  other  approved  equivalent
international standard. The safety system shall provide two level of protection to prevent or minimize the
effects  of  an  equipment  failure  within  the  process.  In  general,  the  two  levels  should  be  provided  with
functionally different types of safety devices for a wide spectrum of coverage. Two identical devices would
have the same characteristic and might have the same inherent weakness.

Duplication of identical safety devices given different set points does not satisfy the requirement of two
levels of protection.

7.5.1.  ACOUSTIC INDUCED VIBRATION

Valves passing high mass flows of gas with large pressure drops can generate high sound power levels
in the downstream pipework. This can induce vibrations into the piping which lead to fatigue failure in
relatively short duration.

Start-up or abnormal operation can also result in valves being used at more arduous duty than during
normal operations.

Blowdown valves and large safety relief valves are potential sources of high sound power levels and the
susceptibility of pipework to failure is a function of diameter to wall thickness ratio.

Flare piping is the main area where acoustically induced failure may be experienced.

The noise generated inside piping downstream of PSVs and EDPVs shall be checked to ensure that the
noise induced fatigue does not occur in the system during venting.

The flare piping must be screened using approved techniques.

7.7.  EMERGENCY SHUT DOWN (ESD)

The ESD system is the primary control measure for avoiding escalating fire and explosion scenarios, and
in combination with the separation of plant and equipment reduces the consequences of the incident to
plant and personnel. The operation of the ESD system and blowdown system is to isolate and remove
the inventory from the pressurized plant as rapidly as is practicable to reduce the consequences of vapor
cloud  explosions  and  jet  fires  to  personnel  and  equipment.  The  ESD  system  including  all  associated
support systems necessary to perform this function, shall survive the initial event in order to perform this
target.

7.7.1.  FUNCTIONALITY

The ESD functionality shall be developed to prevent risk of exceeding design conditions, associated with
the failure of the equipment and bring the plant or sub-system into safe condition.

Generally, an ESD system will be a controlled shutdown of a specific unit, package or sub-system initiated
by protective trips or operator action and also or shut-off all equipment likely to constitute a source of
ignition (gas or diesel engines, gas turbines, fired heaters, etc.) pertaining to the concerned fire zone(s)
except diesel driven fire pumps.

electrical equipment;

Stop the rotating machines (by switching the power off);

Isolate  the  equipment  containing  considerable  amount  of  flammable  /  hazardous  fluids  and
depressurize the equipment/piping (usually manually by operator);

7.8.  EMERGENCY DEPRESSURIZATION (EDP)

The principal objectives of emergency depressurization and blow down system will be to:





Mitigate the consequences of a hydrocarbon release by reducing the pressure and inventory in
the process section and hence the potential leakage rate; and

Avoid  catastrophic  rupture  of  the  pressure  vessels  in  a  fire  scenario,  by  reducing  the  internal
pressure to lower the stress.

The  EDP  (or  blowdown)  is  one  of  the  most  effective  risk  reduction  measures  as  the  decay  of  system
pressure results in shorter jet flames and reduces the duration of the release. This significantly reduces
the escalation potential by reducing the probability of flame impingement onto other sections of process
plant or key structures and buildings. In addition, by reducing the pressure, the stresses on a vessel under
fire  attack  will  reduce  and  will  be  less  likely  to  reach  dangerous  levels  and  thus  the  probability  of
catastrophic vessel failure will be significantly reduced.

All  PSVs  releasing  from  hydrocarbon  tanks  and  vessels  shall  be  connected  to  the  flare  system.  For
remote and unmanned facilities such as well head towers, well pad areas, etc. where provision of flare is
impractical, alternate provisions/means shall be considered in line with ALARP principle.  This shall take
into account amount of gases to be vented, dispersion modelling/pattern and location of various receptors
such as adjacent facilities, manned areas, etc. with respect to vent locations.

The flare network sizing shall be carried out considering "high rate emergency" depressurization due to
any event that may lead to severe consequences in a relatively short time frame which requires to meet
the criteria for depressurization of the equipment by 7 bar (g) (100 psig), or 50 % of design pressure,
whichever  is  lower  within  15  minutes  as  per  API  521.  However,  in  order  to  minimize  the  duration  of
hydrocarbon leaks, consideration shall be given to the practicality of reducing the EDP time below the
code requirement of 15 minutes. Further improvements to the blowdown especially for high pressure or
high H2S system, may give risk reduction benefits provided that the temperatures in the relevant process
systems are not reduced below the lower design temperature.

The isolation of the process equipment’s/facilities/fire zones shall be considered to minimize the risk of
escalation  effectively  from  upstream  or  downstream  parts  of  the  process  by  minimizing  the  isolatable
inventories.

spare/empty vessel is recommended.

All EDP pertaining to one fire zone shall be provided with a common reset capability from the main control

detrimental to safety (e.g. if the relief piping is damaged and relief flow fuels a fire in the process units).
This functionality is required for both automatic and manually activated EDP systems.

For details, refer to ADNOC Standard on Emergency Shutdown & Depressurization System [Ref. 46].

7.9.  FIRE ZONES

Fire zones are areas of the plant sub-divided based on the potential for fire & explosion hazard to cause
escalation,  as  assessed  by  the  consequence  and  risk  modelling.  Fire  zones  are  areas  within  the
installation where equipment is grouped by nature and/or by similar level of risk attached to them.  Fire
zone assists in managing:







Separation distances between the units to prevent escalation;

Depressurization rate; and

Firewater requirements.

The partition into fire zones is such that the consequence of fire or an explosion corresponding to the
reasonably worst event likely to occur in the concerned fire zone shall not impact other fire zones to an
extent  where  their  integrity  could  be  put  at  risk.    The  partition  of  the  fire  zone  is  intended  to  limit  the
consequence (escalation) of credible events but is not intended to avoid the occurrence of the credible
events.  A fire zone may be physically separated from other areas by space, fire barriers, diking, special
drainage (e.g. impoundment basis) or by a combination of these so that fire is expected to be contained
within that area. The escalation of the event is restricted to the affected fire zone and only a minor impact
on adjacent fire zones.

Fire zones shall be defined through consequence modelling of reasonably worst event (e.g., tank top fire,
or loss of containment via a 25 mm (1 inch) hole in a pressurized system), giving distances to thermal
radiation  levels.  Fire  zone  criteria  is  outlined  below;  each  of  below  criteria  is  defined  based  on  either
potential to cause an escalation of the Major Accident to adjacent unit or due to damage caused which
can lead to secondary loss of containment and subsequence fires.





Thermal Radiation - 32 kW/m2 (initial release rate, T=0 minute) and 12.5 kW/m2 (T=5 minutes from
initial release rate) whichever is governing; and

Explosion Overpressure  - 200 mbar at 1E-04/year arising from the unit.

For thermal radiation at release duration of 5 minutes, engineering barriers such as emergency shutdown
and  emergency  depressurization,  pump  shutdown,  etc.  should  be  considered.  Fire  zones  should  be
optimized to increase safety; however, the number of fire zones should be reduced in order to minimize
the complexity of the ESD system and flare system.

For  facilities  where  automatic  blowdown  is  provided,  units  shall  be  segregated  based  on  reasonably
worse event LFL distances otherwise flare load for both fire zones should be considered in the design.

7.10.  F&G SYSTEM

The  objective  of  Fire  and  Gas  (F&G)  detection  and  alarm  system  is  to  provide  an  early  warning  to
personnel  of  potentially  dangerous  releases  of  toxic  and/or  flammable  gas  or  fires,  and  to  initiate

shutdown and depressurization.

All detectors shall be integrated into an overall Fire and Gas logic system and shall provide an input to
the emergency shutdown system for each unit.

The location, elevation and the quantity of detectors shall be based on applicable code, best practices in
industry and the applicable hazards that exist in each area. Fixed toxic gas detectors shall be provided
at an elevation to cover the breathing zone of the personnel.

coverage should be verified based on Technical Report published by ISA (TR84.00.07-2010 Guidance
on the Evaluation of Fire, Combustible Gas and Toxic Gas System Effectiveness) or any other equivalent
International Standards.

Detectors shall, as far as possible be protected against environmental effects that could influence their
operation.

Executive actions shall be specified on F&G Cause and Effects matrix document and shall comply with
the following high-level guidelines:







A  voting  system  shall  be  incorporated  to  reduce  spurious  alarms  without  unduly  extending
confirmed detection times;

Personnel shall be alerted to the potential for imminent danger by audible and visual alarms so
that they may escape from the area; and

area  shall  initiate  executive  action  such  as  emergency  shutdown  (ESD)  and  emergency

and in line with facility cause and effect matrix. (Refer ESD, section 7.7).

Further details on F&G design shall refer to ADNOC Standard for Fire Detection and Protection System
[Ref.  47].

7.12.  PAVING, DRAINAGE, ENVIRONMENTAL CONTAINMENT & BUNDING

7.12.1. PAVING

Plant areas where hydrocarbon leakage may occur in normal or accidental situations shall be paved and
provided with adequate slope for effective draining of flammable liquid.

The process unit shall be graded to segregate adjacent processing areas from each other by varying the
slope  of  the  drained  areas  to  the  catch  basins.  The  paved  area  shall  have  a  sufficient  slope  to  avoid
accumulation of flammable liquid or vapour adjacent to or beneath equipment and pipe ways. If sloping
is not practical, low kerbs may be used, provided that access is not impaired.

For details refer ADNOC Standard for Facility Layout and Safe Distances [Ref. 44].

7.12.2. DRAINAGE

The principal methods of draining hydrocarbons and chemicals from operational areas shall be defined.
Requirements for segregation of open and closed drain systems and of hazardous / non-hazardous drains
shall be specified. The principles of treatment methods and disposal shall also be defined.

All applicable drain systems should be designed in accordance to ISO13702.

Closed drain systems shall be provided to prevent any discharges of hydrocarbon or hazardous materials
into the environment, air, land or sea. The drainage systems shall ensure that the passage of flammable
vapours is prevented, and the likelihood of hydrocarbon escape is minimized.

A  system  of  shallow  kerbs  shall  be  employed  around  vessels  including  vendor  packages  where
appropriate, in order to contain and prevent any spillage from one vessel spreading to adjacent plant or
equipment. The kerbs shall direct any spillage, with an aid of a sloped concrete paved area to hazardous
open drain system.

Hazardous open drain system shall not be located directly beneath, but adjacent to vessels because any
blockage could create pooling of flammable liquid underneath the vessel, which could lead to rapid fire
escalation by directly impacting the vessel.

The design of hazardous open drain system shall ensure rapid removal of any hydrocarbon spillage from
all process areas by providing adequate slope. The design shall assess and provide for the removal of
potentially  large  volumes  of  hydrocarbon  and  contaminated  firewater  from  monitors  and  deluged
equipment to an Accidental Oil Containment (AOC) or designated safe area such as pits or collection
sumps.

The design shall consider sloped foundations beneath vessels, flow control channels, flame traps, drain
manifolds and maintenance requirements, i.e., prevention or minimization of sand ingress into drainage
systems.

Open non-hazardous drain system shall be designed to handle surface water run-off of the facility.

Within  buildings,  designs  shall  incorporate  drainage  of  fire  water  sprinkler  and  hose  reel  systems,
provisions shall also be made for routine system testing.

7.13.6. LIGHTNING PROTECTION

Lightning tends to strike the tallest object on the ground in the path of its discharge. Parts of structures
most likely to be struck are those that project above surrounding parts, vents, edge of roof, wind sock,
etc. The lightning bolt generally follows a conductive path to ground.

Proper lightning protection provides a controlled path for the current to follow back to earth and minimizes
the development of hazardous potential differences. It may not be possible to completely eliminate the
possibility of damaging accidents caused by lightning, however, a low impedance path (e.g., lightning rod
to ground) should be offered to prevent the lightning current from taking other possible destructive routes.
This path must be a continuous from the ground terminal to the air terminal (lightning rod). This requires
that metal parts be interconnected or bonded so that they maintain the same electrical potential.

For  tanks  holding  flammable  substances,  protection  devices,  such  as  air  terminals  (lightning  rods),
bonding and appropriate grounding systems, conductors (connects air terminals to grounding system),
masts,  overhead  ground  wires,  and  other  types  of  protection,  should  be  considered.  The  National
Lightning  Safety  Institute  (NLSI),  USA  recommends  that  connector  bonding  should  be  thermal,  not
mechanical,  where  possible.  Grounding  systems  can  be  created  with  driven  ground  rods,  plates,  and
perhaps  a  counterpoise  (a  buried  cable  encircling  the  site).  Materials  adequate  to  withstand  lightning
strikes  should  be  used;  specifically,  use  of  low  impedance  materials  (e.g.  metals)  is  essential.  The
grounding system should be designed for a target resistance of five ohms/meter resistance or less.

The  lightning  protection  system  shall  conform  to  the  following  appropriate  standard  not  limited  to  the
following.





EN/IEC 62305 – Lightning Protection Standard [Ref. 35]

NFPA 780 - Standard for the Installation of Lightning protection Systems [Ref. 36]

7.21.2. SHALLOW FRESH GROUNDWATER USAGE

Fresh  water  is  scarce  commodity  in  a  desert  environment  and  deserves  the  highest  attention  for
conservation  and  protection.  The  majority  of  freshwater  in  the  UAE  comes  from  groundwater  but  is
supplemented  by  significant  freshwater  production  from  coastal  desalination  plants.  The  shallow
groundwater  reservoir  takes  a  long  time  to  be  replenished.  If  groundwater  is  used  faster  than  it  is
replenished,  high  saline  waters  can  intrude  and  “pollute”  the  freshwater  making  it  unusable  as  a
freshwater source or water depletion becomes more evident.

It is ADNOC strategy to consider renewable and sustainable source of fresh water for desalting crude oil
as part of normal operations.  Groundwater reservation is not only in ADNOC’s best interest but also has
become a national interest for future generations.

The  design  must  therefore  explore  and  consider  all  feasible  options  to  ensure  that  sustainable  water
supply is maintained to all field areas, while reserving the shallow ground water aquifers for emergency
purposes. The primary available resources of sustainable fresh water supply to consider in the project
design should be Utility Company supply.

Moreover, in an effort to minimize fresh wash water requirements, other process elements to control the
efficiency of wash water requirements shall also be considered for further evaluation as follows:

Heat exchanger temperature; and

Lean Gas injection in H2S strippers.

Best Available Technology (BAT) conforms to latest ISO/API standards.

Fuel Use / Combustion Emissions

(b)

The design should be based on minimizing combustion emissions (e.g. SOx, NOx, CO2, CO and
particulates).

Fired Heaters and Gas fired turbines with single fuel capacity shall in principal be equipped with
“Low NOx burners” using preferably sweet gas or very low H2S gas.

Dual fuel fired heaters and fired turbines (gas and diesel) might not have Low NOx burners installed,
unless high burner reliability during automatic fuel change-over has been demonstrated over time
in previous installations.

High-energy efficiency should be sought in any fuel user application.

Standard design for turbine installations might include waste heat boiler or combined cycle facilities.
Fuel user shall have fuel meter installed with online data transmission to control process control
and monitoring systems.

The  design  should  include  evaluation  of  recovery  and  sequestration  of  CO 2  gas  emissions.
BATNEEC (Best Available Technology Not Entailing Excessive Cost) shall be used, as required
by ADNOC Standards.

(c)  Other Halocarbons with Potential for Global Warming

Halocarbons that do not deplete the Ozone layer; but may have high Global Warming Potential
(GWP).  Halocarbons with ZERO GWP shall be used when compared to those having higher GWP,
for example, Inert Gas Fire Suppression agents and other chemical agents which has a low GWP
agent shall be used.

The design should provide sufficient sampling and measurement points to enable adequate monitoring
of emissions during the operating phase of the project.

Flares  shall  be  fitted  with  flare  gas  meters  installed  with  online  data  transmission  to  central  process
control and monitoring system. There shall be two flow meters to capture the low-range flow (thermal
flow meters) and full flare load (ultrasonic flow meters).

Exhaust emissions and monitoring system shall consider the best available practice and technology.

7.21.7. WASTE MANAGEMENT

New facilities and process plants shall be designed to minimize waste generated (hazardous and non-
hazardous).    The  design  should  also  ensure  that  the  generated  waste  must  be  handled  safely  and
disposal should be the final option after applying waste minimization principles.

7.21.8. WATER MANAGEMENT

(a)

Produced Water

Produced water should only be discharged to a receiving environment with which it is compatible
after  appropriate  treatment  (discharge  limits  should  be  in  compliance  with  relevant  ADNOC
Environment  Standards/Limits).  Refer  to  ADNOC  Environmental  Impact  Assessment  Standard
[Ref. 7] and UAE Federal Regulations on Marine Discharge Limits.

The feasibility of produced water reuse or re-injection options is to be evaluated in the design.

Produced water is to be re-injected after being properly treated to ensure reservoir compatibility
and  injectivity  testing.  The  oil  content  in  the  produced  water  shall  not  exceed  500ppm  before
injecting into reservoirs.

(b)  Waste Water

It is ADNOC Policy not to discharge any off spec water to desert/sea during normal operations. A
closed drain system shall be provided to prevent such discharges of wastewater to the desert.

The closed drain system shall consist of a closed drain header, necessary water treatment system,
and injection well(s).  The closed drain system might be combined with the open drain system. If
so,  the  fluid  treatment  system  will  need  to  treat  the  fluid  to  the  allowable  discharge  standards
defined in ADNOC Standards.

All discharge volume must be metered with online data transmission to a central process control
and  monitoring  system.    Discharge  quality  must  also  be  monitored  on  frequent  basis  to  enable
verification  of  ADNOC  discharge  limits.  Contaminated  surface  drain  water  to  be  routed  to  the
produced water disposal facility; either surface discharge after treatment or re-injection option to
be evaluated for produced water disposal.

7.21.9. CHEMICAL WASTE MANAGEMENT

The design should challenge the need for all chemical use. A management strategy will be put in place
to  minimize  the  environmental  impacts  associated  with  chemicals  through  correct  selection,
transportation, storage, deployment and disposal.

Chemicals known or suspected to cause taint, endocrine disruption or contain heavy metals as defined
by  UK  OCNS  (Offshore  Chemical  Notification  Scheme)  shall  be  avoided.  In  the  event  that  suitable
alternatives  are  not  available,  the  impact  of  the  chemical  should  be  risk  assessed  and  mitigation
measures agreed.

No chemicals shall be discharged to sea in the project execution phase (e.g. chemically treated hydro
test fluids) without complete identification and thorough assessment of their impacts. System shall also
be in place to re-circulate oil and chemicals back into the process stream wherever practical.

The facility  design should  as far as reasonably practical, avoid  the need to discharge  production and
utility chemicals to sea.

7.22.  VENTS & FLARING

7.22.1. EXHAUSTS & VENTS

Designs shall ensure that any turbine or combustion motor exhaust should not be a source of ignition for
any hydrocarbon gas leak scenario. Exhaust ducts shall be designed to be gas tight and heat insulated
if necessary.

Vents shall not be located adjacent to any potential sources of ignition or hot surfaces, i.e., exhaust pipes
or any potential manned area. Flame arresters shall be considered for the hydrocarbon vents.

Consider any potential for liquid carry-over spills in vents and surrounding ignition sources, verify this
point within all HAZOP studies.

7.23.  PERSONNEL PROTECTION

7.23.1. HOT SURFACES PROTECTION

Appropriate design measures shall be provided to protect against injury from hot equipment with external
metal or conducting parts having a surface temperature that may be hotter than 60OC in normal operations
and there is risk or being touched by personnel.

In general, vessels, exchangers, pumps, miscellaneous equipment, valves, flanges shall not be insulated
for personnel protection. If however, a possible hazard is evident, personnel protection shall be provided
in the form of thermal insulation or preferably guard barriers with warning signs to prevent personnel from
coming in direct contact with hot surfaces.

Extent  of  personnel  protection  insulation  shall  not  exceed  2m  above  not  more  than  1m  away  from
accessible areas.

The design should not rely on personnel wearing gloves or other PPE, except for items such as handrails,
which may exceed 60OC due to solar radiation, and where the use of insulation would be impractical.

7.23.2. MACHINERY GUARDS

All  exposed moving parts  on  all machinery shall be  suitably guarded to protect personnel from injury.
Guards and safety devices provided shall be kept in position whilst machinery is running.

Emergency trip button shall be provided, as applicable.

7.23.3. PERSONAL PROTECTIVE EQUIPMENT

The  PPE  requirements  for  normal  and  emergency  operations  are  to  be  identified  and  provided  as
required based on operational requirement and risk assessment.

Provision of PPE shall be in accordance with ADNOC Personal Protective Equipment Standard [Ref. 28].

7.23.5. EMERGENCY SAFETY SHOWERS & EYE WASH STATIONS

Emergency safety showers and eye wash stations shall be installed upwind or crosswind of the most
prevailing wind direction at all locations, wherever personnel are likely to be exposed to any injury from
contact with harmful chemicals/vapours.

Risk areas include, but not limited to, chemical loading/off loading facilities, chemical injection  points,
pumping areas, valve manifolds, chemical storage and battery rooms etc.

All permanently installed units within plant areas will be in high temperature desert conditions; hence the
unit shall be associated with a chiller to maintain the temperature of the water.

Emergency Safety Showers and Eye Wash Stations temperature, pressure and flowrate requirements
shall be in accordance to ANSI Z358.1:

Minimum distance of the eyewash & shower from hazard shall be no closer than 3m (10ft) and no more
than 15m (50 feet)

Remote places such as wells locations, where there is no adequate power supply available to run the
chiller mounted emergency showers and logistically difficult to replenish the water periodically, there is
no requirement to provide permanent eye wash stations/showers.  Personnel are advised to carry mobile
eye wash stations along with them every time they visit the location.

7.23.6. HEAT STRESS

Exposure to heat can cause illness and death. The most serious heat illness is heat stroke. Other heat
illnesses,  such  as  heat  exhaustion,  heat  cramps  and  heat  rash,  should  also  be  avoided.  There  are
precautions that can be taken any time temperatures are high and the job involves physical work.

Operator cabins or rest shelters shall be considered where operators are expected to remain for pro-
longed periods of time in the sun.

Heat stress management shall be in compliance to OSHAD-SF CoP 11.0 and ADNOC Physical Hazards
Standards [Ref. 40].
